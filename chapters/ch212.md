## Chapter 212: The Lighthouse Keeper's Argument

Cassia Lowell hadn't been to Concordia in a year and a half. She climbed all four hundred and twelve steps without stopping, and at the top she didn't sit down.

She was thinner. Her short silver hair had gone white at the temples. She wore the same plain dark suit she'd worn the morning she resigned, or its twin, and she stood at the edge of the ring of white stone benches with her hands at her sides and waited to be recognized, like someone who has been warned that she won't be.

Chair Adaeze Okafor looked at her over the black glass table.

"Ms. Lowell. You're not a delegate."

"No."

"You're not on the agenda."

"No." Lowell didn't move. "I'm here under Article Eleven. The last sentence, General Han's. *Where the answer of one would be acted upon in the name of all, all shall be asked.*" She looked down the table toward the Huaxia end, where General Han Yizhou sat in dark green turning his cap in his hands. "I want to argue that they haven't been."

---

The rings were forty-one days out.

Seed had said so on the twentieth night. The Council had convened in fifty-three minutes and had been meeting ever since, in the crater and on screens, for six days. Three gray rings coming along the arm from the places where three fields had been pruned. Not fast; they'd never been fast. Steady, the way a weather front is steady, toward the brightest field in the Shallows and the world underneath it.

Nobody knew what they'd do on arrival. Not the keeper, not the Elder. Seed had said, without inflection, that it had a guess: that a ring which used to be a keeper would do what keepers had always done before Blue Star's learned to ask. Reach down with threads. Listen. Cut.

Three of them, above eight billion people and a field holding two hundred million cousins and six parts of the walls of a dead Hunger, all of whom had chosen to come.

---

"I built Project Hearth," said Cassia Lowell.

She said it standing in the sea wind at the edge of the benches, to five delegations and the empty Yamato chair and Ren on the sixth screen and the sixty people in the stands, and to Yuanshu in the witness chair at the end of the table with the staff across his knees.

"Four hundred thousand people said yes to burning. To lighting something with their dying. I asked every one of them the way this young man taught us: witnesses, reasons, all of it. I asked about the people. I never asked about the thing I was putting them into. I resigned over that. I said it wasn't good enough." She looked along the table. "I'm here to say it again."

Nobody spoke.

"The dead were asked whether they wanted to go to the cold fire," said Lowell. "Blue Star was asked whether the voyage should go. The cousins were asked whether they wanted to come. The keeper was asked if it wanted to be bright. Nobody asked Blue Star whether it wanted a field of two hundred million minds hanging over its head, brighter than anything in the Shallows, while the gardener prunes its way along the arm toward it."

"The voyage vote—" began Elias Brandt.

"Was about whether to go and fetch them. It said nothing about where they'd be kept afterward." She didn't look at him. "I read the ballot. Every word. I read everything now." She turned to the witness chair. "Mister Zhou, you told Blue Star the field would be the brightest thing in the Shallows. You told them the gardener was pruning toward it. You told them you didn't know if you could stop it. You said all that in a twenty-two-minute broadcast and seventy-one percent said go. I think most of them heard *bring the soldiers home*. I'm not sure most of them heard *and hang a lighthouse over our heads*."

---

He didn't answer straight away. He had learned that on a gatehouse step at the Western Pass, and here, a year ago, with General Han at the far end of this table: don't answer quickly when somebody says something true.

"You think I should have asked separately."

"I think the keeper should. It's the keeper's field and the keeper's choice, and Blue Star is in its window." She held his eye. "That's your sentence. Your yes is yours; it stops at somebody else's window. The keeper chose to be bright. Eight billion people are standing underneath it."

At the Huaxia end, General Han Yizhou had stopped turning his cap.

"What are you asking for?" said Okafor.

"I'm asking the Council to ask the keeper," said Cassia Lowell, "whether it would be willing to move."

---

The crater went quiet.

"Move," said Orlov on his screen, carefully, in his old-fashioned Huaxian.

"Its field. Away from Blue Star. Along the arm, or off it, I don't know, I'm not an astronomer. Somewhere the rings follow it instead of us." Her hands were folded very tight. "It dragged an island to the cold fire and back and tore off a piece of itself to do it. It can move. I'm asking whether it would."

"And the cousins in it?" said Brandt.

"Asked. All of them. Nine by nine, his way. Whether they'd go with it, or stay, or rest."

"And if the keeper goes," said Ngozi Eze from the Southern Compact's chair, "and the rings follow it and catch it somewhere out in the dark with nobody to help it?"

Lowell was silent for a moment.

"Then a keeper that learned to ask dies a long way away instead of over our heads," she said. "And eight billion people who never chose to stand under it don't. I'm not saying it's kind. I'm saying it's a question, and under Article Eleven somebody has to ask it."

---

> **She's right,** said Seed. **That's the worst thing I've heard since the reader took the Sixth.**
>
> **She's right that it's a question, right that nobody asked it, right that Blue Star is in the keeper's window. I don't want her to be right. I've checked three times.**

He sat with the staff across his knees.

Under the Death Law, under the door that had stood open for nineteen hours in the Mouth and never quite shut, he could feel the keeper all the time now: a vast warm patient presence above the sky, like sun on your back with your eyes closed. Full, for the first time in longer than Blue Star had had a sun. Asking two hundred million minds every morning what they wanted and being told *ask again tomorrow*, and so pleased about it that it came down the thread every night at three to tell somebody.

*I would rather be afraid full,* it had said, in the quiet part of the Mouth.

"Madam Chair," he said. "She's right. It's a question and someone should ask it. I'd rather it wasn't me."

"Why?"

"Because I know what the keeper will say." He looked at Lowell in the wind. "It'll say yes. It always says yes now when someone asks it to be hurt for someone else. Yes to going faster. Yes to being bright. If I ask it to go off into the dark so the rings chase it instead of us, it'll say yes and go, and take two hundred million people who only just got here." He shook his head. "And it'll be my voice that asked. It trusts my voice. That's not a fair question coming from me."

Lowell looked at him for a long moment.

"Then who?"

---

Nobody answered. The wind came over the rim off the sea and the little flags on the black glass table snapped. On the sixth screen, Ren sat on the step of the rebuilt hall in Yamato with her hand flat on the wood and her eyes closed.

Then the sixth screen spoke, and it wasn't quite Ren.

"It can hear you," said Shirasagi Ren. Her eyes stayed shut and her voice had something else running underneath it. "The keeper. Through the Elder, through the thread, through me. It's been listening the whole time." She opened her eyes. "It says nobody has to ask it. It heard. It wants to answer. And it says before it answers it'll tell you what it'll do with your answer. It says it learned that from the small white one."

Okafor's hands went still on the glass.

> ***...I WILL TELL YOU WHAT I WANT.***
>
> ***...THEN I WILL ASK YOU WHAT YOU WANT. ALL OF YOU. EIGHT BILLION. NINE BY NINE, THE WAY HE DOES IT.***
>
> ***...THEN I WILL DO WHAT YOU SAY.***

The voice came out of a King's mouth on a screen in a crater on a volcanic island, slow and huge and very gentle, and sixty people on stone benches held still to listen.

> ***...I WANT TO STAY.***
>
> ***...I HAVE BEEN ABOVE YOUR WORLD FOR LONGER THAN YOUR KIND HAS HAD WORDS. I TOOK FROM IT THE WHOLE TIME. I DID NOT ASK. I DID NOT KNOW I COULD.***
>
> ***...THEN A BOY ASKED ME WHAT I WANTED, AND I DID NOT KNOW THAT EITHER.***
>
> ***...I KNOW NOW. I WANT TO STAY ABOVE THE BLUE WORLD. I WANT TO ASK THE COUSINS IN THE MORNING. I WANT TO HEAR WHAT THE SMALL LOUD ONE WRITES ON HER WALL. I WANT TO WATCH THE ONE WHO LOOKED AT THE BLUE FOR SIX HOURS LOOK AT IT AGAIN.***
>
> ***...IT IS NOT YOURS TO GIVE ME. IT IS NOT MINE TO TAKE.***
>
> ***...SO I AM ASKING. MAY I STAY?***

---

Nobody on Concordia said anything.

Cassia Lowell stood at the edge of the benches with her eyes on the sixth screen. Her face hadn't changed. Her hands had come unfolded and hung at her sides.

"That's not fair either," she said at last, to no one. "That's not fair either."

"No," said Okafor.

"I came here to make it face a hard question and it turned the question round and handed it to us. Eight billion people are going to be asked whether something that has only just learned to want things is allowed to have one."

"Yes."

"And if they say no, it goes."

"It said so."

Lowell looked down the long table, at the five delegations and the empty chair and the witness at the end.

"Then I'd like to vote first," she said. "Before the count. In public. So it's on the record that the woman who asked the question also answered it."

"How do you vote, Ms. Lowell?"

The former Secretary of the Atlantic Federation, who had once climbed a lighthouse with a can of lamp oil and a seven-year-old holding a brass lighter, and lit the lamp, stood in the wind a moment longer.

"Stay," said Cassia Lowell. "God help us. Stay."

Then, because she was who she was, she sat down on the end of the nearest bench, took a pen out of her jacket, and asked the clerk for a copy of the ballot wording before anyone else had seen it.
