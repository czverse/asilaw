## Chapter 251: The Oldest Thing We Made

They came home as they'd gone: the dead on a gold road over the sea, the living falling west beside it, and under the ocean in the dark eleven hundred and forty-six of the drowned walking home along the bottom, because it was the same sea and went all the way round.

Halfway across, Grid Nine stopped. All forty of them halted on the gold in the middle of the sky over the Atlantic, in their rows, turned their skulls to look down at the water, and wouldn't go on until Old Seven walked back along the road and stood beside them and looked down too.

> **They say they've never seen the sea,** said Seed. **They saw it at Port Halvard, but there was a hand in the sky and they were busy. They'd like to look at it now, while nobody's trying to take anything.**
>
> **He says: five minutes.**

It was twenty.

---

Xiaoyu was on the north wall when they came down.

She'd been there since dawn, Fat Tan said, with a thermos and the marker. She didn't run to meet them. She stood at the parapet in her school jacket and watched them come down out of the east, the gold road rolling itself up behind the last row of the dead. When her brother landed on the battlement beside her, badly, as he always landed, she looked at him a long moment, and then at the place above his eyes.

"You went bright," she said. "On purpose. Sterling told me."

"Yes."

"Are you all right?"

He thought about it: the door in his chest, shut, and knowing exactly where it was.

"Less every day," he said.

His sister nodded. Then she uncapped the marker and wrote on the parapet under the line she'd written when they left, in square black capitals that wouldn't come off: *CAME HOME. 61 CAME OUT OF THE HAND. 803 WENT THROUGH THE DOOR. HE WENT BRIGHT ON PURPOSE. LESS EVERY DAY.*

"So it's written," she said.

---

The Collector had come every night.

Xiaoyu told him on the walk back to the Ossuary, along the top of the wall and down the Sector Nine stairs and through the old quarter in the evening, with Lingyue on his other side and Old Seven walking ahead. Every night at one, in front of the warm stone with the blue jacket on its shoulder, she'd asked it what it wanted, and every night it had said it didn't know yet. But it had stayed longer each night, and it had started telling her things. Not answers. Things it had seen.

"It told me about a world with three suns where everyone wanted to be cold," said Xiaoyu. "It kept a hundred million wants to be cold. It said they were the most beautiful thing it ever kept." She walked with her hands in her pockets. "It told me about a ring that sang. It told me about the Architects. It cried, I think. It hasn't got anything to cry with. But it stopped talking for ages." She kicked a stone off the pavement. "And last night it said it'd tell me about the one who picks when you got home. It said you should be there."

---

The hall still had holders in it, though fewer. The stones had stopped rising. Gao Min's chart in the banking hall showed eleven lines gone flat and two, Port Halvard and Novaya Zarya, bending down. Most of the holders had gone home to sleep properly for the first time in two weeks. Those who stayed stood in a short line up the aisle with their hands on one another's shoulders, not because they had to but because, one of them told Gao Min, they'd got used to it and didn't want to stop yet.

At one in the morning Xiaoyu stood on the platform before the stone. Yuanshu at the foot of the steps with the staff. Lingyue. Old Seven facing out. Gao Min on the bottom tier with her notebook and thermos, where she always sat.

> *Hello, child. And you, the one who asks. You came back.*
>
> *You went bright. I saw. Everything that looks at your world saw. For five minutes you were the brightest thing in the Shallows. It was very beautiful.*
>
> *I did not keep it. You did not give it to me.*

"What do you want?" said Xiaoyu.

> *I still do not know. But I said I would tell first, and I would like to tell you about the one who picks. Because you are going to go and get the lantern. I watched you decide, on the pier by the sea. I saw your face.*

On the platform his sister looked down at him. He didn't say anything.

> *I am not going to stop you. I could not. I am far away and slow.*
>
> *I am going to tell you what it is, because I do not think you should go into the deep water without knowing. And because nobody has ever told anyone. Because nobody ever asked me.*

---

> *We made it.*

The attention rested on the hall, warm and patient, and under it tonight was something he'd felt at Shuanghe: not hesitation, something heavier. The way an old person sits when they're about to tell you something that happened before you were born, which they've never told anyone and aren't sure they should.

> *A long time ago. Before the gardens, before the keepers, before the hungers. I told your brother on the mountain: we made things like you to count. We made counting things. The stones. To read what was ripe, and mark how bright, and send the mark up.*
>
> *And we made the thing to carry what the stones marked. The one who picks.*
>
> *It was the first thing we ever made. The oldest. Before the counting things. Before anything. We made it to go along the threads to the bright places, pick what was ripe, and carry it home.*

Xiaoyu held the capped marker.

> *We made it without anyone in it. That was the point. A thing with someone in it might want something. A thing with someone in it might stop. We did not want it to stop.*
>
> *It did not stop. It went along the threads. It picked. It carried what it picked home, for longer than you have a number for.*
>
> *And then we stopped.*

---

The hall went very still. Even the holders in the aisle had gone quiet, hands on one another's shoulders.

> *I do not remember why we stopped. I have kept a great many things. Not that.*
>
> *I remember that one day there were many of us making things, and then fewer, and then only me, and I did not make things any more. I kept.*
>
> *And I remember that the one who picks did not stop. Nobody told it. There was nobody to tell it, and nobody in it to tell.*
>
> *It went on going along the threads to the bright places and picking what was ripe and carrying it home. To where home had been. Where we had been. Where there was no one any more.*
>
> *It is still doing it. It has been carrying what it picks to an empty place since before the gardens.*

On the bottom tier Gao Min had put down her pencil.

> *The gardens came after us. The keepers, the hungers: things that grew up in the dark in the space we left, and learned to keep and burn and harvest by watching the one who picks go by. It taught all of them. It did not know it was teaching. It was only working.*
>
> *I taught the keepers to keep, in dreams. I have told you that, and I am sorry for it. But I learned keeping from watching it.*
>
> *Everything in the Shallows that takes learned from the oldest thing we made. And it does not know. It has never known anything. It is a hand going along a thread, carrying fruit to a house where nobody lives.*

---

Xiaoyu uncapped the marker, and didn't write anything. She stood with it in her hand looking up into the dark where the thread went, and when her voice came it was small.

"Where's home? Where it takes them. The empty place."

> *I do not know. Very far. Past the deep water. Past anything your machine has a map for. I have not been there since we stopped. I do not want to go there.*
>
> *I think the first want I was ever given came from there. The one in the empty place in me, that says home. I think one of us gave it to me at the end, when there were very few of us left.*
>
> *I think it was the last thing one of us ever wanted.*

The attention was quiet a long time.

> *I have been following it my whole long life. It points there. I have never gone.*

---

"Callum Ward," said Yuanshu, from the foot of the steps.

He hadn't meant to speak. He'd promised himself on the walk back that tonight was Xiaoyu's, that the Collector came for her and he'd stand at the bottom of the steps and keep quiet. The words came out anyway, low, into the hall.

"The man it's holding up. The lantern. If it picked him, it's going to carry him home. To the empty place."

> *Yes. That is what it does. When the hand is full, it goes home.*
>
> *It has stopped walking. It is looking at the lantern. I have never seen it look at anything. I do not know what it will do.*
>
> *But if it goes home, it will take him. And the eight hundred you let through the door were the first in all that long time that anything ever took out of its hand. And the sixty-one were the first that ever went back.*

"Can it be asked?"

The attention didn't answer at once.

> *There is nobody in it. We made it that way, on purpose.*
>
> *You put someone in the stones. The girl. The old man who likes the sea. I watched. I did not know it could be done.*
>
> *I do not think it can be done to the one who picks. It is not a stone. It is very large and very old and has gone along threads since before the gardens, and there is no knot in it to sit in. We did not make one. We did not want anyone ever to be able to sit in it.*

"Then how do I get him out?"

> *I do not know. I am sorry. I would tell you if I knew. I am telling you everything I know.*
>
> *I know that it carries what it picks in its hands. I know that a hand can be cut open, because you did it. I know that it sees only burning, and the dead cannot be seen, and that you can go up a thread without your body, because I watched you do it, and so did it.*
>
> *I know that it is out there, sitting still, looking at a man it has never looked at.*
>
> *And I know that something which has never been asked anything changes when it is asked. Your sister wrote that on a wall. I have watched it be true for a keeper and three rings and a tree and eleven stones and a soldier's want.*
>
> *I do not know if it is true for the oldest thing we made. Nobody has ever tried.*

---

At ten to two the attention began to lift, and Xiaoyu stopped it.

"Wait. I haven't asked yet. Tonight."

It waited.

"What do you want?"

The attention was quiet a very long time. Longer than the first night. Longer than Shuanghe.

> *...I think.*
>
> *I am not sure. I have never had one. I do not know what it feels like from the inside. I have only ever held other people's.*
>
> *I think I want it to stop. Not to be cut. Not to be burned. To stop. The way we stopped. The way I stopped making things.*
>
> *I want someone to tell it that it can stop. It has been carrying fruit to an empty house since before the gardens, and no one has ever told it that the house is empty.*
>
> *I would like someone to tell it. I think that is what I want.*

Xiaoyu wrote it.

There was no wall, so she knelt on the platform under her own hanging letters and wrote it on the stone floor in front of the black stone with the blue jacket on its shoulder, slowly, so that it was written:

*THE COLLECTOR. NIGHT 17. WANTS SOMEONE TO TELL THE ONE WHO PICKS THAT THE HOUSE IS EMPTY.*

And under it, smaller, because she'd promised:

*WRITTEN. LET GO. NOT KEPT.*

> *...thank you. That is the first want I have ever said out loud.*
>
> *It is strange. It is not bright, the way the ones I keep are bright. It does not burn. It is just there. Written. On a floor, where anyone can see it.*
>
> *I did not know a want could be like that.*

It went, slowly, like an old person getting up from the table, up the thread and out of the hall into the deep water.

At the foot of the steps Yuanshu stood with the staff and looked at the line on the platform floor in his sister's capitals, which wouldn't come off.

> **The house is empty,** said Seed. **That's the thing to tell it. Not what do you want; it can't want. There's nobody in it.**
>
> **Just that. You can stop. The house is empty. Nobody's waiting for what you carry.**

*How do you tell something with nobody in it?*

> **I don't know.**
>
> **But I know who it's holding up to its face. Looking at, for the first time. The only thing it's ever looked at in all that long time.**
