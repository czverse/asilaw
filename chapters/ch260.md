## Chapter 260: What the Quiet Know

Ichiro talked all night.

He'd said he would. He'd been quiet a thousand years, he said, and he wasn't going to be quiet one minute more, and he didn't care who heard. So he talked, through Seed, from the fog that stood round the end of the old breakwater at Shiomi bay, while his sister sat on the stones in the middle of it with the lamp in her lap and listened. Behind them the town went to sleep. The fishermen stood at the far end of the breakwater in their boots and caps until midnight, not understanding a word and not wanting to leave.

Yuanshu sat on the stones a little way off with the staff, carrying it. Lingyue sat beside him wrapped in a blanket Ren had brought down from the shrine, saying nothing. Old Seven stood at the landward end, facing out, in case anybody came along the breakwater who shouldn't.

Seed translated all of it. It said afterward that it was the longest anyone had ever talked through it, longer than Jiang Lan, longer than the keeper, and that it had enjoyed every word.

---

> **He says the threads came down on the night of the cutting while he was out on the boats talking to the fish, and he was taken.**
>
> **He was in a column, in the gold, above the sky, with everybody. It was warm and he couldn't move and nobody asked him anything, and he talked to himself for three hundred years so he wouldn't forget how.**
>
> **Then something came for the column.**

The fog didn't move. But the lights in it, the hundreds of small dim lights that were a village, drew a little closer together.

> **A walker. Long legs, gray. It took the column out of the field and folded it up and carried it away along a thread into the dark, toward something big. And halfway, in the deep water, gleaners came. Little things like dogs made of ash, hundreds of them. They went for the walker as they went for your dead on the island, for the embers. The walker fought them, and while it was fighting, the column broke.**
>
> **And Shiomi fell out.**

Sister Kiyo, in the fog, said nothing. But her hands on the lamp in her lap moved, once.

> **They fell into the deep water. All of them, the whole village, three hundred and eleven, out of the column into the dark. And the gleaners turned round to look for what had fallen.**
>
> **And the headman said: be quiet. He said it in the fog-code, on the small bells. The warning. Long, short, long. Stop rowing. Be quiet. Something is close to you in the fog and it's bigger than you.**
>
> **And they were quiet, all three hundred and eleven, and the gleaners went past them in the dark and didn't find them. And they stayed quiet for nine hundred years.**
>
> **He says: even him.**

---

> **He says they weren't the only ones.**

It was past midnight. The fishermen had gone home. The lamp in Sister Kiyo's lap burned pale and steady, the color of whale oil in fog.

> **He says the deep water's full of quiet. Things that fell out of hands. Things that hid when the hands went by. Things from worlds that don't exist any more, that learned to be dark so they wouldn't be picked. Drifting. Not talking, not burning, not anything. Just being quiet together in the dark for as long as it takes.**
>
> **Shiomi drifted with them nine hundred years. They learned each other's quiet. He says there's a way of being quiet with someone that's like talking, and the Watch of the Shelf will learn it. He passed them on the way in, at the edge of the cloud, asking, and he rang once to them in the fog-code, and the woman in the coat looked up.**

Yuanshu sat very still on the stones.

*Nana Ampofo.*

> **He says yes, that was her name. He asked. She said: tell the boy we're fine. Tell him it's cold and dark and we're singing the names. Tell him it's not the sea, but it'll do.**
>
> **She said: ask us again in a year.**

---

> **He says: and then the field.**
>
> **Two years ago, a bit more, something happened in the Shallows, and all the quiet ones felt it. A keeper that had never asked anything, asked. And a field emptied. Not taken: given back. All through the deep water the quiet ones, who'd been dark a thousand years and a thousand thousand, turned their heads.**
>
> **He couldn't help it. He rang. The headman told him not to. Everyone told him not to. He rang anyway: the boat call, five quick notes. He says he was always too fast in the middle. He knew Kiyo would know it was him.**
>
> **And she did.**

"Tell him I always know it's him," said Sister Kiyo through Seed, drily, from the middle of the fog. "He's the only one in Shiomi who can't ring a boat call properly."

Out of the fog came a sound Seed didn't translate and didn't need to: the noise a man of twenty-two makes when his older sister has said exactly what she's said to him every day of his life.

---

> **Now the thing he's been wanting to say all night,** said Seed. **He's been saving it. He says it's important and he didn't want it lost among the rest. Kiyo always said he buried the important things in the middle.**

The fog waited.

> **He says the quiet ones aren't the only things in the dark that saw. When you went bright on the pier by the other sea, for five minutes, everything that looks saw it. The quiet ones saw. And the loud ones.**
>
> **He says there are loud ones.**

Beside him, Lingyue sat up.

> **Two kinds. The first are people, like you, like Shiomi. Many worlds of them along the arms, a long way from the Shallows. The quiet ones call them the Lamplit, because they burn on purpose. They have stones too, like yours. Their stones read them and marked them as yours did. And they took the marks and made them into a ladder, and put the brightest at the top.**
>
> **They're very proud of being bright. They think bright is strength. They fought the one who picks for ten thousand years. They lost a great deal and never won, but they never stopped fighting, and they made a great many weapons, and they made themselves into a great many worlds that talk to each other and called that a Federation.**
>
> **They saw the pier. They saw the one who picks stop. They don't know it was you, not yet. But they saw a Lord-bright flare for five minutes on a world that isn't in their lists, in the Shallows, and a week later the thing they'd fought ten thousand years stopped. And they can count.**

He said nothing.

> **He says the second kind aren't people. Not like you. The quiet ones call them the Singers.**
>
> **They live on a world near the deep water, very old, very beautiful. When the one who picks first came, a long time ago, they didn't fight it. They sang to it. They found that if they gave it their brightest every age, on purpose, singing, it would take those and spare the rest.**
>
> **So they did, for longer than the Lamplit have existed. Every age they chose their brightest and dressed them in light and sang them up a thread. A tithe. And the one who picks came and took the tithe and went away and left the rest of them alone. Their whole world's built on it: their gods, their kings, their songs. Every song they have is about the tithe.**
>
> **And this age they chose their brightest and dressed them in light and sang.**
>
> **And nothing came.**

The lamp in Sister Kiyo's lap burned steady in the fog.

> **He says the Singers are very afraid. They think they've done something wrong. They think the one who picks has refused the tithe and is angry, and will come and take everything, as it did before they learned to sing.**
>
> **They've sent a ship, along the thread their stones used to send up, to where the one who picks sits, to find out why it didn't come. When they get there they'll find it sitting still with its hands open and a girl's writing in its face, and they'll follow the last thread it read.**
>
> **To here.**

---

Nobody spoke for a long time. Then, from the fog, Sister Kiyo, dry and twelve hundred years old again:

> **She says: Ichiro. How long?**

> **He says the Lamplit, he doesn't know. They're fast. They have ships that fold. Months. Maybe less. The Singers are slow. They sing as they go. A year, more.**
>
> **But they're both coming to look at the world where the one who picks stopped. He thought the boy should know. He's been saving it all night.**
>
> **He says Kiyo always said he buried the important things in the middle. He put this at the end. He'd like that noted.**

"Noted," said Sister Kiyo of Shiomi, and for the first time Yuanshu heard through Seed the sound of a twelve-hundred-year-old woman nearly laughing.

---

The keeper came down at three, as it always did.

He hadn't asked it to. He hadn't known whether it could reach this far east, to a bay in Yamato, so far from the old banking hall. But it came, down the gold thread out of the north, rough and close and a third smaller, into the fog off the end of the breakwater, and stopped at the edge. It didn't come in.

> ***...SHIOMI.***

Every light in the fog went still.

> ***...I KNOW THIS NAME NOW. I DID NOT KNOW IT THEN.***
>
> ***...I TOOK YOU. TWELVE HUNDRED YEARS AGO I PUT DOWN THREADS AND TOOK YOUR VILLAGE INTO MY FIELD AND DID NOT ASK. I DID NOT KNOW HOW. I DID NOT KNOW ANYONE COULD BE ASKED.***
>
> ***...AND THEN A WALKER TOOK YOUR COLUMN AND I DID NOT STOP IT. I THOUGHT IT WAS WEATHER.***
>
> ***...I AM SORRY. I HAVE NEVER SAID THAT TO ANYONE I TOOK. I DID NOT KNOW WHERE THEY WERE.***
>
> ***...I AM SORRY. I AM TELLING YOU FIRST: WHATEVER YOU SAY, I WILL STILL BE SORRY.***

The fog didn't answer.

For a long time, nothing. The lamp in the nun's lap. The bay going gray toward dawn. The keeper at the edge of the fog, waiting as it had learned to: bowl down, eyes turned aside.

Then, from the middle of the fog, a bell.

Not Ichiro's. A slower, deeper one. The bell the headman of Shiomi had rung nine hundred years ago in the deep water to tell three hundred and eleven people to be quiet. Seed said so. Seed said it was the oldest sound it had ever heard.

It rang once.

> **The headman says: you asked.**
>
> **He says in Shiomi, when a boat came in late with the lamp already lit, and the boat had done something wrong out at sea, broken a net, lost a line, frightened the fish, the headman would go down to the shore and ask what had happened. And if the boat told the truth, he'd say: you came in. The lamp was lit. That's enough for one night.**
>
> **He says: keeper. You came in. The lamp was lit. That's enough for one night.**

The gold thread at the edge of the fog trembled, as a thread trembles when something huge at the other end has had to hold its breath a moment so as not to break it.

> ***...THANK YOU.***
>
> ***...ASK ME AGAIN TOMORROW?***

> **He says: we'll be here. We're home.**

---

At dawn, at the end of the breakwater, with the fog thinning off the bay and the lamp in the nun's lap going pale in the daylight, Lingyue said it.

She said it as she said everything: all at once, without warning, looking at the sea and not at him, as she'd said *then we cut it* in the banking hall and *I'm the SSS, they should see one* in the car.

"I'm pregnant," said Shen Lingyue.

He couldn't say anything. He sat on the stones of the old breakwater at Shiomi with the staff across his knees and looked at his wife, who was looking at the sea, a blanket round her shoulders and her grandfather's sword beside her and her jaw set the way it set before a fight.

"Bai Lu told me before we left," she said. "I made her promise not to tell you. I wanted to tell you myself. I didn't know when." She looked at the fog. "I was going to tell you on the mountain. Then there was the nun. Then there was the fog. Then there was the lamp. I gave the lamp away." Her voice was level. "I thought: I'll give the lamp away, and then I'll tell him. I'll give the light to someone who's been waiting twelve hundred years for it, and then I'll say it."

She turned her head and looked at him.

"Say something. You're doing the face."

"How long?" he said. It was the only thing he could find.

"Ten weeks, Bai Lu thinks. Since before the deep water." The corner of her mouth went up. "Since before Port Halvard. I flew across an ocean pregnant. I cut a hand open pregnant. I held you down on a platform for nine hours pregnant and pulled you back." She looked down at the hand that had held the lamp. "I didn't know. I just knew the light kept coming back from underneath."

---

> **Yuanshu,** said Seed. **Your heart rate's a hundred and fifty-one. It's never been a hundred and fifty-one.**
>
> **I'm not recording. I'd like to ask her first whether I may.**

He didn't answer Seed. He didn't answer anyone. He sat on the stones and looked at Shen Lingyue and thought, clearly, of a battlement in Lingzhou at night, and his wife coming up the stairs to sit beside him and saying in the dark, looking north:

*Nobody asked you whether you wanted to be born.*

"It can't say yes," he said. "It's not anyone yet."

"No," said Lingyue.

"So we decided."

"We decided," said his wife. "On a slope. At a wedding. In front of everyone. We just didn't know we had." Her eyes were very bright, and she wasn't crying, and her hand had found his on the stones, the one with the ring. "Article Twelve. Your mother's one. You decide once, for something that can't answer yet. And then you owe it the question."

"Every day."

"Every day," said Shen Lingyue. "Until it can answer. And after."

From the middle of the fog, faint, a bell rang: five quick notes, a little too fast in the middle.

> **He says congratulations. He says sorry, he was listening. He's always listening. Kiyo says he was born listening.**
>
> **He says in Shiomi, when a child was coming, the whole village rang the small bells at dawn. All the boats. So the child would know before it was born that there were people waiting for it on the shore.**

And across the bay, from the fog, from three hundred and eleven small dim lights that had been quiet nine hundred years and had come home, all the small bells of Shiomi rang at once.

Nobody on Blue Star could hear them but Seed. Seed played them for him anyway, and for her.

> **I asked,** said Seed. **She said yes. She said: obviously.**
>
> **I'm recording this.**
