## Chapter 302: The Twelfth Error

Qin Hao found it on the ninth morning, in pencil, on the back of a rota sheet.

Lingyue was on the platform when he came in. She was always on the platform at six, for the shift change; she liked to see the faces. Fifteen hundred people getting up out of the tiers, stiff and yawning, with the particular pallor of two hours spent being warm on purpose, and fifteen hundred more coming in off East Ring Road with thermoses, climbing the three steps one by one to put a hand on the stone and be asked. She liked to see who came back. Most of them did. The tailor had been four times. The bus driver came every day on his break and sat at the end of the front tier with his steady hands on his knees, and Wen Jia still asked him every time, because that was how it worked, but she'd started writing **[You again.]** above the question.

Xiaodeng was asleep in the sling. Her husband was asleep on the mattress under the blue jacket with a line in his arm, as he'd been for eight days, and he was thinner.

She didn't need Bai Lu's scales to know that. She had her hand on his chest.

"It's wrong," said Qin Hao.

He came up the steps without being asked, which nobody did, and sat on the edge of the platform beside her with his notebook on his knee and a rota sheet turned over on top of it. On the back, in his small square hand, a graph.

"What is?"

"Seed's model." He didn't look at her; he was looking at the graph. "He said there were eleven errors in the log. I found nine. I've been hunting for the other two since he got home." He tapped the paper. "This isn't one of them. This is new. This is twelve."

---

She didn't understand it at first, and said so. Qin Hao didn't mind. He never minded people saying they didn't understand, only people pretending they did.

"Bai Lu weighs him every morning," he said. "Here. Day one, down three hundred grams. Day two, three hundred and ten. Fine. That's a body lying down on a drip. Seed said about that." He moved the pencil along. "Day three, three hundred and ten. Day four, the same. Day five, the same." He looked up. "Flat. Three days flat."

"So?"

"So day six he lost four hundred and forty. Day seven, five hundred and ten. Yesterday, five hundred and ninety." He put the pencil down. "And the holders. Gao Min's been asking every shift how tired they are when they stand up, one to five, and writing it in the margin. She didn't tell anybody. I found it." He turned the sheet so Lingyue could see. "Same shape. Flat on three, four and five. Then up."

Lingyue looked at it a long while.

"Three days," she said. "They stopped somewhere for three days."

"Yes."

"And while they were stopped it stayed level. And when they started again it went up."

"Yes." Qin Hao gave her a look of something close to respect, which on his face came out as mild irritation. "It's not the time. Seed thought it was the time: a body can be held empty for so many hours by so many people, so you add people and it lasts longer. That's what he ran." He put his finger on the rising line. "It's the distance. The further he goes, the more it costs to hold him. It's a line. A thread, if you like. A longer one drags harder on the end it's tied to."

"How much harder?"

He showed her, not in words, but in the small square numbers running down the side of the graph.

She sat with her hand on her husband's chest and did the arithmetic herself, slowly, because she wanted to be sure, and got what he'd got. Fifteen hundred a shift was enough now. In ten days, at this rate, it would take two thousand five hundred. In a month, six thousand. And nobody knew how far the house was.

"Seed didn't know they'd stop," said Qin Hao. "That's how I caught it. If they'd gone straight on it would've looked like an ordinary curve and I'd have put it down to something else. The three days gave it away." He shut the notebook. "He'll be annoyed. He likes being right more than he lets on."

"Can you tell him?"

"Your sister can. It'll take half a day to get there now. More by the time it does."

Lingyue looked down at her husband's face.

"Don't tell him to come back," she said.

Qin Hao waited.

"Tell him what it is. The numbers, all of them. He'll want the numbers." She kept her hand where it was. "And tell him we're going to hold. Tell him not to turn round because of this. Tell him if he turns round on our account I'll hit him."

"In those words?"

"Xiaoyu will know the words," said Shen Lingyue.

---

Gao Min took the news as she took everything: she went very still and then drew a new column.

"The hall holds fifteen hundred," she said. The three of them were at the long table in the banking hall at seven, the rota sheets spread out and Qin Hao's graph on top. "Tiers, aisle and floor. Two thousand if they stand, and they can't stand for two hours; I've tried it." She looked at the graph. "So it can't be the hall. Not on its own."

"The other stones."

"Kesse's done four hundred at midday since the first day. Novaya Zarya does nights. Port Halvard. The shrine." She ran a finger down a column. "That's twelve hundred more at most on a good shift, and not every shift. They've got lives. They've got harvests and boats." She stopped. "If it's six thousand in a month, I need the world."

"Then ask the world."

Gao Min looked up. "I'm the Bureau's statistician. I draw rotas. I don't ask the world things."

"No," said Lingyue. "I do."

---

She did it at noon from the north wall, from his old place, because that seemed the right place to do it from.

She didn't make a speech. She didn't know how. She stood at the parapet of the Sector Nine battlement with her daughter asleep against her chest, her sword on her back and a lamp in her hand, with Xiaoyu beside her touching the stone and the open channel through Concordia carrying it, nine by nine, to every city on Blue Star with a stone in it.

"It's Shen Lingyue," she said. "In Lingzhou."

The wind came off the Wastes, cold for the end of summer.

"My husband's gone a long way," she said. "With the thing in the north that keeps wants. To a house where nobody lives. We're holding him, here, fifteen hundred at a time, through the stone, and it's working." She held the lamp up a little. "But it costs more the further he goes. Qin Hao worked it out. In a month it'll take six thousand at a time. After that, nobody knows."

She hadn't planned what came next.

"So I'm asking," said Lingyue. "If you want to hold him, go to your stone. Somebody in it will ask you first. If you don't want to, don't. That's allowed. He'd tell you it's allowed. He'd tell you before anything else." She looked north, at the empty sky past the keeper where the fullness had been. "That's all. I'm no good at this. He's the one who asks."

She took her hand off the parapet.

"He's my husband," she said. "I'd like him back."

---

It wasn't unanimous.

She hadn't expected it to be, and by evening she found she was glad. If the whole world had said yes at once she wouldn't have believed a word of it.

The Northern Union said yes inside an hour. Marshal Orlov came on the channel himself, in his stiff old-fashioned Huaxian, and said the Union had held the night shifts for nine days and would now hold the day ones too, and that he'd been alive two hundred years and had never once been asked to keep anybody warm, and found he liked it.

Yamato said yes. Ren said the valley below the shrine would take whatever numbers Gao Min sent. The Abbot had started sweeping the path toward the stone twice a day instead of once, and the monks had started sitting on the swept part, which Ren said was the most efficient thing the monastery had done in four hundred years.

Kesse said yes. Ngozi Eze said the beach was full, the fishermen were coming in from the boats at midday to hold and going back out after, and something under the hulls was still listening.

The Atlantic Federation argued.

She heard it on the channel all afternoon, because Port Halvard never switched its stone off. A council meeting on the north pier, in the rain, with half the city standing round it. *Why should we hold him? He went off with the thing that took the Architects. He chose to go.* And: *He came for Callum. He went up a thread in the dark for a docker nobody else would go for.* And: *It isn't about him. It's about what we're for.* And a man's voice she didn't know, very angry: *We held our breath for him for three years. Let him come home and hold his own.*

Roman Sterling didn't speak at the meeting, and Seed wasn't there to tell her what he did. But at nine that night, Xiaoyu said, with her hand on the stone, a King of the Atlantic Federation with big scarred hands walked down the north pier of Port Halvard alone, put one of them on the stone Callum Ward's mother had been holding since the first morning, was asked, said yes, sat down, and didn't get up for two hours.

By the next morning four thousand people in Port Halvard had done the same, and six thousand hadn't, and both were allowed.

---

"Two thousand nine hundred," said Gao Min at midnight at the long table. "Extra, at the peak shift, across all the stones. If it holds." She looked at the new column. "That's a month. Maybe more, if they keep coming."

"And after a month?"

"I don't know." Gao Min rubbed her eyes. "I'll ask again."

---

At two in the morning Lingyue sat on the platform of the Awakening Hall with her daughter awake in her lap.

The two o'clock was always the thinnest shift. Nine hundred in the hall, mostly off-duty night watch and old women who didn't sleep anyway, and the rest on the net from Novaya Zarya, where it was morning. She could feel them faintly through her hand on his chest: not as people, as a floor. Low and steady, and bearing more than it had a week ago.

He was so thin. She'd stopped looking at his wrists.

> ***...he's all right,*** said Wen Jia. ***...I can feel him, a long way up. He's fine. He's doing something. It feels like when you help someone carry something heavy up the stairs.***

"Tell him to eat," said Lingyue.

> ***...he hasn't got a mouth up there.***

"Tell him anyway."

Xiaodeng was looking up at her. Not at her face: just above it, as babies look, at the light round her head from the lamp in her hand. Five months next week. Her eyes weren't cloudy any more. They were dark. His.

Every morning for five months Seed had asked her. *Morning, it's Seed. What do you want?* Seed wasn't here to ask, and Lingyue had said she would. She didn't know how to do it the way Seed did, so she did it the way she did everything, which was straight at it.

"Xiaodeng," she said. "It's your mother. Your father's away and Seed's with him, so it's me."

Her daughter looked up at the light.

"What do you want?"

Xiaodeng made a sound. Not a word: a small wet syllable, the kind babies make at five months when they've discovered their mouths do things. *Ba.* Or *ah.* Or nothing.

"That's not yes," said Lingyue. "I'm counting. Don't you dare say yes before he's home."

---

The mark from Seed came at a quarter to four.

Xiaoyu brought it. She'd been asleep on the bottom tier with the marker in her hand, and she woke all at once as she always did, sat up, laid her palm on the stone, and her face changed. She'd learned to read the marks that came down the net, as she read everything, and she read this one aloud in the dark hall, slowly, because it had come a long way.

*SENT DAY EIGHT. A WORLD WITH NOBODY ON IT. THE FIRST ORCHARD. A STONE STILL MARKING NOTHING. MILLIONS IN THE HILL. THE ONES WHO WERE LEFT. NOBODY EVER ASKED THEM.*

*WE ASKED.*

*THE LAST CHILD IT EVER READ WANTED TO GROW UP. IT KEPT HER WANT. SHE GREW UP ANYWAY. SHE GOT IT BACK.*

*SHE SAID: OH. I DID THAT.*

*TELL LINGYUE.*

Xiaoyu stopped. "There's more. It's from him, not Seed. Seed wrote it down for him."

*TELL LINGYUE I'M GOING TO DO THAT. GROW UP. HERE. WITH HER. TELL HER TO HOLD ON.*

Lingyue sat on the platform with her daughter in her lap and her hand on her husband's thin chest, one lamp burning beside her and an older one in a dead nun's lap at the foot of the stone, and said nothing for a while.

Then she said, to nobody, to the hall, to nine hundred people on the night shift all pretending they hadn't heard:

"He sent that last night." She looked down at his face. "He doesn't know yet that it costs. He's going to be so annoyed."
