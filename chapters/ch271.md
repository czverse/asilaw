## Chapter 271: Light the Lamp

The stone was too hot to touch, and he touched it anyway.

He didn't think about it. He came down the aisle of the dark hall at a run with the staff in his hand and Old Seven behind him, took the three steps, and laid his free hand flat on the black stone with the blue jacket on its shoulder, which he hadn't touched since the morning he turned eighteen, because Wen Jia was screaming.

It burned. Not his hand: something under his hand, as the light had burned against the door in his chest in the deep water. Heat poured down out of the thread into the stone, down through the crown of the tree of Laws toward the knot at the bottom where a girl of seventeen sat holding.

And the stone read him.

He felt it start. The runes, sliding round and round under the pressure from above, swung toward his hand. Toward him. Toward the place above his eyes. READ WHAT RIPENS. OPEN IT THAT WAY.

> ***...no,*** said Wen Jia. ***...not him. You ask first.***

The runes turned away.

> ***...captain's boy, get your hand off my stone. I've got enough to hold.***

He didn't take it off.

*What's coming down?*

> ***...them. Eight hundred and ninety-seven, burning. The Old Chord's singing them down the thread like you'd push a boat off a beach. They're nearly here.***
>
> ***...if they reach the knot, the stone will read them, all of them at once, and open them. The old knot's still in here under me. READ. OPEN. It's what it does. I've been holding it back eight months, one child at a time.***
>
> ***...I can't hold back eight hundred and ninety-seven at once. It'll open them all the way, like the hammer man, and they'll burn up in here, inside my stone. And the Old Chord will sing that the hunger ate the tithe.***

---

> **I'm here,** said Seed. **I'm holding the knot with her, through you, through your hand. The others are holding too, along the threads. The old man at Port Abiri. The docker. The girl in Yamato. The one who counts the no's. All eleven, holding the knot, not the thread.**
>
> **It isn't enough. The push is too big. Eight hundred and ninety-seven, and the Old Chord singing behind them, and the whole thread hot.**
>
> **Six minutes. Maybe seven.**

He stood on the platform with one hand on the burning stone and the staff in the other, and had nothing.

He couldn't cut the thread. Wen Jia had said months ago that the threads were how the eleven heard one another, and if they were cut the Old Chord would come down to the ground instead. He couldn't open the door; the chosen weren't dead. He couldn't ask them: they were burning in a thread, being pushed, and the only word they had was *chosen*, and they'd say it, and the stone would read it and open them.

He couldn't do anything.

Behind him, the side door banged open.

---

Lingyue came down the aisle.

Not fast. She couldn't do anything fast; she was eight months pregnant and Bai Lu had forbidden her to run, fly, fight, or carry anything heavier than a bowl. She came down the aisle with one hand on her stomach and the other on the seat backs as she passed, and the light was coming off her as it did in the mornings, pale, all round her, so the aisle lit up as she came, like a path being drawn on the dark.

And behind her came Ninth.

Three meters tall, translucent, her own small light showing through her like a lamp through paper, ducking through the side door, her throats opening and closing. And behind Ninth, one after another, the two hundred and fourteen: every Singer who'd come down the road and stood before the stone and been asked. Tall and pale, their own lights low inside them. Filling the aisle. Filling the hall.

"She woke me," said Shen Lingyue. "Ninth. She came to the window. She said her age-mates were being pushed down the thread and she didn't know what to do." She reached the foot of the steps. "She said, on the Hollow Bell, when a boat is lost in fog—" She stopped.

> **She says they don't have boats,** said Seed. **They've never had boats. They're a world of glass and singing.**
>
> **But the man in the fog in Yamato told them, on the first night, while he was teaching me their song. He said on his world, when the boats are lost in the fog, a woman on the shore lights a lamp, and the boats come in.**
>
> **Not pushed. Not pulled. They see the lamp and they come in.**

Lingyue looked up at him on the platform.

"They're boats," she said. "In a fog. Being pushed off a beach in the dark."

"Yes."

"So light the lamp," said his wife.

---

She didn't wait for him. She never had.

She climbed the three steps slowly, hand on her stomach, and stood beside the burning stone. She held up her other hand, palm open, toward the roof, toward the thread rising from the stone's crown through the roof into the heart of the shell, as she'd held it out at the end of a breakwater in Shiomi with a lamp in it for a nun of twelve hundred years.

And she lit.

Not as she'd lit on the north wall over Lingzhou, everything at once, burning like a star come down. He watched her not do that. He watched her face as she chose not to: jaw set, eyes shut, the light pouring up out of her from underneath as it had poured all winter, wanting to go all the way, and her holding it. Not burning. Held. A lamp, the size of a lamp, pale and steady. The color, Sister Kiyo had said, of whale oil in fog.

She held it up toward the thread, and it went up.

Not thrown. Not falling uphill, as she'd learned to throw light a lifetime ago. It went up the thread as a lamp is seen from a boat: not travelling, just there at the end of everything, steady, so that whatever was in the dark could see where the shore was.

> **They can see it,** said Seed. **In the thread, burning, pushed, eight hundred and ninety-seven of them. They can see a lamp at the bottom.**

---

And Ninth sang.

Not the song. Not the one sentence of ten thousand years. She stood at the foot of the platform with her throats open, three meters tall, translucent, her own small light inside her, and sang one note: the note she'd sung on the first morning when the stone wrote *chosen* over her head and her light went out to her own.

One, in one throat, very small.

Then another, in a second throat. A different note. Hers too.

Then the two hundred and fourteen behind her in the aisle opened their throats. Not all of them. Eighty-one: the ones Gao Min had marked in the column she'd headed *Notes*. Eighty-one Singers who'd come down the road and been asked and sung one note that wasn't in the song, and they sang them now, each their own. Not together, not a chord. Eighty-one different small notes rising through the roof of the hall, up the thread into the dark, toward a shell over the city where the Old Chord was singing a new sentence to push eight hundred and ninety-seven burning children off a beach.

> **It isn't a song,** said Seed. **It hasn't got a sentence. It doesn't mean anything. It's eighty-one people each singing the one thing that's theirs.**
>
> **Ichiro says: oh.**
>
> **Ichiro says: that's a boat call.**

---

And from very far away, from a bay in Yamato, through Ren's phone and the keeper's eye and Seed and the staff and the stone and the thread, came bells.

The small bells of Shiomi. Not the warning. Not the name. The boat calls. Every boat's call in the village, the headman's and the priest's and the five quick notes of the boat Ichiro's father built, all rung at once from the fog of the bay, as the shore rang them when a fleet was coming in through the dark and the women wanted every boat to hear its own name. Three hundred and eleven people who'd been quiet nine hundred years, ringing every call they had. And with them, from the end of the breakwater, very small, a twelve-hundred-year-old woman holding up a lamp that was Lingyue's light.

Two lamps: one on a breakwater in Yamato, one on a platform in Lingzhou. And between them in the thread, the eight hundred and ninety-seven.

---

They stopped.

His hand was still flat on the burning stone, and he felt the push in the thread stop. Not because the Old Chord had stopped singing; he could feel the new sentence still pouring down from the heart of the shell, the dark sentence nobody else was allowed to hear. But what it was pushing had stopped moving. Halfway down the thread, eight hundred and ninety-seven children of the Hollow Bell, dressed in light, burning, shoved off a beach in the dark, had seen a lamp at the bottom and heard eighty-one boat calls they didn't know they knew, and stopped.

As boats stop in a fog when they hear the shore.

> **They're holding on to each other,** said Seed. **In the thread. The way the Candle's children hold on, each to the one in front. Ninth taught them. In the courtyard she watched the Candle's children do it, and she sang it up to them. Not in words. In a note.**
>
> **They're holding on, and the Old Chord's pushing, and they're not moving.**

---

Then one of them came down.

Not pushed. He felt the difference, as you feel the difference between a boat shoved off a beach and a boat being rowed. One burning light came out of the stalled mass in the thread, slowly, toward the bottom, toward the lamp, toward the knot where Wen Jia sat.

It stopped just above the knot.

> ***...hello,*** said Wen Jia through the staff, very tired. ***...you came down. You weren't pushed. I felt it.***
>
> ***...what do you want to become?***

And the burning light above the knot said, in a shape like a chord, very small:

> **She says: I don't know yet. I want to see the lamp.**

> ***...that's allowed,*** said Wen Jia. ***...come out the side. Not through me. Out the side of the thread. The keeper's got a road. Come down it and see the lamp.***

And the light went sideways, out of the thread onto the keeper's road, which had come down out of the north without being asked because the keeper had heard the word *lamp* and knew what it meant. Down it, through the roof of the Awakening Hall, burning, dressed in light, came a child of the Hollow Bell onto the platform, and stood before Shen Lingyue, who was holding up a lamp.

Then another came.

And another.

---

He took his hand off the stone at eleven minutes past four. It was warm. Only warm. The runes had stopped sliding and pointed down.

The thread was empty.

Not all of them had come down the road. Seed counted. Eight hundred and fifty-six had, one after another down the keeper's gold road through the roof, burning, to stand before a woman holding a lamp. Some went to the stone and put their hands on it and said *chosen*, and the stone said *you came down*. Some didn't go to the stone at all. They stood in the aisle beside Ninth and the two hundred and fourteen and looked at the lamp, and their light, slowly, of its own accord, began to go down.

Forty-one hadn't.

> **They were too near the front,** said Seed. **When the Old Chord pushed, they were first off the beach. They were farthest down the thread when the others stopped.**
>
> **They burned out in the thread, before the lamp. Before they could see it. They're dead.**

He stood on the platform.

*Ask them,* he thought. *First.*

> **Yes.**

He went down into the Death Law, to the cool dark place where the door stood a little open, and asked: forty-one embers, very small, very bright, in a thread over a city on a world they'd never heard of, who'd been dressed in light since before they could stand and pushed off a beach in the dark toward a mouth that wasn't there.

*Do you want the door?*

And forty-one small voices said, in a shape like a chord, the only word they had.

*Chosen.*

Then one of them, after a long time, in a different shape, small and very new:

*...the lamp. I saw the lamp. Just at the end. I saw it.*

*...yes. The door. Please.*

He opened it, and they went through, forty-one, to wherever finished things go.

> **Death Law: 55.9 to 56.3 percent.**

---

He turned round.

Lingyue still stood by the stone with her hand held up and the lamp in it, pale and steady. Her eyes were still shut and her jaw still set. The aisle was full of tall pale translucent children of the Hollow Bell with their own small lights low inside them, looking at her.

"You can put it down," he said. "They're in."

She didn't open her eyes.

"I can't," said Shen Lingyue.

"It's all right. They're in. It's done."

"No," said his wife through her teeth. "I mean I *can't*. I can't put it down." She opened her eyes. They were very bright. "I've held it too long. It's holding me back. It's coming up from underneath and it won't stop and I can't put it down." Her other hand pressed flat on her stomach. "And something's happening."

He looked at her. At her white face. At the hand on her stomach. At the polished floor under her bare feet, where in the pale light of a lamp that wouldn't go out, the black stone was wet.

> **Yuanshu,** said Seed. **Her heart rate's a hundred and forty. The baby's is a hundred and sixty-two.**
>
> **Bai Lu's on the road. She was in bed. I asked her first. I'm asking her now.**
>
> **It's four weeks early. It's coming.**
