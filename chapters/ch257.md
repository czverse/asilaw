## Chapter 257: Home

The dead came home all morning.

He watched from the platform of the Awakening Hall, sitting against the warm stone with a blanket somebody had put round his shoulders and a cup of Fat Tan's tea going cold in his hands, because Bai Lu had said that if he tried to stand she'd sit on him. He couldn't see them; nobody could. But he could feel them, and so could Seed, and so, Gao Min said, could her laser, which jumped every time a column of them came down the thread into the stone and went sideways into the net and home.

Twelve thousand embers. Down eleven threads into eleven stones, out through the knots where eleven people from the bottom of a well sat holding, and along the net nine by nine, slowly, to the War Memorial Cemetery and the slope by the river and a valley in the Northern Wastes and the Western Pass and stone No. 7.

The Spear Corps first, two hundred and seventy-two. He felt them come down into the Lingzhou stone in a knot, together, as they'd gone up, and out through Wen Jia, and away. Fifteen minutes later Kwan, who'd driven out to the cemetery at dawn with the laptop, rang to say the bones round stone No. 7 had stood up all at once and faced out.

Then the First Legion in their rows of nine. Then the cemetery companies. Grid Nine came last of the Lingzhou dead, slowly, and paused in the stone a moment before going home, and sent a message up the net that took four hours to arrive.

> **They say they saw the sea and the deep water in one week,** said Seed. **They'd like to sit down for a while.**

---

Old Seven came at noon.

He hadn't come down the Lingzhou thread. He'd gone up it lying on his back on the floor of the Awakening Hall beside the platform, spear across his chest, a finger and a thumb closed round it. His bones had lain there twelve hours and nobody had touched them, except Fat Tan, who at four in the morning had slipped a folded blanket under the skull, very carefully, and then gone and stood by the side door with his arms folded and his eyes wet and dared anybody to say a word.

At noon the bones stood up.

Slowly, as he did everything. One hand on the spear, pushing up off the floor. Two meters ten. He stood beside the platform a moment, the blanket fallen at his feet, and turned his skull to the boy sitting against the stone with the cup of tea.

> **He says Port Halvard. They took the man down the Port Halvard thread, the sergeant and him, and the docker in the stone caught him. The docker said: steady, lad. Steady. You're home.**
>
> **Then they came across sideways along the threads under the world. The sergeant's coming the long way with the Sixty. He says they'll be a day.**
>
> **He says the man's down, in the hall by the sea. His mother was there.**

Yuanshu put the cup down.

*Was he—?*

> **He says ask the King on the radio. He was there. He saw. It's not for a captain to say what a mother looked like.**

---

Sterling was in a wheelchair.

He said so himself on the radio at twenty past twelve, in a voice that wasn't quite steady. The doctors had said a month before he could decide for anything bigger than a door. They'd said nothing about wheelchairs. He'd made them wheel him down the hill from the hospital to the harbor at four in the morning, when Seed's message came down the net, and park him at the head of the roofless hall beside the black stone, and leave him there.

"She'd been there since the hand," said Roman Sterling. "Since before. She never went home. She had her hand on that stone every day for six weeks. And every morning she brought something with her and put it on the floor beside her, and every night she took it home again."

"What?"

"His jacket." Wind off the Atlantic on the line. "His work jacket. Port Authority, orange, with the reflective strips. He'd left it on the back of a chair the night he went in through the window with the hammer. She said she wasn't having him come home with nothing on. She said it every morning, to the stone. I heard her."

Yuanshu said nothing.

"He came down at nine minutes past eleven," said Sterling. "Out of the top of the stone. The docker said something; I didn't hear it. I heard her. She didn't scream. She said *Callum*, the way you'd call somebody in for his tea." The King of Force was quiet. "He came down into the jacket on the floor, like the sixty-one came down into their coats. He was lying on the floor of the hall in an orange work jacket and he was a man. Not burning. Just a man. Thin. Gray. Twenty-five. Eyes open."

"And?"

"And he looked up at her and said: Mum. I told it. I held it up and said oi and it looked."

A pause.

"And she said *what do you think*," said Roman Sterling, "and then she sat down on the floor and put her arms round him, and I made them wheel me out, because it wasn't for me to see."

---

The platform was quiet.

Then Lingyue, who'd sat beside him against the stone since six that morning with her hand on his arm and the flame in her other palm, said: "Is he all right?"

"He's tired," said Sterling. "The doctors say he'll be tired a long time. He's lost weight. He doesn't remember most of the six weeks. He remembers the fire and the voice and the bit at the end." A pause. "He asked for a bacon sandwich. They said he couldn't have one. He said he'd been the brightest thing in the dark between the arms of the galaxy and he'd like a bacon sandwich, please. They gave him one."

On the platform Xiaoyu laughed out loud with her hand over her mouth. She hadn't slept either.

"And Sterling," said the King of Force.

"Yes?"

"He asked me what grade I was at eighteen." A long pause. "I told him. SSS. Ironclad. He said, and did it tell you what you were? I said yes. He said, was it right?"

The line was quiet.

"I didn't know," said Roman Sterling. "I'm seventy. I've been a King fifty years. And a docker with an F asked me if the rock was right, and I didn't know." He breathed out. "I'd like your sister to put that on the wall too. Small. Not in red."

---

The Sixty came the next morning, the long way, and didn't come down any thread.

They came up out of the ground.

Kwan saw it first. She'd gone back to the cemetery at dawn because Seed said the sergeant was close, and she rang the Ossuary at 5:51 as the sun came up over the river, and couldn't speak for a while, and then said: "Stone No. 7."

They all went. Gao Min drove, because Kwan had stopped answering her phone, and they climbed the long green slope from the river in the early light between the rows of small gray stones and past the cypresses to the eastern field.

The Spear Corps stood round stone No. 7, facing out, as they always stood.

And between them and the stone, in a ring in the grass: ash.

Not bones. The bones of the Third Battalion were at the cold fire, where they'd stood at the edge of the quiet part four hundred and forty days and sixty-odd more, and there they'd stay, Seed said, because they'd been left facing out and the dead don't like to be moved once they've been left facing something. What had come home wasn't bones. It was what the dead keep when there's nothing else: their shape. In ash. As Hundred-Arrow Lin had been a shade of ash with an iron arrowhead for a heart, on the north wall, for two years.

Sixty shapes of gray ash standing in a ring in the grass round stone No. 7 in the gray light, facing out.

At the front, a short one, square in the shoulder, with the shape of a pick over it.

---

Old Seven walked up the slope.

He hadn't come in the car; he never did. He'd walked from the Awakening Hall in the dark, and he came up the long green slope between the small gray stones as the sun rose over the river, two meters ten, spear grounded in a finger and a thumb, slowly, as he'd walked out of this cemetery on the first night two years ago at three in the morning, when a boy with a training spear and no permit asked him to.

The Spear Corps made room for him without being asked.

He stopped in front of the sergeant.

The ash shape of Huo Changsheng looked up at him, then down at the left arm, the sergeant's own arm, with a finger and a thumb.

> **Huo says: sir. We're home.**
>
> **Old Seven says: yes.**
>
> **Huo says the cold fire's empty. There's nobody left in there to stand in front of. He asked them all every morning, and nobody's answered for a month. He thinks they've all gone through. Old Seven says: I know. You told me on the thread. Huo says he's telling him again, so it's said here, on the grass.**
>
> **Huo says the Third Battalion stood at the edge of the cold four hundred and forty days so the cold came to them first, and then some more. Now they're home. And he'd like to stop facing out for a bit, sir. If that's all right.**

Old Seven was quiet a long moment.

Then he turned round. Not out: in. Toward stone No. 7, the small gray stone on the slope with a number and no name, the one he'd come out of on the first night. He turned his back on the river and the city and the rising sun and faced the stone, and laid his spear down on the grass.

> **He says: at ease.**

The sixty ash shapes of the Third Battalion turned round, in, toward the stone and their captain.

And sat down on the grass.

---

He stood at the edge of the eastern field with the staff in his hand, Lingyue beside him and Xiaoyu on his other side with her marker, and watched sixty shapes of gray ash sit down in the grass of the War Memorial Cemetery round a small stone with a number on it, at sunrise, with a tall dead captain standing among them facing in, for once, and not out.

He found he couldn't say anything.

Lingyue took his hand, the one with the ring.

"Say it," she said. "You say it every morning."

He looked at the short square shape of ash at the front with the pick across its knees.

"Huo Changsheng," he said. "Sergeant. Third Battalion. You held."

The ash shape didn't turn round. It was facing the stone. Facing in. At ease.

> **Huo says: I know. You can stop saying it now, boy. I'm home. Say it to someone who isn't.**
>
> **Say it to the ones in the deep water.**

---

That evening Xiaoyu climbed the stepladder in the banking hall and wrote at the end of the INSIDE column, below the queen of hearts on its single pin, below *HUO CHANGSHENG. SIXTY. AT THE COLD FIRE. FACING OUT. SOMEONE IS WAITING WITH THEM* and *SAY HIS NAME*:

*HOME. STONE NO. 7. FACING IN. AT EASE.*

And on a new column round the corner, at the top of the last of the white plaster:

*THE WATCH OF THE SHELF. 1,146. NANA AMPOFO. YAW, WHO ASKED FIRST.*

*IN THE DEEP WATER. ASKING. NINE BY NINE.*

*SAY THEIR NAMES. ASK THEM AGAIN IN A YEAR.*
