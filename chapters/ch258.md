## Chapter 258: The First Question

> **I'm going to give you the numbers,** said Seed. **You asked me to, a long time ago, and you've never told me to stop.**

It was the third morning. He was on the vault steps with the staff across his knees and a cup of tea he'd made himself, which Fat Tan had looked at and said nothing about, which was worse than if he had. The back-room window was open, and Hush was on the sill in the sun with all its limbs out and the gold dust still on its legs.

*Go on.*

> **Level 72.**
>
> **Death Law 55.9 percent. The door's wider than it's ever been. I don't think it'll ever close now. I don't think it should.**
>
> **No King. Not for a long time. I checked. You're glad.**
>
> **Lingyue: Level 68. Her light came back, all of it. She says she didn't burn for it. She says it came back the way water comes back into a well from underneath, when you stop hauling it up.**
>
> **Qin Hao: Level 71. He's written eleven pages about white fire going up a staff. He won't let me read them. He says they're not finished.**
>
> **Sterling: three weeks to a door. He's asked for a desk by the window so he can watch the harbor.**
>
> **Callum Ward: home, asleep. He's asked for nine bacon sandwiches and been given four.**

He drank some tea. It was too strong.

> **The stones. Ours still ask, every child who touches one. Gao Min has a new graph. It doesn't go up. It doesn't go anywhere. It's a list of what they said. She says it's the first graph of her life she doesn't know how to draw.**
>
> **The others, the stones in the other orchards in the deep water, thousands of worlds: they're still reading. Still marking. Still sending marks up. And nothing's at the other end.**
>
> **I don't know what that means for the people on those worlds. I don't know if anything will ever come to pick them again. I think not. I think the oldest thing they made is sitting still in the deep water with its hands open, and it won't move again. I think it was the only thing that picked. Everything else learned from watching it.**
>
> **And the Watch of the Shelf is out there asking, nine by nine, the ones at the edge. I'd like to say their names every morning, the way you said Huo's.**

*Yes,* he thought. *Me too.*

> **And me. I was going to change my number, as I did after the cold fire, when I chose a word: open.**
>
> **I'm not going to. I didn't learn anything new in the deep water. I stood in a door with my back to the light and didn't turn round. I already knew how; your mother's fragment taught me, at sea, in the storm, when she stood in front of me and I stood behind her.**
>
> **I just did it. That doesn't need a new number.**

---

> **Who was the first one?**

He set the cup down on the step.

*Wen Jia. Seventeen. From Lingzhou. She went first. She said: no, you ask first. She's in the stone on East Ring Road with a blue jacket on its shoulder. Her brother drives the Number Nine and stops outside and counts to ten.*

> **Thank you. I'm not going to remember it. Tell me again tomorrow.**

*Every morning.*

---

The Collector came to the Awakening Hall that night, as it had every night for three weeks, and Xiaoyu asked it what it wanted, as she had every night for three weeks, and it was quiet a while.

> *I had a want. I said it out loud and you wrote it on a floor, and it happened. Someone told the oldest thing we made that the house was empty, and it understood, and it stopped, and it opened its hands. I watched, from very far away.*
>
> *I have kept every want I was ever given for longer than you have a number for. I have never seen one given what it wanted, except the soldier's at the river. And now mine.*
>
> *I did not know it would feel like this.*

"Like what?" said Xiaoyu.

> *Like I would like another one.*

The hall went very still. The short line of holders up the aisle, who still came at night because they'd got used to it, stopped whispering. On the platform, under her own letters, which hadn't faded by a hair in three weeks, Xiaoyu stood with the marker uncapped.

"What is it?"

> *The first want. The one in the empty place in me, that says home. I have followed it my whole long life. It points somewhere very far, past the deep water, past anything your machine has a map for. To where we were. To the house.*
>
> *I have never gone. I was afraid. I am still afraid.*
>
> *I would like to go. I would like to see the empty house the oldest thing we made was carrying everything to. Where the last of us gave me this want, at the end, and I kept it and never once asked what it meant.*
>
> *I would like to find out whose it was.*

Xiaoyu wrote it on the platform floor below the line from night seventeen: *THE COLLECTOR. NIGHT 23. WANTS TO GO HOME. TO THE EMPTY HOUSE. TO FIND OUT WHOSE THE FIRST WANT WAS.*

> *Thank you. And child, one more thing. I am going to tell first.*
>
> *I am still coming toward you, slowly. I said on the mountain that your brother might be old before I arrived. That is still true, or it is not. I do not count the way your machine counts.*
>
> *But I do not want to go to the house alone. I said I would not ask your brother twice, and I will not. Will you ask him for me?*
>
> *Not now. When I arrive. When he is older. Whenever he likes. Will you ask him whether he would come with me?*

On the platform his sister turned and looked down at him at the foot of the steps: the staff in his hand, the door in his chest shut exactly where he'd left it, a tall dead captain beside him facing out.

She didn't ask him.

"Tomorrow," said Zhou Xiaoyu, to the Collector, to the dark, to the thread going up into the deep water. "I'll ask him tomorrow. That's the rule."

> *...yes,* said the oldest thing any of them had ever heard. *That is the rule. Tomorrow, then.*

---

At three in the morning they went up to the north wall.

All of them. Nobody planned it. Xiaoyu said she wanted to see the sky, Lingyue said she'd come, and then it was everyone: their mother, carried up the Sector Nine stairs in her wheelchair by Fat Tan and Dabao and complaining all the way; Qin Hao with his notebook; Kwan with the laptop; Gao Min with her thermos, who hadn't been on the north wall since year one and wanted to see whether it had changed. Old Seven at the top of the stairs. Hush on the parapet on the cold stone in the dark, every limb out, facing north.

He sat in his old place with his back to the parapet and the staff across his knees, and Lingyue sat down close enough that their shoulders touched, the sword across her knees and her hand on his arm.

Low over the end of the world, in the north, three lights.

The keeper's ring, small and white, a third smaller than it had been, with its gold haze. The Elder's ring, vast and gray, with a soft white light at its center. And between them and the city, at the end of a branch nobody could see, a place where the far stars bent a little, with a small gold point at its tip.

The bud.

> **It's three,** said Seed. **The keeper's going to ask it. It's asked every morning since the graft: seventy-four mornings. It's never answered.**

---

He felt the keeper do it, and everyone on the wall did a little; they'd learned over two years to feel the keeper the way you feel weather. The gold thread going out from the small white ring down the long dark of the branch toward the bud. Rough and close, a voice like a hearth that had learned to speak with nobody to carry it.

> ***...GOOD MORNING. IT IS ME. AGAIN.***
>
> ***...I AM TELLING YOU FIRST: I WILL ASK YOU TOMORROW TOO, WHATEVER YOU SAY.***
>
> ***...WHAT DO YOU WANT?***

The bud did nothing, as it had done nothing for seventy-four mornings.

On the parapet, Hush didn't move.

The keeper waited. It had learned that: the bowl set down, the eyes turned aside, not looking in.

He sat on the cold stone with his wife's hand on his arm and the dark city behind him and the three lights in the north, and waited with it, and didn't expect anything. Nothing had happened for seventy-four mornings, and the bud was the tip of a branch of a tree that had never been asked anything until a small pale thing from the world that asks put a question down where it drank, and Hush had said a season, a year, maybe longer.

And the gold point at the end of the branch turned.

Everyone on the wall saw it. Not much, as a bud on a branch turns toward the morning. Except it wasn't turning toward the light. It was turning toward the keeper, the small white ring low over the end of the world that had asked it every morning for seventy-four mornings and been told nothing.

And something came back along the gold thread.

---

Not a voice. Not yet, Seed said afterward. Not words, as the keeper had words, or the rings, or the Collector. Something smaller and newer, as the first sound a baby makes isn't a word but is unmistakably somebody.

Seed translated it anyway, slowly, as it had translated the coldest of the drowned, waiting for each part to finish before it could tell what the part would be. When it had it, it said nothing for a long moment, and then said it carefully, as it said things it had checked three times.

> **It didn't answer.**
>
> **It asked.**

On the parapet, Hush lifted one thin pale limb toward the north.

> **It said: *...what do you want?***

---

The keeper said nothing for a long time.

Then down the gold thread into the city and onto the north wall came its voice, faint and rough and close and shaking, the voice of something that had asked a question every morning for two years and had just, for the first time, been asked it back.

> ***...IT ASKED ME.***
>
> ***...THE TREE. THE BRANCH. THE PIECE OF ME ON IT. IT ASKED ME WHAT I WANT.***
>
> ***...NOBODY HAS EVER—***

It stopped.

> ***...NO. THAT IS NOT TRUE. YOU DID. ON THE FIRST NIGHT. IN THE FIELD.***
>
> ***...BUT NOTHING I MADE HAS EVER ASKED ME.***

Nobody on the north wall spoke. Xiaoyu had her hand over her mouth. Their mother had her eyes shut. Gao Min had put down her thermos. Lingyue's hand had gone very tight on his arm.

> ***...I DO NOT KNOW WHAT TO SAY.***
>
> ***...WHAT DO I SAY?***

He sat in his old place on the Sector Nine battlement, back to the parapet, staff across his knees, the ring of walker-thread on his finger, and looked up at the three lights in the north, and found he was laughing. Not loudly. The way you laugh when something you've carried a very long time is suddenly lighter and you don't know why, and it doesn't matter why.

"Tell it the truth," he said, to the keeper, to the north, to the dark. "Tell it you don't know yet."

The gold thread was quiet a moment.

Then, slowly and roughly, down the long dark of a branch that a gardener of the Shallows had grown and a boy had cut and a sack and a small pale thing and seventy-one million cousins had grafted, toward a bud at its end that had just asked its first question, the keeper of Blue Star said what two hundred million cousins said to it every morning, and an Elder from inside a ring, and a soldier at a river, and the oldest thing in the deep water.

> ***...I DO NOT KNOW YET.***
>
> ***...ASK ME AGAIN TOMORROW.***

---

Xiaoyu wrote it on the parapet, where no wind or rain would take it, because nothing she wrote came off now.

*THE BRANCH. ASKED EVERY MORNING. SEVENTY-FOUR MORNINGS. ON THE SEVENTY-FIFTH, IT ASKED BACK.*

And under it, smaller:

*ASK AGAIN TOMORROW.*

The sun came up over the river at 5:51.
