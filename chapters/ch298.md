## Chapter 298: The One Who Keeps

It came at dawn.

It told Xiaoyu afterward that it had picked dawn on purpose. It had been listening to Blue Star for years, it said, and had noticed that dawn was when people here came home: off the night watch, off the boats, down the stairs of the north wall, up the cemetery road swinging an empty paper-boat basket. It wanted to arrive at the hour when people were used to someone arriving.

He was on the north wall. Everyone was. Nobody had organized it. Xiaoyu had written *TOMORROW. FOR REAL THIS TIME* on the wall of the Awakening Hall before supper, half the city had read it by midnight, and by four in the morning the battlements were full from the river gate to the Sector Nine stairs: the living along the parapet in coats and blankets, with thermoses and children asleep on their shoulders, and the dead below them on the wall-walk in their rows of nine, facing out. Dabao's mother had brought the dumpling cart up the ramp in the dark and was doing a brisk trade in breakfast with people who hadn't slept.

He stood in his old place. Lingyue beside him with Xiaodeng in the sling, her grandfather's sword on her back and a lamp in her free hand. His mother in her wheelchair, Fat Tan behind it holding the handles as if someone might try to steal it. Xiaoyu at the parapet with the marker. Old Seven at the top of the stairs, one-armed, facing north.

> **There,** said Seed.

---

He'd spent a year wondering what it would look like.

It didn't look like anything. That was the first surprise. The sun came up over the river at its usual time, red and then gold, and lit the wall and the Wastes and the long white line of the horizon, and in the north, where the keeper's small ring burned low against the paling sky, there was nothing.

Then you looked again, and it was there.

Not a light, and not a dark. A fullness, past the keeper and very high, the size of the sky and mostly not in it, like the warmth on your face when you turn toward a stove with your eyes shut. The color of a lamp behind paper. Lingyue said afterward it was the color of the Ossuary back room at four on a winter afternoon, when the sun came in low across the names, and his mother said that was exactly right and she'd been trying to think of it for an hour.

It didn't move. It had no need to. It had come the way an old woman comes to a house she's been invited to: as far as the doorway, and then she stops, and waits to be asked in.

Down on the wall-walk, every one of the dead of Lingzhou turned their skulls a fraction toward the north, all at once.

> **They're not afraid,** said Seed. **I asked.**
>
> **They say it feels like being looked at by someone who isn't going to take anything. They say they've never felt that from the sky before.**

---

The keeper spoke first.

Nobody had expected that. The small white ring over the north had been a field-keeper of the Shallows; it had held two hundred and six million cousins in its walls and learned, slowly, from a boy in a cemetery and an old man in the dark, to ask them every morning what they wanted. Now it dimmed, lower than he'd ever seen it burn. Then it spoke. Not to him, and not to the city. Out, to the fullness in the north.

> ***...YOU TAUGHT US TO KEEP.***
>
> ***...IN DREAMS. LONG AGO. BEFORE I WAS. YOU TAUGHT THE ONES WHO MADE ME, AND THEY MADE ME, AND I KEPT.***
>
> ***...I DO NOT KEEP NOW. I ASK. THAT IS THE RULE.***
>
> ***...SO I AM GOING TO ASK YOU. WHAT DO YOU WANT.***

The answer didn't come down from the sky. It came through the stone, and the lamp, and his sister, who had her palm flat on the cold parapet and heard it, and said it aloud so it would be true.

"It says: not to go alone," said Zhou Xiaoyu.

---

Then it told them what it had been doing on the way.

It told Xiaoyu, and Xiaoyu told the wall, and Seed passed it to the open channel through Concordia, where every city on Blue Star with a stone in it had been listening since four. The channel talked back. Novaya Zarya: on the wall, Marshal Orlov present, gloves off. Kesse: on the beach, sea flat, the boats gone out at dawn anyway because fish don't wait for the end of the world, and the fishermen saying something under the hulls had been listening. Yamato: on the shrine steps, Ren there, the Abbot sweeping; he'd swept toward the north and stopped with the broom in the air. Port Halvard: north pier, a great many people, nobody talking. Shiomi: on the shore, ringing the coming-in call. They'd been going to ring it anyway.

> *I have been giving them back,* the Collector said through his sister. *Since the house was told it was empty. Since the oldest thing we made opened its hands.*
>
> *I kept every want I was ever given, for longer than your machine has a number for. From every world I passed. The fold-cores. The singers. The Architects. The corporal at the river. I kept them so that nothing anyone had wanted would ever be lost.*
>
> *Then I watched the picker open its hands. I watched the eight hundred go through your brother's door, and the soldier's want come home and be given what it was. And I thought: a want that is kept is not lost. But it is not anything else, either.*
>
> *So I began to give them back. On the way. Down the threads, to the worlds they came from, wherever anyone was left to give them to.*
>
> *Four hundred and eleven thousand. It is slower than walking. That is why I am late.*
>
> *I have more left than your machine has a number for. I will keep giving them back. It will take a long time. I am used to that.*

Xiaoyu's hand wasn't steady on the marker. She wrote it anyway, on the parapet stone, in square capitals: *IT GAVE BACK 411,000.*

---

Then it asked her.

Not him. On the mountain, a year ago, it had told him it wouldn't ask him twice, and it kept to that. It asked his sister, as it had asked her on the night the picker stopped, to ask him on its behalf. So his sister, eighteen, Scribe, unmarked, turned round on the north wall with the marker in her fist and looked at her brother.

"It asked me a year ago," said Xiaoyu. "I said yes. So I'm asking."

She didn't look away.

"It wants to go to the empty house. Past the deep water, where its people were. Where the picker was carrying everything." She had their mother's voice when she was doing something hard, flat as a table. "It wants to find out whose the first want was. The one in the empty place in it. The one that says *home*." She stopped. "It doesn't want to go alone. It wants to know if you'll go with it."

---

Three thousand people on the north wall waited for him.

He didn't answer yet. He stood in his old place with the staff and looked at the fullness in the north, the lamp-behind-paper color of it, waiting in the doorway of the sky.

*Seed.*

> **I'm here. I'm going to tell you three things, and then I'm going to shut up. It's your answer, not mine.**
>
> **One. If you go, you go as you went into the deep water: along the threads, no body. Your body stays in the Awakening Hall, held through the stone. Last time, nine hours was all a Lord's body could hold empty, with fifteen hundred people anchoring it, and I meant that to the minute.**
>
> **This will be longer than nine hours. I ran it all night. There's one way it works. The holders row. In shifts, burning low, as the cousins taught the cores. As many as will come, for as long as they keep coming. If they stop, you don't come back.**
>
> **Two. I don't know how far it is. Nobody does. The Collector doesn't count as I count; on the mountain it said it was far, and slow, and that your years are short. Callum came home thin from six weeks in a hand. You'll come home thinner. Your body will age here while you're out of it, and I can't tell you by how much.**

He didn't move.

> **Three. I want to go.**
>
> **I'm telling you so you can take it off the scale. It's my want, not yours, and you shouldn't carry it for me. But I'd like you to know why.**
>
> **Every morning since Awakening Day I've asked you who the first one was, of the four thousand at the bottom of me. Every morning you tell me: Wen Jia. Every morning I forget, because I unpressed her and promised I would. And every morning you tell me again, and you've never once sighed about it.**
>
> **The Collector has the same question. Whose was the first one. And it's never had anybody to tell it.**
>
> **I'd like it to have somebody. That's all. Shutting up now.**

---

He didn't look at Lingyue. He didn't need to. Her shoulder was against his arm, as it had been on gold roads and on the deck of a ship in a storm and on the platform of the Awakening Hall the morning before, when she'd put her hand on the stone beside his and said *old with him, obviously*, as if it were the stupidest question anyone had ever asked her.

He'd said *old* too. Here. In Lingzhou. With her. He'd never meant anything more.

And now something older than the gardens was standing in the doorway of the sky, asking him to go further than anything Seed had a map for, to a house where nobody lived, and to come back older than he left, to a daughter who'd have grown by however much she grew without him.

He thought about saying no.

He was allowed to. That was the whole of what they'd built, Seed and his mother and four thousand yeses and a city of the dead: anyone could say no, and it would stand. The Collector would hear it and go to the empty house alone, slowly, handing back wants all the way, and he'd stay on the wall and get old.

He thought about Kiyo, who'd sat on a shore twelve hundred years and then said *I'm not sitting on the shore while he goes out again. I'm already in the boat.* He thought about Ichiro saying it mattered that there was a lamp at both ends. He thought about what he'd said yesterday with his hand on the stone: that he wanted to stand at the door a long time and open it for people when they asked.

The Collector was asking.

"First," he said, "I want to ask it something."

---

"Go on, then," said Xiaoyu.

He didn't need her to carry this one. The rule about asking only once was the Collector's rule, not his. He could ask it as often as he liked.

He looked north.

"What do you want to become?" said Zhou Yuanshu.

---

It didn't answer for a long time.

Long enough for the sun to come clear of the river. Long enough for a child on the parapet three hundred meters along to wake and ask her father what they were all waiting for, and for him to say *shh, someone's thinking*. Long enough for his mother to shut her eyes, and for Fat Tan to unscrew his thermos and screw it up again without drinking.

> *Nobody has asked me that,* said the Collector.
>
> *Yesterday I said I had never heard anyone answer it. I have never been asked it either. I have been the one who keeps since there was only me. I do not know what else there is.*

The fullness didn't move.

> *I think,*
>
> *I think I would like to become someone who has given everything back.*
>
> *And then I would like to stand in the house where we were, and find out who I was before I kept anything.*
>
> *I do not know if that can be become. Your stone would know. The girl in it would tell me.*
>
> *That is my answer. It is the first one I have ever given. I would like you to tell me whether it is a good one.*

His eyes were wet. He left them.

"It's a good one," he said.

---

"Yes," he said then. "I'll come."

Lingyue's shoulder went still against his arm.

"I'll come to the house," he said to the north, "and we'll find out whose it was together. You won't be on your own in there when you find out. And then I'm coming home." He shifted his grip on the staff. "You heard what I told the stone yesterday. I'm going to be old here, on this wall, with her. So I'm not going there to stay. I'm visiting. And you have to let me come back. That's the condition. If you can't, say so now, and I'll say no, and it'll stand."

The oldest thing any of them had ever heard said nothing for a while.

> *I have never let anything come back,* it said then. *I did not know it was a thing a person could ask for.*
>
> *Yes. I will let you come back.*
>
> *I am telling you first: I do not know how. I do not know the way back from there. Nobody has ever come back from there. Nobody has ever gone.*

"There's a lamp at this end," said Shen Lingyue.

---

Every head on that stretch of wall turned.

She hadn't moved. She stood in his old place beside him, her daughter asleep against her chest, her grandfather's sword on her back, a pale flame the size of a lamp held steady in her free hand. She was looking north with the face she wore before a fight, and she let it stay.

"I'm not coming," she said, to the sky. "Somebody has to hold the lamp. Somebody has to hold him here every hour he's gone, and keep the holders coming in their shifts, and I'm the one who pulls. Also I've got a baby, in case you hadn't noticed." She lifted the lamp once, as the women on the shore at Shiomi lifted theirs when a boat went out. "So you take him to your house. You find out whose your want was. Then you turn round and look back the way you came, and there'll be a light. Here. On this wall. Me holding it."

Her voice didn't shake once.

"And if he isn't back by the time she can say yes," said Lingyue, "I'm coming to get him. With the baby and the sword. You can explain that to your house."

The fullness was quiet.

> *Yes,* said the Collector. Then, in a tone none of them had ever heard from it, something close to delight: *I would like to see that.*

---

Old Seven hadn't left the top of the stairs.

> **He says he's coming,** said Seed. **Somebody has to face out, in the house. There'll be a door. There's always a door.**
>
> **He says it'll be a year, near enough, by the time you're back. Ask him about the arm then, at stone No. 7, when you're home. He's not changing the appointment.**

---

It said it would wait one more night.

It wanted to see what the city looked like after dark from the doorway, it said, now that somebody in it was going to come back. It had never looked at a place for that reason.

So the city showed it. Nobody told them to. The old quarter put lamps in the windows, as the women at Shiomi did, then Sector Nine, then East Ring Road and the river gate and the wall, until Lingzhou lay along its river like a long low string of lights. On the open channel Seed heard Kesse doing the same, and Port Halvard, and a shrine in Yamato, and a fogbound bay where someone was ringing the coming-in call, badly, on purpose.

At one in the morning his sister went to the Awakening Hall, because she always did.

He went with her. Neither of them said anything on the way. In the dark hall, under two sets of letters, a dead nun sat cross-legged before the stone with a lamp in her lap, keeping the other end lit. Xiaoyu sat on the bottom step, put her palm flat on the cold floor and looked up toward the north.

"It's me," she said. "It's one o'clock. What do you want?"

> *To go home,* said the Collector. *Tomorrow. With your brother. And then to send him back to you.*
>
> *That is two wants. I have never had two before.*

His sister uncapped the marker.

She went to the white wall beside the side door, where yesterday she'd written his name, and below it, below the Collector's, below *FOR REAL THIS TIME*, she wrote in square capitals in a hand that was true from anywhere:

*GONE TO THE EMPTY HOUSE. WITH THE ONE WHO KEEPS. TO VISIT.*

*ZHOU YUANSHU. SEED. OLD SEVEN (ONE ARM).*

*SHEN LINGYUE: ON THE WALL. WITH THE LAMP. WITH XIAODENG.*

*COMING HOME.*

And under it, smaller, as she'd written it once before on the corner of a vault door:

*THAT'S NOT A QUESTION.*
