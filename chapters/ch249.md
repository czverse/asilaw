## Chapter 249: Bright Enough

He had a decision to make. He'd known he would since Shuanghe.

He'd worked it through a thousand times with Seed on the gold road over the sea. The hand saw only bright. The holders were bright because the stone had opened a little holding in each of them: fifteen thousand little brights in a line. The hand would go down the line taking them one by one, faster than the dead could cut, until there was nobody left holding.

Unless there was something brighter.

Not Lingyue. She'd promised, and so had he. *Not like that.*

Him.

*Docker,* he thought, down through the staff into the black stone beside him, into the knot at the bottom where a man who'd hauled ropes on the Atlantic docks for thirty years before something sang now sat holding a thread and sending nothing up it. *It's me, the captain's boy. I'm going to ask you something. First I'll tell you what I'll do with the answer.*

> ***...go on, son.***

*I want you to open me. Not all the way, not like that night in Lingzhou. A little. Toward bright. Just enough that I'm the brightest thing in the harbor.* He gripped the staff. *So it comes for me instead of them.*

The docker was quiet a moment.

> ***...and then?***

*Then I go and stand at the end of the north pier, away from the line, and it comes for me. And nineteen thousand dead it can't see are standing round me when it does.*

> ***...you'll want it. The burning. You'll want it like your missus wanted it on her wall. Like the lad wants it out there.***

*I know.*

> ***...Seed. Will you hold him?***

> **Yes. I'll stand where his mother's fragment used to stand, in front. I won't let it open further than he asks.**

> ***...right then,*** said the docker. ***...what do you want to become, son? Say it proper. The stone's fussy.***

He put his hand on the black stone.

**[What do you want to become?]**

"Bright enough," said Zhou Yuanshu. "For five minutes. So it looks at me."

**[Chosen.]**

---

It was the best thing he'd ever felt.

He'd felt it on the platform in Lingzhou and in the lantern's fire and thought he knew. He hadn't. The door in his chest opened a little, exactly as far as he'd said, and light came through, and everything he'd ever touched burned up in him at once: the Death Law, the pale fire, the Field, the net of the dead, bright and brighter. He wanted it. All of it. More. He wanted the door to swing all the way open.

And Seed stood in front of the door.

He felt it, not as words but as a shape: something built in a basement out of a harvest sack and four thousand people's yeses, standing in the doorway of his chest with its back to the light, as a woman had once stood in front of it when something tried to look into the staff. Not holding the door shut. Holding it where he'd asked.

> **This far. You said this far.**

He walked out of the roofless hall and down the steps and along the quay toward the north pier, burning.

---

Fifteen thousand people turned to watch him go. He felt them do it: a boy from Lingzhou with a white staff, lit from inside like a lamp in a window, walking down the quay past the line toward the end of the pier, away from them.

Above the harbor, the hand turned.

All its hundreds of fingers and millions of threads lifted off the line, out of the hair and shoulders of the people they'd been about to take, and swung up and round, slowly, as the crown of the tip had swung toward Lingyue on the wall.

Toward the brightest thing.

He reached the end of the north pier. The Atlantic in front of him, black, the wind off it, the stars. He turned and planted the staff on the stone.

The hand came down.

---

And they were there.

He'd told them on the gold road that when he walked to the end of the north pier some of them should already be waiting. Not on the roofs, not in the line. On the pier itself, on the bollards and the cranes at the end of the quay. And in the water.

The harbor at the end of the pier boiled. Out of the black Atlantic, streaming, rose the Watch of the Shelf: eleven hundred and forty-six of the drowned off Kesse, who'd walked five days along the bottom of the ocean in the dark because it was the same sea and went all the way round. Nana Ampofo at their head with water pouring off her coat. They rose round the end of the pier in a ring, banked, cold, invisible, with rusted boat hooks and anchors and lengths of chain in their hands.

On the pier round him stood Old Seven, and the Spear Corps, two hundred and seventy-two of them in a ring facing out, and Grid Nine, forty, who'd never seen the sea and were looking at it now.

The hand came down onto the brightest thing in the harbor, and every finger that reached for him came down through a ring of dead it couldn't see.

He gave them the fire.

Not one thread each this time; he'd worked that out too. Pale fire was Lingyue's light and his Law, and he was burning now, opened, bright enough for five minutes. He didn't send the fire down the dead. He poured it. Everything the docker had opened in him, all the brightness the hand had come down to take, he turned round and poured through the staff into the dead around him, so that every rusted spear and boat hook and anchor chain on the north pier didn't burn pale.

It burned white.

And beside him, healed pink hands open, Qin Hao let the white fire that burns what binds go into it.

---

The fingers came apart.

Not cut: unbound, as the branch had come apart over Lingzhou. The white fire went into every cut the dead made, found whatever held the next thread to it, and burned that: the pressing, the weave, everything that made a finger the width of a street into a finger.

They fell into the harbor by the hundred, silver-gray, unravelling as they fell, thread drifting down over the black water like snow.

> **Level 70 to 71,** said Seed. **71 to 72. Yuanshu, four minutes.**

And in the palm, as the fingers came apart around it, something was shining.

He saw it at the last moment, as the palm came low over the end of the pier, wide as the harbor mouth, open, emptied of fingers. In the center, where a hand holds what it's picked, a cluster of lights. Small, dim, hundreds of them, burning low as the cousins had burned in the Low Fire. Held.

Sixty-one from the quay at Port Halvard, bright and new.

And under them, older and dimmer and many more, from places he didn't know the names of. Other harbors. Other orchards. Picked and held in a hand and carried for longer than he could understand.

> **It carries what it picks,** said Seed. **In the palm, until it goes back to the rest of itself. They're still there, all of them, burning low.**
>
> **Three minutes.**

---

He didn't open the door. Not yet.

He stood at the end of the pier, burning, white fire pouring out of him into the dead and fingers falling into the harbor all round, and asked first: through the staff, up into the palm of a hand the size of a harbor, to sixty-one people from Port Halvard and hundreds from places he didn't know.

*It's me. I'm from Blue Star. I'm asking. Do you want to go home, or through the door, or stay where you are?*

From the sixty-one, all at once, as a crowd answers when it's been standing in the cold waiting for someone to ask: *home.*

And from the older ones, the dim ones from other orchards, slowly and faintly, in languages Seed didn't have, a word Seed didn't need to translate.

*Door.*

"Lingyue," he said. "Now."

His wife came down out of the sky onto the end of the pier with her grandfather's sword in both hands and her light in it. Not burning, not everything at once, nothing like the wall. Careful. Exact. Her old way, the way she'd been taught at eighteen and had hated: bend it, don't burn it. She cut the palm open from one side to the other in a single stroke of pale light, as a fisherman slits a net to free what's caught.

The lights fell out.

The sixty-one came down onto the quay at Port Halvard like sparks, into their own coats, into the arms of the people who'd been holding their shoulders, and woke gasping on the stones with the faces of people who've been dreaming of falling.

The old ones, the dim ones from other orchards, went past him at the end of the pier and through the door at the bottom of the Death Law, which he opened the rest of the way for them, to wherever finished things go.

So many. He didn't count them. Seed did.

> **Eight hundred and three. From eleven worlds.**
>
> **Death Law: 48.8 to 50.6 percent.**
>
> **One minute. Let me close the door in your chest.**

*Yes,* he thought. *Close it.*

Seed did, gently, all the way. The light went out of him and he sat down hard on the end of the north pier in the cold Atlantic wind, with nothing in his chest but his own heart beating a hundred and forty, and wanting, wanting, wanting the light back.

He held on to the staff and didn't ask for it.

---

What was left of the hand went back up the thread.

The wrist, the cable of pressed thread where it had slid down out of the dark, didn't let go and fall as the branch's tip had. It went up, slowly, as a hand is drawn back when the arm it belongs to has felt something it doesn't understand. Up into the dark toward the rest of itself.

> **It's going back,** said Seed. **It'll tell the rest what it found. A world where the stones are quiet and the dead can't be seen and the bright things walk to the end of a pier on purpose and the palm can be cut open.**
>
> **I don't know what the rest of it will do with that.**
>
> **And it saw you. Bright, for five minutes. It knows what you look like now.**

At the head of the harbor, in the roofless hall, a woman in a docker's jacket still had her hand flat on the black stone, and fifteen thousand people still had their hands on the shoulders in front of them. Roman Sterling was sitting on the granite floor with his back to the stone and blood on his chin, his enormous hands open on his knees.

"Put it on the wall," said the King of Force, to nobody, to the sky. "I decided for a stone. It stayed."

On the radio at Yuanshu's belt, faint, from Lingzhou, his sister's voice:

"I'm writing it. In red. Come home."
