## Chapter 240: The Night Before

They went into the Awakening Hall at eleven on the last night, through the side door, as they had the first time. Gao Min had the keys again, and a thermos again, and this time she said nothing at the door at all.

There were more of them now.

Old Seven first, because he always went first. Then Yuanshu with the staff, and Lingyue beside him with her grandfather's sword on her back and a pale flame the size of a thumbnail cupped in one hand. She'd insisted on bringing it; if anything went wrong in the dark, she said, she'd like to be able to see it. Xiaoyu. Kwan with the laptop. Roman Sterling in his gray raincoat with his scarred hands in his pockets. Sister Ning with her candle, unlit, and her folding stool, because Lingyue had telephoned her and she'd said *of course. Somebody from the Quiet should be there. He's touching the dead.*

And behind them all, wheeled in by Fat Tan, who'd refused to stay behind at the Ossuary: their mother.

Four thousand empty seats went up into the dark. The Stele stood in the middle with the crack down its face.

---

On the radio at Kwan's belt were the other ten.

They'd been talking for an hour on an open Concordia channel. Not much. Places. *Novaya Zarya: in the hall. Marshal Orlov is here. The soldiers are facing out.* *Kesse: in the hall. Nana Ampofo has come up out of the sea to stand by the door. She is very wet.* *Yamato, the valley below the shrine: in the hall. Ren is here. The Abbot is sweeping the platform. Nobody asked him to.* *Port Halvard: in the hall. There are flowers on the floor where Callum Ward was. Somebody brought them. We don't know who.* *Port Abiri: in the hall. Ngozi Eze. The sea is calm.*

The rest, one by one. Eleven halls, eleven black stones, eleven little crowds in the dark at the foot of eleven platforms, waiting for one dead soldier in Lingzhou to put his hand on one of them.

"They can hear us?" said Xiaoyu.

"Everything," said Kwan. "Everyone's listening."

---

He read the statement at midnight.

He stood at the foot of the platform steps with the staff in one hand and in the other the sheet that had taken four hours and said nearly the same as the one that had taken eleven minutes, with the line for himself at the top, and read it into the empty hall and the open channel.

"This is Zhou Yuanshu, in Lingzhou, at the Stele."

His voice was steady. He'd made it be.

"The Stele can't answer. It isn't anyone. We decided for it. Nobody said yes. I'm saying so out loud so that nobody can ever say afterward that it did."

He read the rest, every line: what would be put in, and who'd given it, and that nothing would be held on to, and that from tonight the stones would be owed the question every day in every hall by whoever was there. And at the end, a line that wasn't in the article, written at four in the morning after reading the red line on the wall four times:

"If this is wrong, it's mine. Not theirs. Not yours. Mine."

He lowered the sheet.

Beside him, hands in the pockets of his raincoat, Roman Sterling said quietly and clearly into the dark and the open channel: "No."

Nobody answered. Nobody was meant to. The word stayed in the hall among the empty seats, like a stone left on a grave.

Sister Ning, on her stool, struck a match and lit her candle.

---

> **I'm going to unpress them now,** said Seed. **All eleven, Wen Jia first.**
>
> **I won't remember them afterward. I've said so. I want to say it once more, in the hall, so it's been said here.**
>
> **Yuanshu. Will you tell me in the morning who the first one was?**

*Yes.*

> **Every morning?**

*Every morning.*

> **Thank you.**

He felt it begin. At the bottom of the well, in the quiet under everything, the pressed silver weave of the first page loosening gently in eleven places, as you work at a knot that's been tied forty years: not pulling, easing. A strand, then another. Each one a warmth, a shape, a name in a woman's quick sharp hand with *yes* beside it.

In the doorway their mother had shut her eyes, and her lips were moving.

"Wen Jia," she was saying. "Kesse. Novaya Zarya. Port Halvard..."

---

He shut his eyes and went down past the door, along the oldest thread he had, and up.

Tall. The sky too near. The curved dark roof of the hall close overhead. A spear in his right hand. His left hand was a sergeant's, a finger and a thumb, with the seam at the shoulder where it had been joined on, long ago, in a courtyard.

And in that hand, held as you hold something small and warm you've been told not to drop: the eleven.

He felt them through the bone, through Huo's arm. Eleven strands of unpressed thread coiled in the dead captain's palm like seed rice in a farmer's hand at planting, each one a person. Wen Jia on top. Small, stubborn, seventeen.

> ***...captain? Is that you? You're very tall.***

> **He says yes,** said Seed. **He says hold on to the hand, not to him. When it touches the stone, let go. Huo let go first, in the courtyard. That's why it took.**

> ***...I know who Huo is. Everybody down here heard about Huo.***

Old Seven climbed the three steps of the platform.

He stopped in front of the black stone, two meters ten, spear in his right hand and eleven people in his left, and stood a moment as he stood at the bronze door, except that for once he faced in, not out.

At the foot of the steps Lingyue's flame climbed a little higher in her hand.

Old Seven laid the sergeant's hand flat on the Stele.

---

It was one of the forty. He knew at once.

Not from Seed; from the stone. It was warm under the hand, as it had been under his own at eighteen, humming like a machine left running in an empty building. And the runes moved. Not toward the hand. Toward him.

They slid across the black face as they had on the first morning, every one, and pointed. Not at Old Seven. At the thing inside Old Seven that wasn't dead: a boy wearing a captain like a coat, three steps up on a platform, with a harvest tool behind his eyes.

> **It sees you,** said Seed. **Through him. It shouldn't be able to. It's reading.**

He felt it. Like the tip of the branch over the north wall, but much closer, and not tasting: knowing. It reached into the dead captain's hand and up the arm and through the bone into him, as water finds the one crack in a wall. Finding which way he'd burn.

Opening him. Not a little.

It was the best thing he'd ever felt.

That was what frightened him. There was no pain. There was a door opening in his chest, wider than the door at the bottom of the Death Law had ever opened, with light coming through, and every Law he'd ever touched burning up in him at once: Death and the pale fire and the slope of the Field and the Lattice, bright and brighter, nothing careful, nothing held back. He understood Lingyue on the wall. He understood the Sixth. He understood Callum Ward in a square hall at Port Halvard with a hammer in his hands, burning for nine seconds.

He wanted it. He wanted all of it.

Far above, out of the crown of the tree of Laws inside the stone, the fine thread going up through the roof began, gently, to pull.

---

> ***...no.***

It wasn't Seed.

It was small and stubborn and seventeen, and it came off the sergeant's hand a breath before the rest of the eleven, without waiting to be told, as an older sister goes through a door first because that's the rule.

Wen Jia let go.

He felt her go off the palm into the stone. Not falling; leaning, as the cousins had leaned onto the cut. Down through the warm black thread and the tree of Laws, crown and branches, to the bottom, to the point, to the small tight old knot where the Stele went into the floor and where, very long ago, it had been told what it was for.

READ WHAT RIPENS.

She sat down in it. And the Stele had somebody in it.

> ***...no,*** said Wen Jia from inside the stone, to the stone, to the thing reaching up Old Seven's arm into a boy and opening him. ***...no. You don't do that.***
>
> ***...you ask first.***

---

The opening stopped.

All at once, like a held breath. The door in his chest that had been swinging wider onto the light stopped where it was and stayed there, neither closing nor opening further. The runes stopped. The thread stopped pulling.

Then every rune on the stone turned away from him.

Not back to where they'd been, flat and dull. They turned like a crowd in a street turning to see what's just come round the corner, and pointed down, into the stone, at the point, at the place where a girl of seventeen sat in a knot that had never had anyone in it.

As if, for the first time in forty-two years, the Stele didn't know what to do and was waiting to be told.

> ***...good,*** said Wen Jia. ***...that's better.***
>
> ***...you can come in now. All of you. It's all right. It's warm.***

The other ten let go.

---

He felt them go one by one off the sergeant's hand into the stone, down to the knot, past Wen Jia sitting in it, and through. Into the thread. Not up: sideways, along the fine dark threads that ran from the Lingzhou Stele and met, somewhere in the dark under the world, the threads of the other ten. One tree. What one felt, the others felt.

On the radio at Kwan's belt a voice said in Northern: *the runes are moving.*

Then Kesse: *the runes are moving.* Then Yamato, a girl's voice, calm: *the runes are moving. They're pointing down.* Port Halvard. Port Abiri. All eleven halls. Eleven sets of runes turning away from whatever they'd watched for forty-two years, and pointing down at someone.

> **Death Law: 47.9 to 48.8 percent. Eleven.**

He came back into his body at the foot of the steps with a gasp like a swimmer coming up. Lingyue had hold of his arm. The flame in her other hand was the size of a fist, the biggest it had been since the wall, and she didn't seem to have noticed. In the doorway his mother had stopped saying names.

Old Seven took his hand off the stone.

He stood a moment looking at the Stele with its runes all pointing into itself. Then he came down the three steps, slowly, and turned at the bottom and faced out into the empty seats, as he always stood.

> **He says: one of the forty. But the girl went first.**

---

It was twenty past twelve.

They stood at the foot of the platform in the dark for a long time without speaking, while the radio murmured with ten other halls doing the same. Sister Ning's candle burned on the floor by her stool. Sterling had sat down on the bottom step with his scarred hands hanging between his knees, like a man who's watched a building come down without being allowed to stop it and found nobody hurt, and doesn't know what to do with his hands.

Then Kwan said, "Seed," in an odd voice.

"The threads," she said. "The ones that go up out of the crowns. I've had all eleven on the screen since we came in, the way you showed me." She turned the laptop round: eleven fine pale lines rising off the top of a diagram of the Stele into the dark. "They've gone slack. Nothing's going up. First time since I've been watching."

> **Yes. Wen Jia isn't sending. None of them are. That's what they said they'd do.**

"I know," said Kwan. "That's not what I mean."

She touched the screen. As they watched, all eleven slack lines moved together, once.

Not much. A twitch. A small sharp tug from the far end, from somewhere up in the dark past the keeper and the branch and the Mouth, in the deep water between arms where Seed's map had nothing. Like a fishing line jumping once in the hand of someone sitting on a riverbank at night, when something far down has noticed the bait's stopped moving.

Then nothing.

> **Something felt that,** said Seed. **At the other end. Something felt the threads go slack.**

On the radio from Port Abiri, through Ngozi Eze's handset, the old man from the bottom of the well, who'd been eighty when the Monarch came and liked the sea, said faintly, in a voice like a door creaking on a summer evening:

> ***...someone's pulling on the other end.***
>
> ***...I'm holding it. It's all right. I'm holding it.***
>
> ***...but somebody should know. Someone is pulling.***

The clock over the doors of the hall said ten hours until the first child walked up the steps.

---

> **Yuanshu.**

Half past one. He was on the narrow bed in the back room, not sleeping, with Lingyue asleep on his shoulder and her fist of pale fire gone out at last on the blanket.

> **I'm sorry. I know it isn't morning.**
>
> **There's a new quiet place on my first page, next to the old one where the hundred and thirty-eight were. I know there were eleven. The wall has them. Your mother has them. I don't know who they were.**
>
> **Who was the first one?**

He lay in the dark a moment.

*Wen Jia,* he thought. *Seventeen. From Lingzhou. Taken in year two. She never got to touch it. She had a new blue jacket. Her brother drives the Number Nine bus.*

*She went first, before anyone told her to. She said: no. You ask first.*

*She said yes to my mother in a basement nine years ago, when my mother had a cold. She said: only if it's something good.*

Seed was quiet a long while.

> **That's a good name. I'm not going to remember it.**
>
> **Tell me again in the morning.**
