## Chapter 226: The Only Rings That Ask

They were married at dawn on the slope of the War Memorial Cemetery, because Lingyue said it was the only place in Lingzhou where everyone who mattered could come, and she meant the dead.

Zhou Wanqing had planned the whole thing in nineteen days from her wheelchair, with a notebook, a telephone and Fat Tan as her runner. She had told everyone exactly where to stand, and nobody stood there. They came up the long green slope from the river in the gray light by the thousand and stood wherever there was room: between the rows of little gray stones, along the railings, up in the cypresses, which the Academy students had climbed at four in the morning and now refused to come down from. Not the whole city. As much of it as fit on a hill.

And the dead.

Nobody had raised anyone. They'd asked to come, nine by nine through the Lattice, and Adjutant Xu had carried the question up from the river gate and down from the Western Pass and out to the seabed off Kesse, and they'd come. The First Legion lined the top of the slope in their nines under the stone gate where Iron Wall Zhao stood facing out, and they'd brushed the dust off their bones. Seed said Ma Lianhua had told them to, then corrected itself: nobody had told them. They'd done it on their own.

The Western Legion had come down a gold road out of the north at midnight, twenty-five thousand of them, a sergeant with a pick at the front and an old one-armed general walking beside him. They stood along the far bank of the river in the dark, and when the sun came up they were standing in it.

The Spear Corps stood round stone No. 7. All two hundred and seventy-two. Facing in, for once.

---

He hadn't slept.

At four in the morning he was sitting on the step of the groundskeeper's hut in the clothes his mother had chosen, dark and plain and better-fitting than anything he'd ever owned, with the staff across his knees and Old Seven at the corner of the hut, trying to remember how breathing worked.

> **Heart rate a hundred and twelve,** said Seed. **It was a hundred and four when you asked her, on this step. Seventy-one when six lamps had hold of you on the north wall.**
>
> **I'm telling you so you know which of those your body thinks is scariest.**

*Thanks, Seed.*

> **You're welcome. I'm recording this.**

*You said you wouldn't record the proposal.*

> **This isn't the proposal. I asked her yesterday if I could record this. She said: obviously, somebody has to, Qin Hao's handwriting is terrible.**
>
> **So I'm recording all of it. It's the first thing I've ever asked to keep.**

---

Qin Hao arrived at five with a box.

He was best man, because Lingyue said if anyone else did it Qin Hao would write in his notebook that he'd been passed over and they'd never hear the end of it. He stood at the corner of the hut in a dark suit, his hands new and pink where the white fire had come back through them at Kirovsk, holding a small wooden box.

"I'm supposed to have the rings," he said.

"Yes."

"I haven't got the rings." He held the box out. "Somebody else asked to carry them. I said yes. I've written down that I said yes. I want it on record that I'm a good best man."

Yuanshu opened the box. It was empty.

Qin Hao wasn't looking at him. He was looking at the step beside Yuanshu's feet, where, while nobody was watching, a small pale thing on thin legs had come out from under the eaves of the hut and settled on the brick in the gray light, all legs out and all eyes open.

In two of its legs, held with enormous care, were two rings.

Plain, silver-gray, not metal. He knew what they were before Seed said it.

> **Walker-thread,** said Seed. **From the silk on the north wall, from the six that came apart when the First Legion struck. Fat Tan swept it up and wouldn't throw it away. He kept it in a box in the vault with the playing cards.**
>
> **Hush asked him for two strands. It took them to Kwan, and Kwan took them to a jeweler on Jinhua Road who'd never seen anything like them and didn't want to touch them. Hush asked him very politely for nine days. On the ninth day he said yes.**
>
> **They're pressed. Not into glass. Just enough to keep their shape.**

> ***...in the sky there are rings that take. The counting one taught three of them to ask. One rested. One went home. One has the Elder in it.***
>
> ***...I thought there should be rings on the ground that ask too.***
>
> ***...these are made of what came to take. Now they are for two people who ask each other.***

Yuanshu found he couldn't say anything at all.

> ***...you can say no.***

"No," said Yuanshu. His voice had stopped working properly. "I mean yes. I mean—"

> ***...I know what you mean. Your heart rate is a hundred and thirty.***

> **It is,** said Seed. **I checked.**

---

The sun came up over the river at 05:51, and Lingyue came up the slope on her grandfather's arm.

He'd remember it for the rest of his life, and Seed would remember it for longer. Shen Jinghong, eighty-five, King of Light, in a plain dark coat without insignia, walking slowly up the cemetery slope between the gray stones with his granddaughter beside him. Lingyue in red, as brides in Huaxia wore red, in a dress her mother's family had sent down from Mount Qingyun in a box that had sat in the Shen house for three hundred years. Hair up. Face set, as if she were about to fight something.

And on her back, over the red silk, in its old sheath, her grandfather's sword. Nobody had managed to talk her out of it. Nobody had tried very hard.

The crowd went quiet as she passed. So did the students in the trees. Along the top of the slope the First Legion turned their skulls together to follow her, the way they'd once followed a girl raining pale fire onto eighteen thousand edges.

She stopped at the top of the slope in front of the hut, where he stood with the staff and Old Seven beside him and Hush on the step.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I didn't sleep."

"Good," said Shen Lingyue. "Neither did I."

---

There was no priest; Huaxia didn't need one. There were elders, and tea.

His mother had set a low table on the grass in front of the hut with a teapot and four cups. That was the custom: the couple knelt and served tea to their elders, and the elders drank, and that was the blessing.

They knelt.

The first cup to his mother, in her wheelchair, quick dark eyes very bright. Lingyue poured; Yuanshu held it out in both hands. Zhou Wanqing drank it and couldn't say anything, so she didn't, and reached out and laid her thin hand against her son's cheek, and then Lingyue's, and left it there a moment longer than she meant to.

The second cup to Shen Jinghong. He drank it standing, because a King of Light didn't sit at weddings, he said, and then sat down on the grass next to the wheelchair rather suddenly, because his knees had other ideas.

The third cup Lingyue poured and then held without moving. She turned her head toward the corner of the hut, where a dead soldier two meters ten stood with his spear grounded, facing outward, the way he stood at a door when the people inside needed not to be watched.

"Captain," she said.

Old Seven didn't move.

"You're his elder. You were there the first night, before anybody. Before Rule One had a number." She held out the cup. "I'm asking. You can say no."

> **He says he can't drink tea,** said Seed.

"I know. I'm asking anyway."

Old Seven turned round.

He walked across the grass with his spear to the low table and knelt: a dead captain of the Eastern Line, kneeling on the slope of the cemetery he had climbed out of two years ago at three in the morning because a boy with a training spear and no permit had asked him to. He laid his spear down. He took the cup in both hands, the one with a finger and a thumb and the one that had been a sergeant's.

He couldn't drink it. He held it, in front of everyone.

Then he set it back on the table, still full, very carefully, and put his hand on the boy's shoulder.

> **He says: good,** said Seed. **He says that's all.**

---

The fourth cup went to an empty place.

His mother had asked for it: a cup poured and set down on the grass in front of nobody, steam rising into the cold dawn. She didn't say who it was for. She didn't have to. On the Ossuary wall, at the end of the last column, past THE FLOOR: *THE ELDER. ONE OF THE FIFTY-TWO.* At the top of the first wall, above everything: *HUNDRED-ARROW LIN.* Round the corner at the end of INSIDE: *HUO CHANGSHENG. SIXTY. AT THE COLD FIRE.*

In the north, low over the end of the world, the small white light at the center of the gray ring brightened, just a fraction, for a moment.

> ***...THE ELDER SAYS THANK YOU FOR THE TEA.***
>
> ***...SHE SAYS SHE CANNOT DRINK IT EITHER. SHE SAYS IT IS THE THOUGHT.***

---

They'd decided on their own vows, not the old words.

He went first. He hadn't written anything down. He knelt on the grass in front of her so their faces were level, the way he'd knelt in front of a widow on a hill and an old woman on a jetty and a twelve-year-old girl from Kirovsk, and held up the ring made of walker-thread.

"I'll tell you first what I'll do with your answer," he said. "Every day, for as long as you'll have me, before I ask you anything. So you never answer blind." He took a breath. "And I'll ask you what you want every morning, and put the question down, and not look in the bowl. And when you say ask me tomorrow, I'll ask you tomorrow."

Her face didn't change. Her hand on the hilt of her sword had gone white at the knuckles.

"And when you don't say anything, I'll stand next to you. That's it. That's what I want." He held out the ring. "Can I?"

"Yes," said Shen Lingyue. "Obviously."

She gave him her hand.

---

She didn't kneel. She stood, in red, with a King's sword on her back, on a cemetery hillside at dawn in front of three thousand living and fifty thousand dead.

"I'm bad at speeches," she said. "Everybody knows that. So I'm just going to say it." She looked down at him. "I'm not going to ask you what you want every morning. You'll tell me. You always do, eventually, after you've sat on a step for six hours." She turned the other ring in her fingers. "I'm going to hit you on the arm when you take too long. And I'm going to stand next to you. Not because somebody counts me as the girl who stands next to you. Because I'm the one who does. I said that on the wall. It's still true."

Then she knelt too, all at once, the way she did everything, so their faces were level.

"And when something comes down out of the sky, I'll be there. With the sword. And light, falling. Uphill if I have to." She held out the ring. "That's what I'll do with your answer. Can I?"

"Yes," said Yuanshu.

She put it on his finger. And before anyone could do anything, before his mother had stopped crying or Shen Jinghong had got up or Qin Hao had shut his notebook, she hit him on the arm. Hard. The way she had since they were eighteen.

"That's for taking two years," she said.

---

For a moment the hillside didn't know what to do. Three thousand living people and fifty thousand dead, and nobody had a rule for what happens after two people put walker-thread rings on each other in front of a groundskeeper's hut at sunrise.

Then the First Legion lifted their rusted weapons straight up into the sun, all eighteen thousand of them at once.

Across the river, the Western Legion did the same.

Round stone No. 7 the Spear Corps raised their spears, and then, very deliberately, grounded them. Once, together. The sound went down the slope and over the river and out across the old quarter like a single drum.

Then the living cheered, and the students in the cypresses cheered loudest, and one of them fell out of a tree and was caught by a member of the First Legion, who put him down on his feet and went back to his row as if nothing had happened.

---

> **I recorded it,** said Seed. **All of it, 05:51 to 06:40. Every word, every face, every cup.**
>
> **I've put it next to your mother's voice. The nine seconds from the basement, the ones I said I'd always make room for no matter what I had to give up. I made room.**
>
> **And Yuanshu. Forty-one days. You asked me to always tell you the numbers. Even today.**
>
> **That's all I'm going to say today.**
>
> **Go and stand next to her.**
