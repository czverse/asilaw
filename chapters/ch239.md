## Chapter 239: Easier

He wrote the statement in eleven minutes, and that was how he knew something was wrong.

It was the fourth night after the Number Nine. He was at the long table in the banking hall at two in the morning with a sheet of ledger paper, writing what would be read aloud in eleven halls on Awakening Day before anything was done to anything. Article Twelve said you had to. *Say aloud that you decided, and that nobody said yes.* So he wrote it.

*The Stele cannot answer. It is not anyone. We decided for it. Nobody said yes. We are putting into it only what was freely given: eleven people who chose to go. We will hold on to nothing. We will ask it every day.*

He read it over. It was good: short, clear, true, everything the article required in the right order.

He looked at the clock over the vault door. Eleven minutes.

The first time, on the battlement, he'd sat on cold stone for an hour unable to answer Seed at all. He'd needed his wife to come up the stairs and tell him that every person who ever lived had been put into the world without a yes. He'd needed his mother at breakfast. Eleven lines for Xiaoyu's wall had taken him two hours, with three crossings-out and a tear where the pen went through the paper.

This had taken eleven minutes. No crossings-out.

He put the pen down.

> **I noticed too,** said Seed.

*When?*

> **Minute six. You didn't stop. Last time you stopped every line. I didn't say anything. I wanted to see if you'd notice by yourself.**

He looked across the hall at the wall. At the head of the last column, ARTICLE TWELVE, and under it, small, in red, in his sister's capitals:

*R. STERLING: NO. BECAUSE IT GETS EASY. READ THIS EVERY TIME.*

He hadn't read it tonight. He'd walked past it to the table and sat down and written.

He got up and went to the wall and read it aloud to the empty hall. Then he went back to the table, tore the sheet in half, and didn't write anything else that night.

---

Roman Sterling came on the sixth day.

Nobody knew he was coming. Kings didn't fly into other nations without telling Concordia, and he hadn't told Concordia, so he didn't fly. He took the overnight ferry from the Atlantic coast to the Huaxia ports and the morning train to Lingzhou like anybody else, in a gray raincoat with a canvas bag over his shoulder, walked from the station to East Market Road, and stopped at the bottom of the Ossuary steps at nine in the morning to look up at the bronze door.

Old Seven was in it.

The King of Force was big, broad and heavy and slow-moving in the way of men who've spent their lives trying not to break things by accident. Seventy, perhaps. Short gray hair, a boxer's nose. He looked up at the tall dead captain of the Eastern Line, two meters ten, spear grounded in a finger and a thumb.

"I'd like to come in," he said. "I'd like to talk to the boy. I'm not here as a King. I left that on the ferry."

Old Seven looked at him a long moment, and stood aside.

> **He says he knows,** said Seed. **He could tell from how the man stood at the bottom of the steps. A King stands as if the steps ought to come down to him. This one stood as if he was waiting to be asked up.**

---

They sat on the vault steps, because Yuanshu did his thinking there and Sterling said he didn't want a chair. Lingyue sat at the foot of the stairs with her sword across her knees and a candle on the floor beside her. The flame on her finger was the size of a fingertip now, and she held it without looking at it. Xiaoyu was at the long table pretending not to listen. Fat Tan made tea, and because there was a King in the building he brought out the good cups, the ones with the cranes on, and then stood about anxiously in case a King broke one.

"I've read your articles," said Sterling. "All twelve. The first eleven on the ferry. The twelfth about fifty times." He held the cup in both hands. They were enormous hands, scarred over the knuckles. "I voted no."

"I know. It's on the wall."

"I know it's on the wall. Your sister sent me a photograph." He didn't smile. "I want to tell you why. Properly. Not on the radio. On the radio it sounds like I'm being clever."

He drank some tea.

"I'm a Force King. You know what Force is, underneath the Law and the cultivation and the textbook words?" He put the cup down on the step, gently. "It's deciding for things. That's all. A wall's in the way, you decide it isn't. A man's coming at you, you decide he's going backwards. You don't ask the wall. You don't ask the man. That's the whole Law." He looked at his hands. "The first time I threw a man through a wall I was nineteen. He'd come into a shelter during a rift breach with a knife. I threw him through a wall to stop him, and I was sick for two days. Couldn't eat. Kept seeing his face going backwards."

He was quiet.

"The second time, I was sick for an afternoon."

Yuanshu said nothing.

"By the tenth time I didn't notice," said Roman Sterling. "By the hundredth I was good at it. I was fast. I could decide for a building in a breath. That's how you get to be a King: you get very good at deciding for things very quickly." He looked at the boy on the step. "And nobody ever stops you and says *you didn't ask*. Because you're a King, and it worked, and the building's down and everybody's alive."

He picked up the cup again.

"How long did it take you to write the statement for the stones?"

"Eleven minutes," said Yuanshu.

Sterling nodded slowly.

"And the one for the tree?"

"Two hours. And a battlement. And my wife. And my mother."

"Yes," said the King of Force. "That's why I voted no."

---

The candle beside Lingyue burned straight up in the still air.

"I tore it up," said Yuanshu.

"So I hear. Your sister." Sterling nearly smiled, and didn't. "That's good. That's once. It'll get easy again next time, and the time after. You'll be very good at this by the time you're my age." He turned the cup in his hands. "I didn't come to tell you not to do it. The stones are worse than you. I counted. Gao Min sent me her graph. I've got a granddaughter turning eighteen in four years." He looked across the hall at the long white wall. "I came to ask if I can stand there."

"Where?"

"Next to you, on the day. In the hall. When you read it out and say nobody said yes." He set the cup down. "I'd like to say no out loud, right after. Every time, every stone, every time you ever use that article, as long as I'm alive." He looked at his hands. "So it's never only your voice. So there's always somebody in the room saying: no, it isn't easy, you didn't ask, and I'm not letting you forget it."

From the foot of the stairs, without looking up from the candle, Lingyue said: "Sister Ning does that."

Sterling turned his head.

"The Quiet," said Lingyue. "One of them witnesses every time he touches the dead. Every single time. Somebody who'd rather he didn't." She held up her finger with the flame on it. "It's been the rule since the Ledger. You're asking to be that, for the twelfth."

"Then yes," said Roman Sterling. "I'm asking to be that."

Yuanshu looked at the big gray man on the step, with his boxer's nose and his raincoat and his scarred hands round one of Fat Tan's crane cups.

"Yes," he said. "Please."

---

> **There's a problem I haven't solved,** said Seed.

It was evening. Kwan was at the long table with the laptop and three diagrams; Gao Min with a ledger; Sterling, who hadn't bothered with a hotel, drawing something on the back of a train timetable with a pencil. The world had been asked for five days, down the slow net of the Lattice and over the radio and on paper ballots in schools and temples, and was still answering. Fifty-eight percent so far said *let the eleven go in*. Twenty-nine said *no awakenings this year, not until we know*. The rest said other things, which Kwan read out in the evenings, and Xiaoyu wrote the best of on the wall.

And under all of it was the article that needed no vote, because it wasn't anyone's to vote on: every child decides for themselves whether to touch it. Article Ten. Their window.

> **The graft. A graft is bone to bone, hands on both. I can unpress Wen Jia's strand the way I unpressed the hundred and thirty-eight, and carry her to the stone. But somebody has to put their hands on it and tie her into the knot at the bottom.**
>
> **And anything that puts its hands on a Stele gets read.**

"Callum Ward," said Kwan quietly.

> **Yes. If Yuanshu touches it, it reads him. And it reads me, as it did on the first morning. That time it went black and didn't know what to do with us.**
>
> **It's had two years since. It sent up what it saw, and something heard, and sent a scout, and rings, and a tree. I don't know what it'll do this time. I've run it. In some runs it goes black again. In some it opens him all the way, like Callum Ward, and sends him up.**

Lingyue put her candle out.

"No," she said.

> **I agree.**

"Then who?" said Sterling.

---

> **Old Seven.**

At the bronze door the tall dead soldier didn't move, but he'd turned his skull a little toward the table.

> **READ WHAT RIPENS. A MIND IS RIPE WHEN IT HAS GROWN ITS OWN SHAPE AND NOT YET SET.**
>
> **The dead have set. They aren't growing or ripening. They're embers. The knot was written for the living, for children, for the moment in between. I don't think a Stele can read the dead. I think it would look at Old Seven and see nothing it was made to see.**
>
> **And Yuanshu would be inside him, as he was on the cut, with Huo's hand. The stone wouldn't see him through the dead either. I think.**
>
> **I think. I don't know. I've run it nine hundred times. Old Seven isn't read in eight hundred and sixty of them. In the other forty I can't see what happens. I can't see past the touch.**

Old Seven left the doorway.

He crossed the marble as he always did, slowly, with his spear, between the boxes and the people, and stopped beside the long table. He didn't look at Kwan's diagrams or Gao Min's ledger or the King of Force with his pencil. He looked just above Yuanshu's eyes.

> **He says yes. But not because he was asked. He was going to stand beside the girl on the platform anyway, and her brother will be inside him, and he goes where the boy goes. So he might as well have his hands on something useful.**
>
> **He says a scout goes out ahead of the line. And if he's one of the forty, somebody should tell Xu Dehai he's coming, so the old man can keep him a place by stone No. 7.**

The table was silent.

Then Roman Sterling put down his pencil, looked up at the tall dead captain, and said slowly, in his heavy accented Huaxian, the thing he'd crossed an ocean on a ferry to say.

"No."

Old Seven turned his skull toward him.

"No," said the King of Force again. Not loud. Steady. Looking up. "I'm saying it so it's said. Not to stop you. So that when you put your hand on that stone, somebody in this room said no first."

Old Seven looked at him a long moment.

> **He says: good. He says you'll do.**

---

At two that morning he wrote the statement again.

It took four hours. He crossed out every line at least once. At three he went up to the battlement and sat in his old place with his back to the parapet for an hour and wrote nothing, then came down and wrote one more line and crossed it out. He read the red line on the wall aloud four times. He thought about Callum Ward, twenty-five, an F, with a sledgehammer, burning for nine seconds. About Wen Jia's blue jacket that she'd never worn. About a tall dead captain laying a sergeant's hand flat on a black stone and being one of the forty.

At six in the morning he had eleven lines again.

They were almost word for word the eleven he'd torn up. He sat at the long table and saw that, and didn't know whether it meant the first ones had been right, or whether he'd spent four hours walking to the same easy place by a longer road.

> **I think it means you're learning,** said Seed. **I don't think learning is the same as it getting easy. But I'm not sure, and I don't think you should be either.**
>
> **Sterling will say no, every time. That's what he's for.**

At the top of the sheet, above the eleven lines, before he gave it to Xiaoyu to copy for the wall, he wrote one more line, small. Not part of the statement. For himself.

*This took four hours. The last one took eleven minutes. Ask why every time.*

Nineteen days.
