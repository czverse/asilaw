## Chapter 248: The Hand

Port Halvard smelled of diesel and fish and the cold Atlantic, and at four in the morning on the day the hand came, fifteen thousand people stood round its harbor in the dark and not one of them was talking.

He came down out of the sky onto the end of the north pier with Lingyue and Qin Hao behind him, and the gold road running out over the water to the west, empty now, rolling itself up. The dead had come down it an hour earlier. Nobody could see them; that was the point. Nineteen thousand of the dead of Blue Star, banked, cold, dim, standing on the warehouse roofs and the cranes and the fish market and in the narrow streets behind the harbor among the living. The living didn't know they were there unless they looked hard at a shadow that was the wrong shape.

The Awakening Hall stood at the head of the harbor: square, granite, built by the Federation forty years ago to look like a bank, as the Ossuary had been. It had no roof. Sterling had taken it off two days before with Force, in one piece, and set it down in the car park, so there'd be nothing between the stone and whatever came down the thread.

And the line. Out of the doors, down the steps, along the quay, round the harbor, past the fish market and onto the coast road and out of sight: fifteen thousand people in coats in the dark, each with a hand on the shoulder of the one in front. Sterling had arranged it. A stone could only be touched by so many at once, he'd said, but a line could be as long as a line liked, and if everybody held the one in front and the one at the front held the stone, then everybody was holding the stone.

> **It works,** said Seed. **I didn't think it would. It's like the Lattice. Each holds the one in front, and the holding goes down the line into the stone.**
>
> **It's very bright.**

---

Sterling was at the stone. Not holding it: standing beside it in the roofless hall under the open sky, scarred hands in the pockets of his raincoat, feet apart on the granite floor. The black stone beside him had a crack round its base you could have put your arm into. Twenty-six centimeters out of the ground before the holders came. Zero since.

At the front of the line, bare hand flat on the stone, stood a woman of fifty in a docker's jacket. Short gray hair. A face like the voices on the radio from that city: flat, hard, used to shouting over engines.

She looked at him when he came in. She didn't take her hand off the stone.

"You're him. You talked to my son."

"Yes. This morning, and yesterday."

"What did he say?"

"He asked if you were still holding."

Her face didn't move, but her hand on the stone pressed a little flatter.

"Tell him yes, next time," she said. "Tell him I said: what do you think."

---

> **There,** said Seed.

It was 4:41.

He looked up. Everyone in the roofless hall looked up, then everyone on the quay, then the whole line round the harbor, fifteen thousand faces turning up into the dark.

At first there was nothing: the Atlantic sky, black, clear, full of stars. And the thread, which nobody could see except Seed and Kwan's laptop back in Lingzhou, rising out of the crown of the stone into the dark, taut and humming.

Then a star went out.

Then another. Not falling. Covered. In a line straight up from the hall, the stars over Port Halvard went out one after another as something came down in front of them along a thread nobody could see. You could only see it by what it hid.

It had fingers.

---

It was the size of the harbor.

When it came low enough for the quay lamps to light its underside, people screamed. Not many, and not for long, because each of them had a hand on their shoulder from behind and a hand on the shoulder in front, and nobody let go.

A hand. There was no other word for it, as there'd been no other word for the tree. Silver-gray, the color of walker-thread, the color of the rings on two people's fingers. Thread pressed on thread, thick as cables at the wrist where it slid down the single humming line out of the dark, and then opening below into a palm as wide as the harbor mouth. From the palm, fingers. Not five. Hundreds. Long and jointed, uncurling slowly like fern fronds in spring, reaching down over the hall and the quay and the warehouses and the fish market and the long line of people in coats.

And from every finger, finer, millions of threads, hanging like willow roots over a river.

Reaching for the bright.

---

> **Now.**

The dead struck.

He gave them the fire: down through the staff into the Death Law, all forty-eight point eight percent of it, and out through the bone of the staff and the bones of nineteen thousand dead like water through a riverbed. Pale fire, Lingyue's light wound with his Law. She laid her hand on the staff beside his, and it went pale as it had on the island and over Lingzhou and a hundred times between.

On the roofs and the cranes and the fish market, nineteen thousand rusted weapons went pale at once.

The hand couldn't see them. It was reaching for the bright, for fifteen thousand people in a line, and between the people and the fingers, on every roof and crane, stood the dead, invisible and ash-gray. As the fine threads came down past them toward the living, the dead cut them, one each. Spears, swords, shovels, lengths of anchor chain and one pick, burning pale, cutting thread.

The first ten thousand threads fell, coming apart, drifting down over the harbor like ash from a bonfire.

> **They count as kills,** said Seed. **Every finger the dead take apart is thread with a weave in it, like walkers. Level 69 to 70.**

The hand didn't stop.

It didn't flinch the way the branch had groaned and pulled back. It didn't feel the cuts. He understood that with cold certainty: it had no more feeling in its fingers than a glove has. The threads that were cut fell, and the ones beside them came on. Millions of them. And each of the dead could cut one.

And it was pulling.

---

He felt it in the stone before Seed spoke, and so did everyone: the whole line, fifteen thousand people, felt it at once through the shoulder of the person in front. A heave. Up. As if the black stone at the head of the harbor had been seized from above by a hand the size of the harbor and lifted.

The crack round its base opened. Granite ground on granite.

Callum Ward's mother set her feet.

Behind her, all the way round the harbor, fifteen thousand people set theirs. He heard it: a sound like a wave dragging back down a shingle beach.

The stone stopped. It didn't go down and didn't go up. It hung there in its crack between a hand the size of a harbor pulling up and fifteen thousand people with a little holding in each of them pulling down.

> **They're holding,** said Seed. **They can't hold long. The pull's ten times what it was last night. It's pulling with everything it's got, because it's here.**
>
> **Six minutes. Maybe seven.**

Roman Sterling took his hands out of his pockets.

---

He'd said on the vault steps in Lingzhou that Force was deciding for things. That you didn't ask the wall. That a King of Force was a man who'd got very good at deciding for things very quickly.

Now he stood in the roofless hall beside a black stone being pulled out of the ground by a hand from the deep water, with fifteen thousand people holding it behind him and a woman whose son was burning at the front of the line, and put one enormous scarred hand flat on top of the stone. Not to hold it. To decide.

"No," said Roman Sterling.

He said it to the stone, as he'd said it on the platform in Lingzhou after the statement: quietly, clearly, so that it was said.

"I decide you stay. Nobody asked you. I know. I'm saying it out loud. I'm deciding for you, and I don't like it, and I'm doing it."

The air in the hall went heavy. Heavier than it had ever gone in Lingzhou with forty-one children holding. Heavy as air goes when a King who's spent fifty years deciding for walls and buildings and men with knives decides, with everything he has, that one thing will not move.

The stone went down a centimeter.

> **He's holding it,** said Seed. **Not like the holders. They're pulling down. He's deciding it doesn't go up. It isn't the same thing.**
>
> **It'll cost him.**

"I know," said Sterling through his teeth. Blood was running from his nose. "Put it on the wall."

---

The hand let go of the stone.

All at once. The pull went out of the thread, the stone dropped its centimeter back into the crack, and the whole line staggered forward with the release and caught one another and held.

It hadn't given up. It had felt what was holding the stone down, and it was reaching for that instead.

The fingers came down. All of them: hundreds of vast jointed fingers the width of streets, out of the dark over the harbor toward the line, and from every finger the fine threads falling on the people like rain.

And the dead could cut one each.

He saw the first ones taken, down the quay where the line bent by the fish market: a man in a fisherman's sweater, a girl behind him, a woman behind her. The threads came down past the roofs, and the dead cut what they could, and it wasn't enough. The threads went into them, into hair and shoulders and chests, as the tip had gone into the people on the wall at Lingzhou.

Not tasting. Taking.

The man in the fisherman's sweater went bright. For a moment, the best thing he'd ever felt; Yuanshu saw it on his face. Then the threads went taut and the man went up, out of the line, out of his coat, which fell empty onto the quay. Up, burning, into the palm of the hand, as Callum Ward had gone up six weeks before.

The girl behind him had had her hand on his shoulder. She was holding his empty coat.

> **Forty,** said Seed. **Sixty. It's taking the holders. It's working down the line.**
