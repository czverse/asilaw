## Chapter 297: Old

Nobody had told him about the children.

He found out at a quarter to seven, standing on the steps of the Awakening Hall with the staff in one hand and a paper cup of soy milk going cold in the other, because Lingyue had bought two and drunk hers in three swallows and then started on his. The first of them came up East Ring Road in school uniforms with their bags bumping on their backs. Then more. A boy of about eight with half a steamed bun in his fist. Two sisters arguing about whose turn it was. A girl who could not have been older than six, towing her grandmother by the sleeve like a tugboat.

They went straight past him into the hall.

> **They started after Awakening Day,** said Seed. **While we were on Anthos. A nine-year-old on his way to school went in, put his hand on the stone and told her he wanted to drive trains. She said okay. The next morning he came back and said he'd changed his mind and wanted to be a vet. She said that was okay too.**
>
> **He told his class. His class told the school.**
>
> **Sixty-one mornings so far.**

"Every morning?"

> **Every morning. Gao Min has a chart. The most popular answer is "I don't know". The second is "a footballer".**

Lingyue finished his soy milk and handed him the empty cup. "Put that in a bin," she said, and went in after the children.

---

Inside, they queued up the aisle the way they would have queued for a school photo: shoving a bit, whispering, one boy doing a handstand against the end of a tier until somebody's mother took him by the collar. They went past the dead nun cross-legged at the foot of the platform, past the lamp burning in her lap, and up the three steps. One at a time they put their hands on the black stone with the blue jacket draped over its shoulder.

The letters came quick and slanted, in the handwriting of a girl who had been seventeen the year the Wastes were evacuated.

**[What do you want to become?]**

*A dancer.* **[Okay.]**

*A doctor like my aunt.* **[Okay.]**

*I don't know.* **[Best answer. Come back tomorrow.]**

*A dragon.* **[I'll see what I can do.]**

The boy who wanted to be a dragon walked back down the aisle looking deeply satisfied.

---

At a quarter to eight the Number Nine pulled in at the stop outside.

He heard it before he saw it: the long hiss of old brakes, then nothing. No doors. The bus just sat there by the curb with the engine running, blue and white, with the dent in its back panel where a clawed thing had hit it during the Black Tide and nobody had ever bothered to fix it.

Behind the wheel, Wen Hao sat with both hands on it and looked at the hall.

Yuanshu knew what he was doing, because everyone on East Ring Road knew. Twelve times a day, every day for more than a year, the Number Nine had stopped outside the Awakening Hall and its driver had counted to ten before he opened the doors. Passengers had stopped complaining about it in the first week. A few of them counted along now.

This time, when he got to ten, he opened the doors, got out, and left the engine running, which was a fineable offense, and came up the steps.

"She told me last night," he said. He stopped a step below Yuanshu, a stocky man of fifty-one with gray coming in at the temples and the steadiest hands in the city. "Through your sister. On my last run. She said, tomorrow he's getting asked, and I want you there." He looked at the open doors of his bus. Two passengers were peering out. "Twenty-six years on this route. Never once late."

"You're going to be late."

"I'm going to be very late," said Wen Hao. "She says that's fine. She's not the one who'll be filling in the form."

---

Then everyone came, the way everyone always came.

Fat Tan pushed his mother's wheelchair up the ramp at the side, swearing at the ramp. Xiaoyu had the marker behind her ear. Qin Hao arrived with his notebook shut, which was unusual enough that Su Mingli looked at it twice. Dabao arrived with a bag of fried dough sticks and tried to hand them out to the children, and Gao Min confiscated the bag on the grounds that it was a hall. Bai Lu. Tam and Seri, barefoot on a cold stone floor because nobody had managed to make them stop. Old Seven came in through the side door and stayed there, one-armed, leaning his spear against the frame.

Nobody had sent for any of them.

"Who told you?" Yuanshu asked Dabao.

"The kids," said Dabao, through a fried dough stick he had kept back. "Bro, the kids tell everybody everything. That's the whole point of kids."

The last child in the queue was the six-year-old with the grandmother. She stood on her toes to reach the stone and announced, loudly, that she wanted to be a bigger girl.

**[That one's easy,]** the stone wrote. **[Eat your breakfast.]**

She ran back down the aisle shouting it. Her grandmother caught her at the door. Then the hall was empty except for the people who had come to watch him, and they were all very carefully not looking at him.

> ***...okay,*** said Wen Jia. ***...your turn.***

---

He went up the steps.

The last time he had touched this stone on purpose, he had been eighteen and there had been four thousand people watching and a dozen camera drones overhead, and it had gone black. Not just this one. All eleven, in eleven cities, the same second. It had tried to read him, found nothing on the tree inside it that matched, and turned the lights out on half the world. Then it had cracked down the middle.

After that, everyone had been happy to tell him what he was. The Bureau. The Institute. The newspapers. The man on the Number Nine who had once leaned over and told him, kindly, that he should think about moving to the countryside. *Calamity Necromancer. Unique. Danger. Weapon. The boy with the dead.* Lately, more politely, *the one who asks.* He had collected names the way a coat collects dog hair.

Nobody had ever asked him.

The stone was warm under his palm. Its runes pointed downward now and had for a year. At its foot, the dead nun sat with her dry hand flat on the rock and the lamp in her lap, and didn't look up, because she hadn't looked up in twelve hundred years.

> **It's listening,** said Seed. **The Collector. Very close now. It isn't doing anything. It's just listening.**

**[What do you want to become?]**

---

The honest answer was that he didn't know, and he stood there long enough for that to become obvious to everyone in the room.

Outside, the Number Nine's engine idled. Somebody's phone buzzed and was silenced. Dabao, in the front tier, very slowly unwrapped another dough stick and then thought better of it.

He tried *King* first, because it was the obvious one. It was what the stone would have printed if it could have read him. It was what Seed's charts were for. A planet's worth of power. The Death Law past the wall, a Fragment, the kind of number that ended arguments. He held the idea up and looked at it, the way you look at a coin you've found on the pavement, and found that he didn't want it. He had nothing against it. It was simply a number, and numbers had never been what he was after. Seed had worked that out on the ship home, which was humbling.

He tried *the one at the door*, and that was no good either, because he already was that. He had been that since three in the morning in the War Memorial Cemetery, holding a training spear he had no permit for. You didn't ask a stone for the thing you already had. Maelis hadn't asked to be twenty-four.

Maelis had asked to be thirteen.

He thought about that for a moment. A girl who had spent twelve years stuck at twelve and wanted, more than anything in the Lamplit Worlds, one ordinary birthday. He thought about Kiyo, who had sat on a beach for twelve hundred years and then got into the boat. About Yaw at the bottom of the sea off Kesse, who had asked nine hundred thousand frightened lights what they wanted and then asked for nothing himself except to lie in the sand and listen to the fishing boats go over.

He thought about his mother.

She was at the foot of the platform with her hands folded on the blanket, looking at the spot just above his eyes where Seed was. Not at him. He knew why. She had known him for twenty-one years, and she knew that if she looked him in the face right now he would not be able to say anything at all.

And Xiaodeng was asleep in the sling against Lingyue's chest, with her mouth open and one fist jammed against her mother's collarbone. Four and a half months old. In a year she would say something. In ten years she would say something else, probably rude. In thirty years she would say something he couldn't begin to imagine, in a room he'd never been in, and he wouldn't understand it, and he'd have to ask her what she meant.

He wanted to be in that room.

It was so ordinary that he nearly laughed. He had walked past it every single day for three years, like his own front door.

"Old," said Zhou Yuanshu.

---

Someone in the tiers let out a breath.

"I want to get old," he said to the stone. He kept his eyes on it. It was easier than the room. "Here. In Lingzhou. With her." He didn't say who. Everybody knew who. "I want to be as old as her grandfather and sit on the north wall in the mornings. I want my daughter to ask me what I want and I want to have no idea. I want her to have to ask twice."

He was surprised how steady his voice was.

"And I want to stand at the door for a long time. Not because I've got to. Because I'm good at it and I like it. Fifty years. Sixty. Until I'm the slowest one there and the young ones roll their eyes. And then one day somebody's going to ask me if I want to go through, and I'm going to say, not yet, ask me tomorrow." He stopped. "And then one day I'll say yes. When I'm old. And it'll be all right."

He breathed out.

"That's it," he said. "Old. I didn't know until you asked."

---

The stone took its time.

He could feel her in it. He had carried her for three years without knowing, one small yes among four thousand at the bottom of Seed, a girl who had said *only if it's something good* to his mother in a cold basement. She had been seventeen when they put her in a sack during the evacuation of the Wastes. She had never been read. She had never been eighteen. She had gone into this rock so that nobody else in Lingzhou would be told what they were.

> ***...me too,*** said Wen Jia.

It was a very small voice.

> ***...that's what I wanted. I never said. Nobody asked me.***
>
> ***...I wanted to be old and horrible and tell my brother he drives like a grandma. I wanted to be fifty-one with him. I wanted to see what he'd look like going gray.***
>
> ***...he looks all right, actually. He's at the back. He's crying. Don't tell him I said he looks all right.***

At the back of the hall, by the doors, Wen Hao had put both hands over his face. One of his passengers had come in off the bus and was patting him on the shoulder.

**[Then get old.]**

She wrote it slowly this time, not in the quick slant she used for the children. Each stroke separate, the way you write something you mean to read again in fifty years.

**[That one takes the longest.]**

**[It's the only one I can't open for you. Nobody can. You have to do it yourself. Every day. On purpose.]**

**[I'll ask you every year how it's going.]**

**[Chosen.]**

And under that, smaller, in the same words she had once written for his sister:

**[Only if it's something good.]**

**[It is.]**

---

He took his hand off the stone. The letters stayed in the air, a line below Xiaoyu's three.

When he turned round, his mother was looking straight at him.

It took him a second to place the expression, because he had not seen it on her in three years. Not in the back room of the Ossuary, with the names. Not ever since the morning the Stele went black. She was not frightened of him. She was not frightened *for* him. She was just looking at her son.

"Good," said Zhou Wanqing. "About time somebody in this family wanted something sensible."

Lingyue came up the steps before anyone else could. She handed him the baby without asking, the way she handed him everything, put her free hand flat on the stone beside the warm place where his had been, and glared at the letters as if they had insulted her.

**[What do you want to become?]**

"Old with him," said Shen Lingyue. "Obviously."

**[Saw that coming,]** wrote Wen Jia. **[Chosen.]**

Lingyue took the baby back.

---

Old Seven didn't come up. He stayed in the side door, which he said through Seed was where captains stood, and that the stone was for the living, and that he had been asked once already, by the only person whose asking he needed, in a cemetery at three in the morning.

> **He says: old. He never got there. Most of the men on the eastern wall didn't.**
>
> **He says it's what they were holding the wall for. None of them ever said so. Soldiers don't.**
>
> **He says he'll stand at the door with you. He's been dead thirty-odd years. He can manage fifty more.**

Then, after a pause, Seed added a line of its own.

> **He also says your mother is right and it's about time.**

---

Xiaoyu was sitting on the bottom step of the platform, with the marker uncapped, when the Collector spoke.

Yuanshu didn't hear it. She did. He saw her head come up and her face go still. A voice was reaching her from very far off. She held up one finger at him: *wait*.

"It heard," she said. "All of it." She listened. "It says: *Old.*" She listened again, longer. "It says it's old and it never got to choose. It didn't know you could."

"Tell it to get in the queue," said Lingyue. "There's a whole chart."

Xiaoyu ignored her. Her eyes had gone wide.

"It says it told you on the mountain you might be old before it got here," she said slowly. "It says it was wrong. It's going to get here first. It's sorry about that. It would have liked to see what old looks like on one of us." She swallowed. "It says the want inside it has started pulling. Ever since the picker stopped. Like it knows it's nearly home."

The hall had gone quiet again.

"It says it'll be here tomorrow," said Xiaoyu. "And it wants to know if I'll ask you. The question. The one it gave me a year ago."

She looked up at her brother. He was standing on the platform with his wife on one side and his daughter between them, and his palm still warm from the rock.

"Yes," she said, to the empty air. "I'll ask him."

Then she got up, crossed to the long white wall by the side door, where nothing had been written yet, and stepped past Old Seven, who moved his spear for her. She wrote at the very top, in her square black capitals:

*ZHOU YUANSHU. ASKED. OLD.*

And underneath, smaller:

*THE COLLECTOR. TOMORROW. FOR REAL THIS TIME.*

Outside, someone finally leaned on the horn of the Number Nine.
