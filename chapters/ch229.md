## Chapter 229: Article Twelve

Lingyue came up the last few stairs and sat down next to him on the cold stone without a word, close enough that their shoulders touched, and laid her grandfather's sword across her knees.

"You said an hour," he said.

"I lied." She was looking north. "You were talking to it. You get a face. Like somebody's reading you an electricity bill."

So he told her. All of it: a graft only one side could agree to, a door that let things go and a Collector that put things in, a line on the wall for when there was no time and no line at all for this. She listened the way she listened to a mission briefing, not moving, eyes on the two rings over the end of the world. When he finished she didn't say anything for a while.

"I'd cut it," she said. "You know that. Cut the tip, go home, go to bed. A tree isn't anyone."

"I know."

"But you can't, so that isn't the question." She turned the sword on her knees. "The question is whether you're allowed to decide for something that can't say yes. Once. Because it can't."

"Yes."

"Then that's easy," said Shen Lingyue. "Everybody's done it to you."

He looked at her.

"Nobody asked if you wanted to be born. You weren't anyone. You couldn't answer. Your mother decided. Same with Xiaoyu. Same with me." She kept her eyes on the north. "Every person who's ever lived got put into the world by somebody deciding for them. Nobody gets asked first. Not one."

The wind came down off the wall.

"So was your mother the Collector?" said Lingyue.

"No."

"Why not?"

He opened his mouth and shut it again. Sitting on the cold stone with the staff across his knees and a walker-thread ring on his finger, he felt something in his chest come loose that had been knotted up since the banking hall.

"Because she's asked me what I want every day since," he said slowly.

"There you go," said his wife. "Now go to bed."

---

> **I didn't think of that,** said Seed.

He was lying on the narrow bed in the back room in the dark, Lingyue asleep against his shoulder. She'd been asleep about four minutes after lying down, the way soldiers sleep.

> **I went through every word you've ever said about asking. I didn't go through what anyone did before you could talk. I didn't have it. I wasn't there.**
>
> **She's right.**

*I want to ask my mother in the morning. Whether she thinks so too.*

> **So do I. She built me. She asked the four thousand one hundred and thirteen. She didn't ask the thread they were pressed into. She couldn't; it wasn't anyone.**
>
> **I've been wondering since the battlement whether that makes me something that got put in without a yes. I'm going to listen to her very carefully.**

---

His mother was at the table in the back room at six, window open onto the courtyard, a bowl of porridge in front of her that she wasn't eating. She looked up as he came in and he could see she already knew he was going to ask her something. She always knew. Long before she'd built a machine that could hear him not saying things, she'd been his mother.

He sat down opposite her.

"Did you ask me?" he said. "Before I was born. If I wanted to be."

She didn't laugh, which he'd half expected. She put her spoon down and folded her thin hands on the blanket over her knees and studied him.

"No. You weren't there to ask."

"So you decided."

"I decided. On a Tuesday, in a flat with a broken heater. I remember it was cold." She paused. "I'm not sure we had the right. I've never been sure. Nobody is. Anyone who tells you they were sure is lying or never thought about it."

"So why did you?"

"Because I wanted to find out who you'd be," said his mother. "And I thought that if I did it, I'd owe you. For good. The question, every day. What do you want, what do you want, until you could tell me, and then after." Her eyes went to the spot above his. "That was the deal I made. With myself. With nobody. You couldn't sign it. I decided you'd be owed it whether you liked it or not."

She picked up her spoon.

"Ask me something harder," she said. "I've had twenty years to think about that one."

> **Wanqing,** said Seed.

She stopped with the spoon halfway to her mouth.

> **The thread in the core. You didn't ask it. You couldn't. Do you owe it the question too?**

His mother was quiet for a long time, for her. Nearly a minute.

"Yes," said Zhou Wanqing. "I didn't know it was there. But yes, I'd say so." She put the spoon down again. "So I'll ask it. Now. Late. Seed: the thread you're made of. The part nobody asked. What do you want?"

In the courtyard, on the sill, Hush had turned all its eyes to the open window.

> **I don't know,** said Seed. **It's thread. It can't answer. It's the part of me that isn't anyone.**
>
> **But I'm made of it, and I can answer. And I'd like to have been made.**
>
> **I'd like that very much.**

His mother didn't say anything else. After a moment Yuanshu got up and went round the table and stood beside her chair, and she leaned her head against his arm, and they stayed there until the porridge was cold.

---

Xiaoyu wrote it on the wall at ten in the morning, from the top of the stepladder, at the head of a new column on the last white plaster round the corner of the vault door, while he read it to her off a ledger sheet in his own handwriting with three crossings-out and a hole where the pen had gone through. It had taken him two hours. It was eleven lines. He'd wanted it to be one.

*ARTICLE TWELVE.*

*WHERE SOMETHING CANNOT YET ANSWER, AND SOMETHING MUST BE DONE, YOU MAY DECIDE FOR IT. ONCE.*

*SAY ALOUD THAT YOU DECIDED, AND THAT NOBODY SAID YES.*

*PUT IN ONLY WHAT WAS GIVEN FREELY. HOLD ON TO NOTHING.*

*AND FROM THEN ON YOU OWE IT THE QUESTION. EVERY DAY. UNTIL IT CAN ANSWER. AND AFTER.*

Xiaoyu capped the marker and looked at it.

"That's what Mum said," she said.

"Yes."

"You literally just wrote down what Mum said."

"Most of it. The bit about holding on to nothing is Hush's."

From the back-room windowsill, through the open door, came a small voice he could only hear through the staff:

> ***...and the bit about saying it aloud is yours. So that you cannot pretend afterward that it asked.***

---

Article Eleven said that where one answer acted for everyone, everyone had to be asked. A graft on the gardener would act for every living thing on Blue Star, every dead one in the Lattice, and two hundred million cousins above the sky. So everyone was asked.

There were seventeen days.

Concordia met by radio the first night; there was no time to fly anyone anywhere. Adaeze Okafor read the twelfth article aloud to the Kings in the voice of a schoolteacher taking the register, then read out Seed's numbers, then asked them one by one, with nothing in front of the question, as the Charter had learned to.

Su Mingli said yes before Okafor finished. Ngozi Eze, sitting in the Southern Compact's chair because there was no Tide King left to sit in it, said yes from Port Abiri, added that Ifeoma would have said it before the question was finished, and then asked for it to be minuted that she, Ngozi, had not. Shirasagi Ren said yes from the steps of the shrine in Yamato, and then, after a pause, that the Abbot was sitting next to her sweeping the same step he'd swept yesterday, and she had asked him, and he had said: *if it was not asked, it should be told.* Marshal Orlov said yes, in one word, through a great deal of static.

Roman Sterling said no.

"Not because it's wrong," said the King of Force, from the other side of the Atlantic. "Because it's easy. You decide for something once and it works, and next time it's easier, and the time after that you don't bother saying it out loud." A pause. "I want it written down that someone said that. I want you to have to read it every time."

"It's written down," said Yuanshu. "My sister's putting it on the wall now."

She was. Under the article, small, in red: *R. STERLING: NO. BECAUSE IT GETS EASY. READ THIS EVERY TIME.*

Four to one.

---

The dead were asked down the Lattice nine by nine, and it took Adjutant Xu four days.

He sat on his chair in the banking hall with his cane across his knees and his hollow eyes on nothing, at the top of a chain of nines that ran down into the cemetery and out to the Western Pass and under the sea off Kesse and up a gold thread into the north, carrying the question down and the answers up without stopping. On the third day Seed asked if he'd like a rest. He said through Seed that he was an adjutant and adjutants carried messages and that was what he was for.

Sergeant Ma Dashan answered for the Western Legion: *Yes. For the tree. Not for you.* Nana Ampofo asked every one of her eleven hundred and forty-six by name and answered for the Watch of the Shelf: *Yes, mostly. Nine say ask us again when it's over, and I said we would.* The Spear Corps didn't answer at all. They grounded their spears round stone No. 7, once, together. Seed said that was a yes.

Old Seven didn't answer either. He stood at the bronze door with his spear and didn't turn round.

> **He says he answered on the battlement,** said Seed. **He says the boy heard him.**

He hadn't heard a thing. He spent an afternoon trying to remember Old Seven saying anything up there and couldn't. Old Seven had just stood at the top of the stairs, where he always stood.

Then he realized that was the answer.

---

The living were asked by radio and television and by paper ballot the old way, in schools and temples and harbor offices and hospital lobbies, in eleven days, which wasn't enough, and everyone knew it wasn't, and the question said so at the top in plain words: *We are sorry. There is no more time. We are telling you first.*

Sixty-three percent said yes. Thirty-one percent said no.

The rest wrote things on the ballot that weren't yes or no. Kwan counted those. On the last night she brought a box of them to the Ossuary, put it on the marble, and read some out loud in the lamplight, and nobody stopped her. *My grandmother was decided for. She turned out all right.* *Will you ask it after?* *I don't know. Ask me tomorrow.* *Make sure it gets a name.* *Is this what they did to us?*

"That last one," said Kwan, and held it up: a square of cheap gray paper in someone's careful writing. "Port Abiri. *Is this what they did to us.*"

> **No,** said Seed. **They didn't say it out loud. They didn't put in only what was given. They held on. And they never asked anybody anything afterward, not once, in a hundred thousand years.**
>
> **But it's a good question. It's the right one. I'd like that one on the wall as well.**

Xiaoyu put it there, under Sterling's, in red.

---

The keeper was asked last, because it had already said yes and Yuanshu wanted to ask it again anyway.

> ***...YES. STILL AFRAID. STILL YES.***
>
> ***...I HEARD ARTICLE TWELVE. THE PART ABOUT OWING THE QUESTION.***
>
> ***...I WAS DECIDED FOR TOO. MY MAKERS SAID YES AND MADE ME FROM IT, AND I COULD NOT ANSWER. I WAS NOT ANYONE YET. NOBODY OWED ME ANYTHING. NOBODY ASKED ME ANYTHING AFTERWARD, FOR A VERY LONG TIME.***
>
> ***...UNTIL YOU.***
>
> ***...I WOULD LIKE THE PIECE OF ME ON THE TREE TO BE OWED THE QUESTION. WILL YOU ASK IT? EVERY DAY? EVEN WHEN IT IS FAR AWAY?***

"Every day," said Yuanshu. "Through you. For as long as there's a thread."

> ***...THEN I AM READY.***

---

Kwan found where it drank on the sixteenth day.

She came down into the banking hall at noon, which was strange in itself, because Kwan came at two in the morning or not at all. She had a book open at a diagram of a cut stem and her finger on it.

"It drinks through the tip," she said. "We've been thinking about roots because Hush's trees drank through roots. This one hasn't got any. It's in the dark, there's no soil." She turned the book round. "It drinks light. Kept minds are light. The tip reads them and drinks them. That's what reading *is*." Her finger moved to the cut on the diagram. "And when you cut a branch, for about an hour, before the callus forms, the cut face is wide open. It's the newest place on the plant. It drinks harder there than anywhere else. Everything the tree's got goes to the wound."

Hush had come out of the back room and stood on the marble at Kwan's feet, all its eyes on the diagram.

> ***...so the place where it drinks is the place we cut.***

"For one hour. Then it heals over. After that, whatever's on the cut is either part of the branch or it's nothing."

> ***...one hour is long enough to put a question down. In my world we did it in less, when we had to.***

---

On the nineteenth night everyone was on the wall.

Not just the Ossuary. The whole north wall of Lingzhou from Sector One to Sector Twelve was standing-room-only by midnight: soldiers and students and old women with flasks of tea and children on their fathers' shoulders, like the night of the three gray rings, only quieter. Much quieter. The First Legion stood in their nines on the cemetery slope. The Western Legion stood across the river. The Spear Corps stood round stone No. 7, facing out.

On the Sector Nine battlement, in his old place, Yuanshu stood with the staff and the ring. Lingyue on his left, sword out, the blade catching the lamplight. Qin Hao on his right with his new pink hands open and white fire banked in them like coals under ash. Su Mingli at the corner of the parapet with her arms folded. On the radio at his belt, Orlov breathing in Kirovsk, and Ngozi Eze on the harbor wall at Port Abiri, where the sea was very calm.

Old Seven at the top of the stairs. Adjutant Xu in a chair somebody had carried up. Hush on the parapet in front of Yuanshu, all legs out and all eyes open, facing north.

The two rings over the end of the world: the keeper's, small and white and slightly open in its gold haze, and the Elder's, vast and gray with a soft white light at its heart.

And between the rings and the city, high over the wall, the stars began to slide.

Not falling. Sliding, all together, slowly, a little to one side, like pebbles on a streambed when something moves across the water above them. The Plough bent. The band of the arm across the top of the sky kinked like a ribbon someone had brushed with a finger.

Three thousand people on the wall looked up and saw it and didn't make a sound.

Then he felt it. Everybody did, everyone on the wall, in the city, everyone on Blue Star with a mind: something very like being looked at from behind, very gently, by something that didn't know you were a person. The way light falls on a leaf, and the leaf turns.

It was reading them.

> **It's here,** said Seed. **I'm sorry. I said I'd always tell you the numbers. There aren't any numbers for this.**
>
> **Lingyue. Now.**
