## Chapter 285: Leaving

The hardest one to tell was the stone.

He went on the last night, alone except for Old Seven, who stood one-armed at the side door of the Awakening Hall, facing out. The hall was dark; even the holders had gone home. The three lines of light hung over the platform as they had for ten months. The warm black stone stood under them with its runes pointing down and a blue jacket over its shoulder, and before it on the platform, cross-legged, one dry hand flat on the stone and a lamp in her lap, sat the body of a nun of twelve hundred years. On the shore.

He sat down beside her.

*Wen Jia,* he thought.

> ***...I know,*** said the girl in the stone. ***...the whole net knows. Huo told everybody. You're going.***

*Yes.*

> ***...up. In the big ship. With the fisherman. To the place with four thousand seats.***

*Yes.*

> ***...good.***

He hadn't expected that. He sat on the platform in the dark and said nothing.

> ***...I mean it. Good. Someone should go and ask them. They've got stones there too. Thousands. Nobody in any of them. Just marking, the way mine did before.***
>
> ***...and they've got a girl there who wants to sit in one. The captain's sister, the twelve-year-old who's twenty-three. She asked you. You said wait a year. It's nearly a year.***

*I know.*

> ***...I'll be here,*** said Wen Jia. ***...I'm not going anywhere; I'm in a rock. I'll ask the kids while you're gone. The bus driver will count to ten. Your sister will ask the old thing in the dark what it wants.***
>
> ***...and when you come back, I'll ask you what you want to become.***
>
> ***...you've never been asked. Did you know that? The stone couldn't name you. It went black. Nobody ever asked you.***
>
> ***...I'm going to, when you get back. I'm telling you first.***

---

His mother didn't come to the north wall to see them go. She said so at breakfast on the last morning, in the back room, over a bowl of rice porridge going cold, with the window open onto the courtyard where Ninth stood by the dry fountain looking at the sky and Hush sat on the sill in the sun.

"I watched you go up a stalk," said Zhou Wanqing. "And into a Hunger. And onto an island that got smaller every night. And up a thread into the deep water without a body. From this chair." She folded her thin hands on the blanket. "I'm not watching from a wall. I'm going to sit here and read the names and wait for a mark."

"I'll send one every day."

"You'll forget."

"Seed won't."

> **I won't,** said Seed. **I'll send one every morning, after I ask Xiaodeng what she wants and after he tells me who the first one was.**
>
> **I'm telling you first, Wanqing, so you can be cross with me if I don't.**

His mother looked a long time at the place above her son's eyes: at the thing she'd built in a basement nine years ago out of a harvest crystal and four thousand people's yeses, with a cold.

"You were always going to go farther than me," she said. Not to him; to Seed. "I knew it in the basement. I didn't know it would be this far."

> **Nor did I. I'd like to come back. I'd like you to know that I want that. It's one of my wants. I've got three now. I counted.**

"What are the other two?" said his mother.

> **To ask Xiaodeng every morning until she can answer. And to know who the first one was.**

---

Xiaoyu wasn't coming.

She said so as she said everything, fast, at the long table, before anyone could argue. Somebody had to ask the Collector what it wanted every night at one in the Awakening Hall, and that was her, and the Collector had said *tomorrow*, and she wasn't going to be the one who broke a promise to the oldest thing in the dark. Somebody had to write the names. Somebody had to read the marks that came down Wen Jia's thread from a ship rowing across the Star Sea and put them on the wall so they were true.

"And somebody has to write the marks you'll need," she said. "Up there. In the Assembly. Whatever you have to tell them, it has to be true. Mine are." She looked at him across the table. "So you send me a mark saying what you need to say, and I write it here, and Wen Jia sends it to the fisherman, and he holds it up."

"That's the plan?"

"That's *my* plan," said his sister. "You haven't got one. You've got a step."

She uncapped the marker, climbed the stepladder, and wrote at the top of a new column on the last of the white plaster round the corner of the vault door:

*GONE UP TO ANTHOS. TO THE ASSEMBLY. TO ASK.*

*ZHOU YUANSHU. SHEN LINGYUE. ZHOU XIAODENG (CAN'T SAY YES YET). OLD SEVEN (ONE ARM). THE THIRD BATTALION (ASH). BAI LU (TO TELL A PARAGON HE'S AN IDIOT). TAM. SERI (TO THROW STONES AT CAPTAINS).*

*ICHIRO AND KIYO, IN THE LENS. A LAMP.*

*COMING HOME.*

And under it, smaller:

*THAT'S NOT A QUESTION.*

---

The Third Battalion went up first.

At dawn, from stone No. 7, on a road of gold the keeper laid down out of the north to the eastern field of the War Memorial Cemetery: sixty shapes of ash in a line, each holding the one in front, because Huo had seen the Candle's children do it and said it was a good way to march when you didn't know where you were going. Up the road toward a vast cathedral in the north that was going home rowed.

They didn't need bodies. Seed said the flagship had halls bigger than the Ossuary, empty, where the Paragons of the House of Severin had once held receptions, and Severin had asked whether the Third Battalion would like one, and Huo had said *yes, sir* to a man who wasn't his captain, and then *sorry, sir*, and Old Seven had said *he'll do*.

Old Seven went last.

He stood at the foot of the gold road in the eastern field, one-armed, spear grounded in his one hand, beside stone No. 7. Beside a small mound of new turf with nineteen dumplings on it in a paper boat, gone cold, which the dogs hadn't touched. Seed said the dogs had been told who they were for.

He looked at the stone a long time.

> **He says he came out of there three years ago, at three in the morning, when a boy with a training spear and no permit asked if he wanted to get up.**
>
> **He said yes. He's still saying it.**

He turned and walked up the gold road after his battalion, slowly, as he walked everywhere. Not fast. Not stopping.

---

They left from the north wall at noon.

Everyone was there; of course they were. Half of Lingzhou, the Candle's children, the night watch with their helmets in their hands, the dead along the battlements in their rows of nine. Gao Min with her ledger. Fat Tan with a thermos and an expression that dared anyone to comment on it. Dabao, enormous, crying openly into his collar, with his mother's dumpling cart at the foot of the Sector Nine stairs wearing a new roof of green canvas, which Fat Tan had put up overnight without asking. Su Mingli at the corner of the battlement with her arms folded.

Qin Hao was staying. Somebody had to burn what binds if anything came down out of the sky while the boy was away, he said, and he had a notebook to finish. He stood at the parapet and didn't say goodbye. He said: "Write everything down. In order. I want to check it."

The keeper laid the road: up from the north wall in a long slant of gold toward the great cathedral in the north that had been the flagship of the House of Severin and was now, Iselde said, the first ship of the Lamplit anyone had ever rowed home by choice.

> ***...GO CAREFULLY.***
>
> ***...COME BACK.***
>
> ***...I WILL ASK YOU WHAT YOU WANT WHEN YOU DO.***
>
> ***...I HAVE SAID THAT BEFORE. I AM SAYING IT AGAIN. THAT IS THE RULE.***

Lingyue went up first, Xiaodeng asleep against her chest in the sling, her grandfather's sword on her back, a lamp in her free hand. She didn't look back. She stopped a little way up the road and turned and looked down at the north wall, at the city, at three thousand upturned faces, and lifted the lamp once, as the women on the shore at Shiomi lifted theirs when a boat went out.

Then Bai Lu with her bag. Then Tam and Seri, holding hands as the Candle's children held hands, barefoot still.

He stood at the parapet in his old place with the staff in his hand. Beside him at the parapet, his sister, with the marker.

"Go on," said Xiaoyu. "You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The step face. The six-hours face." She didn't look at him; she was looking north. "You already decided. You decided at stone No. 7. You asked her and she said obviously. You don't get to sit on a step about it twice."

He looked at her. Seventeen, nearly eighteen. Scribe. Not marked. Square capitals on every wall of a bank on East Market Road, and on a parapet, and on the floor of a platform, and on the back of a statistician's hand. True.

"Ask the Collector for me," he said. "Every night."

"I always do."

"Tell it I'll answer when it gets here. Whether I'll go to the house."

His sister turned her head and looked at him.

"No," said Zhou Xiaoyu. "You tell it. When you get home. I'll ask it if it can wait." She uncapped the marker. "Go away. I've got to write down that you went."

He went.

---

He didn't look back either, not until he was halfway up the road with the city small below him and the cathedral in the north filling the sky ahead, lit from end to end and burning low now, not the old way: the color of the Low Fire, the color the cousins burned. Two hundred and forty thousand people in its walls who'd chosen to be there, rowing.

Then he looked back.

The north wall of Lingzhou, small and far below. Three thousand people. The dead in their rows. And on the parapet of the Sector Nine battlement, in his old place, very small, a girl in a school jacket bent over the stone, writing.

> **I can read it from here,** said Seed. **Through you. Her writing's true from anywhere.**
>
> **She's written: HE WENT.**
>
> **And under it: ASK HIM WHAT HE WANTS WHEN HE COMES BACK. HE'LL KNOW BY THEN.**
