## Chapter 242: The Other End

Dabao's mother brought the dumpling cart to the Ossuary at eight. She wouldn't take any money and wouldn't let anybody else push it, and she parked it on the pavement at the foot of the steps in the summer evening with steam rolling off it into the street, and shouted at Dabao to fetch more flour.

Dabao fetched more flour. He'd come straight off the north wall at the end of his shift, A-rank Shield Guardian, Unbroken Wall, enormous in his gear, and the first thing he'd done on coming through the bronze door was pick Xiaoyu up off the floor, hold her over his head like a trophy, and shout *SCRIBE* at the ceiling of the banking hall until Fat Tan told him the pigeons were complaining.

"Bro," he said to Yuanshu afterward on the steps, a paper boat of dumplings in each fist. "Bro. Your sister told the rock what to call her. On television. My mother cried into the dough. There's going to be salt in all of these." He ate one. "There is. It's good salt."

The street was full. Not as East Ring Road had been full, with a city waiting outside a hospital; full as a street gets on a summer evening when something good has happened. People sitting on steps, children running, the Mo twins arguing with a fruit seller, a queue for the dumpling cart down to the corner. Shi Lei and his father at the end of it in their work clothes, the boy holding a real spear now, plain steel, issued that afternoon, which he kept lifting to look at and lowering again as if somebody might take it back.

Lingyue sat on the top step beside Yuanshu with her grandfather's sword across her knees and a pale steady flame the size of an apricot in her open palm. She hadn't seemed to notice it since the hall.

"You're bigger," he said.

She looked down.

"Huh," said Shen Lingyue. "So I am." She closed her hand on it and opened it again, and it was still there. "It came back when your mother stood up. I felt it. I didn't do anything." She studied it. "I think it was waiting for something worth being bright about."

---

Inside, Xiaoyu was at the wall.

She'd been up the stepladder with the marker since they got home, writing the day. Not the names: there were tens of thousands, eleven halls' worth, and Gao Min had them in her new ledger under a heading nobody had ever used before. Xiaoyu was writing what they'd said, the best of it, the lines that had come over the open channel and made the banking hall go quiet. *SOMETHING THAT MAKES THINGS GROW. I WANT TO HEAR THE DEAD, NOT RAISE THEM. I JUST WANT TO FLY.*

And at the head of the new column, before any of them:

*WEN JIA. LINGZHOU. WENT FIRST.*

He stood at the foot of the ladder and watched her write it.

> **The letters are different,** said Seed.

He looked. To his eye they were the same square capitals she'd put on that wall for two years. But through Seed they weren't ink. They were written as nothing on that wall had ever been written, like the three lines that had stayed hanging over the stone after she took her hand away. Set. Fixed. As if the plaster had agreed to remember.

> **I can't remember her. But I can read it, and when I read it, it's true. It isn't like reading a name off a list. It's like being told by someone who was there.**
>
> **That isn't remembering. I know it isn't; I've checked. But it's something.**

At the top of the ladder his sister had stopped and was looking down at him with the marker in her hand.

"It's talking about my handwriting," she said. "Isn't it."

"Yes."

"Good," said Xiaoyu, and went back to writing. "Somebody should."

---

Kwan came down the stairs at a quarter to midnight holding the laptop open in front of her like a tray of something hot, and he knew from her face.

"It's pulling again."

The threads: eleven fine pale lines rising off the diagram of the Stele into the dark. Slack since the night before, when eleven people from the bottom of a well had gone into eleven stones and sat down in the knots and sent nothing up. One tug, then, all together, from the far end.

Now they weren't slack. All eleven were taut, drawn straight up into the dark like kite strings in a strong wind, and every few seconds they jerked together. Not hard. Steadily. Like a line when whatever's on the end has stopped being curious and started to pull.

On the radio at Kwan's belt, through Ngozi Eze's handset at Port Abiri, the old man who liked the sea:

> ***...it's getting heavier. I'm holding it. We're all holding it. The girl in Lingzhou says hold on to the knot, not the thread.***
>
> ***...but someone should know. It's getting heavier.***

And from the Lingzhou stone, three kilometers off on East Ring Road, through the staff, small and stubborn and seventeen, a voice he hadn't expected to hear again that night:

> ***...captain's boy. There's something at the other end of my thread. Not pulling. Something else.***
>
> ***...it's asking for the girl who writes.***

---

They went at one in the morning.

Not all of them. Xiaoyu said it was her it had asked for, so it was her window and she'd choose who came. She chose her brother, because the staff was the only way anything at the far end of a thread could be heard. Old Seven, because he'd come anyway. Lingyue, because, Xiaoyu said, somebody should be there who could light the room.

Their mother didn't ask to come. She sat in her wheelchair at the bronze door and watched them go down the steps into the warm dark of East Market Road, and as her daughter passed she said only: "Don't answer anything."

"I know."

"It asks. And whatever you say, it keeps. Even no. Your brother told me: on the mountain it asked him and he didn't say anything, and that was the only thing that worked."

"I know, Mom."

"So don't answer anything."

Xiaoyu stopped on the bottom step and looked back up.

"I'm not going to answer anything," she said. "I'm going to ask it something."

---

The Awakening Hall was dark, and the letters were still there.

Three lines of soft light hung over the platform where she'd left them that morning, in the empty bowl of four thousand seats: **[Class: Scribe] [Chosen.] [Talent: — not marked —]**. Under them the black stone with its crack and its downward-pointing runes, and a bright blue jacket over its shoulder. He could feel its warmth from the doors.

Xiaoyu walked down the aisle and up the three steps and stood in front of it under her own letters, in her school jacket, with her marker in her pocket. Lingyue stood a step behind with the flame in her open hand. Old Seven took the foot of the steps, facing out. Yuanshu stood beside him with the staff.

"Hello," said Xiaoyu to the stone. "It's me again."

> ***...I know,*** said Wen Jia. ***...I'm going to let it through now. If you want.***

"I want."

> ***...it's very old. Be careful. Don't tell it anything.***

"I know," said his sister. "Everybody keeps saying."

---

It came up the thread as it had once come down the lens on the mountain.

Not a weight, not a pressure: an attention. From farther away than anything. Up out of the deep water between arms along a thread finer than a hair, through the crown of the tree of Laws inside the stone, past a girl of seventeen sitting in a knot, into the hall.

Warm. That was still the worst of it. Interested. Patient. The kindness of a very old person to a child who's come to visit.

> *Hello.*

It arrived in his mind as it had on the mountain, already finished, in his own language, like a cup set down so carefully that it wouldn't wake anyone. And in Xiaoyu's. He saw her go still.

> *You are the one who writes. I heard you this morning. Everyone did. You told the stone what you wanted, and what to call you. It was very bright.*
>
> *I did not keep it. It was not said to me.*
>
> *I would like to tell you something first, before anything else. Your brother taught the keeper that. I learned it from watching. I learn everything from watching.*

Xiaoyu said nothing.

> *The pulling on the threads is not me. I want you to know that. I do not pull. I ask.*
>
> *The stones are very old. Older than the gardens. We made things like them once, a long time ago, to count. Then we stopped making things. I keep. That is all I do now.*
>
> *But the one who picks still listens at the other end of the stones. It has never felt one go quiet. Not once, in all the orchards. Tonight eleven went quiet at once.*
>
> *It does not ask. It never learned; nobody taught it. It knows only that when the stones are bright it comes, and when the stones are quiet something is wrong.*
>
> *It is pulling to see what is wrong. It is going to come and look.*

---

The hall was silent. The three lines of light hung over the platform. In Lingyue's palm the flame didn't flicker.

> *That is what I came to tell you. And to ask you something.*
>
> *I asked your brother once, on a mountain, what he wanted. He did not answer. I said I would not ask him twice, and I have not. I have not asked you yet.*
>
> *Child. You who write.*

Xiaoyu took her hand out of her pocket.

The marker was in it, capped. She looked at it a moment. Then she looked up: not at the letters, not at the stone, but toward the place far off past the roof and the sky and the keeper and the branch where a thread finer than a hair went down into the deep water.

"Wait," said Zhou Xiaoyu. "I go first."

The attention paused.

"That's the rule. It's not on the wall yet but it's going to be. Whoever's been asked the least goes first." Her voice was steady. He didn't know how. "And nobody's ever asked you anything."

She uncapped the marker.

"Everybody asks everything now. My brother asked the keeper. The keeper asked the rings. The rings asked each other. Somebody asked the tree. Wen Jia asks every kid who touches this stone. You asked all those monks on the mountain, and the soldier behind the curtain, and the Architects, and kept everything they said." She looked up into the dark. "But nobody ever asked you. Did they? Since before the gardens. Nobody ever asked the one who asks."

The attention didn't move.

"So I'm asking. I'll write down whatever you say, on the wall, and then I'll let it go. I won't keep it. It won't be mine. It'll just be written, so nobody can say afterward it wasn't there." She held up the marker. "That's what I'll do with your answer. You're hearing it first."

She drew a breath.

"What do you want?"

---

Nothing.

For a long time: longer than the keeper on the first night, longer than the rings, longer than the bottom of the well. The attention rested on the hall, warm and patient and enormous, and said nothing at all, and standing at the foot of the steps with the staff in his hand and a dead captain beside him, Yuanshu understood that it wasn't waiting for her.

It was looking for the answer.

As the first ring had looked when the Elder asked it every hour for five days: inward, to where something used to be. As a very old thing looks when it's asked a question nobody has asked it since before it was old, and goes to where the answer should be, and finds it has never once been there.

Lingyue's flame burned straight up in her hand.

Xiaoyu stood under her letters with the marker uncapped and waited, and didn't look in the bowl.

Then, faint and very far away, in a tone like a cup set down so carefully it made no sound at all:

> *...no one has asked me that.*

The attention stayed on her a moment longer.

> *...I do not know. I have kept every want I was ever given. I do not know if I have one.*
>
> *...I will look.*

And after a long pause, so quietly that afterward none of them was sure it had been said, the oldest thing any of them had ever heard said what two hundred million cousins said every morning to a keeper above the sky, and what the Elder said from inside a ring:

> *...ask me again tomorrow.*

---

It went.

He felt it lift off the hall and go back down the thread, out through the crown, past Wen Jia in her knot, up through the roof and the sky into the deep water. Not quickly. Like an old person getting up from the table after a visit, slowly, thinking about something.

Xiaoyu stood on the platform a moment longer. Then she capped the marker and came down the steps very straight, past Lingyue, past her brother, past Old Seven, and sat down on the bottom step in the dark and put her face in her hands and shook.

Lingyue sat beside her and put an arm round her and said nothing at all.

> **It didn't keep anything,** said Seed. **It asked her nothing, and she answered nothing. She asked it. And it said tomorrow.**

*Will it come back tomorrow?*

> **I don't know. Everything that's ever been asked has changed. I said that, and your sister wrote it on the wall. I didn't know whether it was true for something that old. I still don't. But it said tomorrow.**

On the radio at his belt, faint, from Port Abiri:

> ***...still pulling. Still heavy. Still holding.***
>
> ***...it's coming. Whatever it is. You can feel it walking along the thread. Very far. Very slow. But walking.***

---

At four in the morning Xiaoyu climbed the stepladder in the banking hall one more time.

She didn't write what the Collector had said. She'd promised to write its answer, and it hadn't answered. Instead she wrote at the very top of the wall, above the first column, above HUNDRED-ARROW LIN and everything else, in a strip nobody had ever used because the ladder didn't quite reach, so that she had to stand on its top step on her toes with Lingyue holding her ankles:

*THE COLLECTOR. ASKED. NO ANSWER YET. ASK AGAIN TOMORROW.*

And under it, smaller, which Seed read through her brother's eyes and found, as it always would now, to be true:

*THE ONE WHO PICKS. COMING.*
