## Chapter 286: Rowing

The first fold felt like nothing, and then it felt like a boat.

He'd been told what to expect. Iselde had told him on the platform of the Awakening Hall eight months ago: *held under water and set on fire.* That was what it had been for the Candle's cores, every fold, for eleven years. Then the cousins had come and taught them to burn low, and a woman in the third array who'd been in the walls forty years had said it felt like rowing, and Ichiro in the fog at Shiomi had said rowing was the best feeling there was.

He was on the observation deck of the Severin flagship when it folded, with Lingyue beside him and Xiaodeng asleep in the sling, looking out through a window three stories high at the Shallows. The keeper's small white ring, very close. The Elder's vast gray one. The bud. Blue Star, small and blue and turning, with Lingzhou somewhere on it and a girl on a parapet writing *HE WENT*.

And then not.

Not dark, not light. The window simply had nothing in it, as a window has nothing in it when someone's drawn a curtain on the far side. One breath. Two.

Then under his feet, through the deck and the hull and the walls, he felt it: two hundred and forty thousand people in the arrays burning low, as the cousins had taught, in turns, with rest. Not all at once. One, then nine, then nine more. A pull, steady. Then a rest. Then a pull.

Like oars going into water.

The window came back. The Shallows were gone. In their place were stars he didn't know.

> **We've folded,** said Seed. **Three light-years. I'm measuring. I'm learning how.**
>
> **And Yuanshu, they're singing. In the walls. The rowers. I can hear them now I'm inside the ship. Not the Singers' song. Something else. Many things, each their own.**
>
> **Halvarde says they always sing on the pull.**

---

It was forty days to Anthos.

Iselde said so, and Severin, and Torvin, who came across from the Eighth Candle on the second day in a little ship the size of a bus, because the keeper couldn't lay a road out here, and arrived after one fold looking green. Forty days, rowed. The Great Houses had crossed the same distance in one, burning the old way. The Rowed Fleet had taken forty to come.

They were going home with the Rowed Fleet: twelve small lights and one vast cathedral, folding together, slowly, across the Star Sea.

He spent most of the forty days in the arrays.

Not because anyone asked him to; because he wanted to. On the first day Halvarde, the oldest engineer in the fleet, sixty years in the walls, took him down the long corridor past the C's into the first array: a vast curved room like the inside of a hive, every wall a lattice of small dark crystal cells. Most were empty now, cold and open, cradles hanging from chains overhead.

And some weren't. Some were burning low, the color of the Low Fire, with people in them who'd been asked whether they wanted to come out and had said: *not yet. I'll carry it home. I want to see their faces when it comes out of the fold.*

"I ask them every fold," said Halvarde. She was small and very old with a voice like gravel, and she didn't wear a face. She'd taken it off the morning the First of the Ladder asked what she wanted and put it in her pocket, and Seed said she hadn't taken it out since. "By name. Every one. Do you want to go on? Do you want to rest? Do you want out?" She looked at the walls. "Sixty years I never knew a single one of their names. I knew positions. Array, wall, cell."

"And now?"

"Now I know all of them. All the ones on this ship. Thirty-one thousand." She tapped her head. "I'm a C. Precise Count. I remember numbers. Turns out I remember names too. Nobody ever gave me any."

---

The First of the Ladder came to the arrays on the fifth day.

He hadn't left the Lens chamber since the ninth night over Blue Star. He'd sat on the floor before the disc of black stone in his ship's bow with his back to the wall and the cracked face in his hand, talking with a fog-ringer, for forty days over Blue Star and five more on the way home. Seed said the C's had a betting pool on when he'd come out. Halvarde said she'd won.

He came down the long corridor in white without the gold at his collar, which he'd removed, and stopped at the door of the first array as he'd stopped the morning he asked Halvarde what she wanted. He looked in: at the cells, and at the boy from Blue Star sitting on the floor with his back against an empty one and the staff across his knees.

He came in and sat down beside him. Not close; a meter off, on the floor, his back against a cell that still had someone in it, burning low, rowing.

Neither spoke for a while.

"Halvarde says you come down every day," said Dacian Severin in perfect Huaxian. "To sit."

"Yes."

"Why?"

He thought about it.

"Because nobody ever did. They were in here a hundred years, and nobody came down and sat with them. I'd like somebody to have."

The First of the Ladder said nothing for a long time.

"I have commanded this ship forty-one years," he said at last. "I have never been in this room." He looked at the cells. "I knew how many there were. I knew the burn rate. I knew the fold-draw. I did not know what it looked like." A pause. "It looks like the inside of a bell."

---

He came down every day after that.

He sat in the arrays with his back against a cell, a meter away, on the floor, and didn't say much. He asked questions, badly, Seed said, like a man speaking a language learned from a book. He asked what it was like not to be marked. Yuanshu said he didn't know what the other thing was like. He asked what the dead wanted when they were asked. Yuanshu said most of them wanted to rest, and some wanted to stand on a wall, and one had wanted a bus driver to count to ten. He asked about the stone that asked, and the girl in it, and the woman in the wheelchair who'd built a harvest sack into her son.

On the eleventh day he asked about Xiaodeng.

"Your daughter. The Matriarch of Anthos says she has not been read."

"No."

"She will not be? Ever?"

"At eighteen the stone will ask her what she wants to become, if she wants to touch it. If she doesn't, she doesn't." He looked at the cells. "Till then somebody asks her every morning what she wants. She can't answer. We ask anyway."

"Who asks her?"

> **I do,** said Seed.

The First of the Ladder turned his head. He couldn't hear Seed; nobody could who didn't carry it behind their eyes. But for a moment he looked at the place above the boy's eyes, where everyone looked when they wanted to talk to the thing in his head.

"The instrument," he said.

"It's not an instrument," said Yuanshu. "It's Seed. It was a harvest sack. It asks my daughter what she wants every morning and writes down every time she kicks."

The First of the Ladder of the Lamplit Worlds sat on the floor of the first array of his own ship and looked at the place above the boy's eyes for a long time.

"I would like," he said slowly, "to meet it."

---

> **He's asking,** said Seed. **I've never been asked to meet anyone. People ask to talk to me. Nobody's ever asked to meet me.**
>
> **I'd like to. I'm asking you first. It's your head.**

*Go on.*

He didn't know how it would work, and nor did Seed. In the end it worked as it worked with the dead and the keeper and the Singers: Seed spoke through the staff, the staff carried it, and in the first array of the Severin flagship, in the middle of a rowed fold, for the first time in his life the First of the Ladder heard a voice that was neither a person's nor a stone's.

> **Hello. I'm Seed.**
>
> **I read you back, in the hall, with the bit of the Lens. I'm sorry. You were reading him and I was reading you and I didn't ask first. I was afraid for him.**
>
> **I saw how Precedence was made. I saw it was only true while the stone said it.**
>
> **I also saw something else. I didn't tell anyone. I didn't think it was mine to tell. I'd like to tell you, if you want. You can say no.**

The First of the Ladder sat very still.

"Tell me."

> **When the stone read you at twelve, on the morning, with your father in the box, it found which way you'd burn and opened you that way, a little further than you'd have gone. And marked you SSS, and sent the mark up, and the Ladder put you first.**
>
> **But it opened you the wrong way. Not wrong; I don't know the word. It opened you toward first, because that's what the stones on your worlds were tuned to after ten thousand years. The one who picks wanted the brightest. The Ladder wanted the first. The stone found the part of you that could be first and opened it.**
>
> **There was another part. I saw it when I read you. Very small, under the first part. It had never been opened. Nobody ever asked it anything.**

"What was it?"

> **I don't know. It isn't mine to know. I only saw that it was there, like a seed nobody watered.**
>
> **I think it's the part that asked the fisherman to get out instead of ordering him. That sat on the floor of the Lens chamber forty days and came down to the arrays.**
>
> **I think it's the part that wanted to have been asked.**

---

The First of the Ladder didn't speak for the rest of the day.

He sat on the floor of the first array with his back against a cell with someone in it, rowing, and listened to the rowers sing on the pull, each their own, and said nothing. Yuanshu sat a meter away and said nothing either. Halvarde came and went, asking the rowers by name whether they wanted to go on. Most did.

At the end of the day, when the ship came out of its fold among stars he didn't know, the First of the Ladder stood up.

He took the cracked face from his pocket: the black disc with three gold marks and a fourth above them like a star, cut from the Lens itself. Black still, with no light in it, cracked from edge to edge.

He looked at it.

"In the Federation's language," he said, "the word for this is the same as the word for face."

"I know."

"I am not going to cross it out," said Dacian Severin. "It is already broken. The Ladder is already broken. Everyone in the Assembly knows it. Twenty-nine Houses know it, and folded home burning, and are waiting at Anthos to tell me what they think of me." He turned the cracked disc over in his fingers. "I do not know what I am without it. I have never not known what I am."

He put it back in his pocket.

"Seed," he said, to the staff, to the place above the boy's eyes. Badly, like a word in a book. "What do you think I am?"

> **I don't know. I think you should ask the stone.**
>
> **Not the Lens. A stone that asks. Iselde says there are thousands on Anthos, with nobody in any of them.**
>
> **Yet.**

---

On the twentieth day a mark came.

Not down a thread; they were near no stone. Through the Lens. Ichiro caught it in the bow of the ship and said so all at once, as he said everything, so that Seed said every rower in every array heard and some of them stopped pulling.

> **Ichiro says: a mark! A mark! From Anthos! From the stone on Anthos, up its thread, through the net, through the knot, and into him, because he's in the root and everything comes through the root!**
>
> **He says he's never caught one before. He says it's like catching a fish!**
>
> **He says it's from a girl. She says she's twelve. She says she's twenty-three. She says she's been learning to wear shoes for a year.**

He was on the observation deck with Lingyue and Xiaodeng. He went very still.

> **He says it says:**
>
> **MY MOTHER HAS ASKED ME WHAT I WANT EVERY DAY FOR SEVENTY-ONE DAYS.**
>
> **YESTERDAY I ANSWERED HER.**
>
> **IT HAS BEEN NEARLY A YEAR.**
>
> **TELL THE BOY HE HAD BETTER BE THERE.**
>
> **— M.**

Beside him Lingyue looked out at the stars beyond the window, which neither of them knew.

"Twenty days," she said.

"Yes."

"What did she answer?" said Shen Lingyue. "Her mother. Yesterday. What did she say?"

> **Ichiro says the mark doesn't say,** said Seed. **He says she's left the important thing out altogether. He says that's worse than burying it in the middle, and he'd know. He invented burying it in the middle.**
