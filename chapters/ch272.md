## Chapter 272: A Note Not in Any Song

They couldn't move her.

Bai Lu tried. She came through the side door at twenty past four in a coat over her nightclothes with a bag in one hand, ran down the aisle past a thousand tall pale children of the Hollow Bell without looking at any of them, climbed to the platform, put her hands on Lingyue, and said in her battlefield voice, *right, we're going to the hospital, now, both of you*. And then stopped.

Because the lamp wouldn't go out.

Lingyue stood beside the warm black stone with her hand still raised and the pale light in it, lamp-sized, steady, her other hand flat on her stomach and her face white, and she couldn't put it down. She said so through her teeth with her eyes shut. It was coming up from underneath as it had all winter, as the moon pulls water, and now that something was happening it was coming faster, and if she let go of the lamp she didn't know what it would do.

"It's the baby," said Bai Lu, to nobody, to herself, her hands flat on Lingyue's back. "It's been pulling on it for months. And now it's coming, and it's pulling on everything." She looked up at the lamp. "If she drops it, it goes everywhere at once. Like the wall."

"Then she doesn't drop it," said Yuanshu.

"She can't hold it up for twelve hours."

"Watch me," said Shen Lingyue.

---

So it happened there.

On the platform of the Awakening Hall on East Ring Road, before a warm black stone with a blue jacket over its shoulder, under three lines of light that had hung there since Awakening Day, at the foot of a thread rising into the heart of a vast pale shell where the Old Chord had stopped singing and gone silent. With a thousand and seventy children of another world in the aisle, three meters tall and translucent, their own small lights inside them, not singing either. Watching.

Bai Lu sent for blankets and Fat Tan brought them. He'd heard; everyone had heard. Seed hadn't told anyone, but Tam had, because Tam heard everything now, and the city woke as a city wakes when something's happening and nobody's said what. The holders came and stood in a line up the side aisle with their hands on one another's shoulders, not because the stone needed holding but because, one of them said, it seemed the right way to stand.

At five Dabao and Fat Tan carried his mother in through the side door in her wheelchair, and she said nothing at all. She had Fat Tan put her at the foot of the steps, and she sat with her thin hands folded on the blanket and her quick dark eyes on her son's wife and her lips moving. He thought she was saying the names. Then he understood she wasn't. She was counting under her breath, as she'd counted the seconds once in a basement with her hand on a crystal, nine years ago, asking four thousand people if they'd let her build something.

Xiaoyu sat on the step beside the wheelchair with her marker in her hand, capped, writing nothing.

Old Seven stood at the side door, facing out.

---

He held her up.

There was nothing else to do. Bai Lu did everything else, Bai Lu and a midwife from the old quarter who'd come with Dabao's mother, who looked at the thousand children of the Hollow Bell in the aisle and the lamp in Lingyue's hand and said only, *well, I've seen stranger, I delivered in the Black Tide*, and took off her coat. He stood behind Lingyue on the platform with his arms round her and the staff on the floor beside them, and held her so she could keep her arm up and the lamp lit.

"Don't you dare," she said at seven, through her teeth.

"Don't what?"

"Count."

"I'm not counting."

"Seed is."

> **I am,** said Seed. **A hundred and fifty-six. A hundred and fifty-two. A hundred and forty-nine: your heart. A hundred and sixty: the baby's.**
>
> **I'm sorry. I can't not count. It's what I am. I asked her last month whether I could count this, and she said yes.**

"I said yes to the heartbeat," said Lingyue. "Not to— AH." The lamp in her hand flared white and the whole hall went pale as the inside of a shell, and a thousand children of the Hollow Bell in the aisle took a step back. Then she held it. Brought it down. A lamp again, lamp-sized. "Not to *all* of it," she said, when she could.

> **I'm sorry.**

"Keep counting," said his wife. "It helps. Heaven help me. It helps."

---

At nine the sun came up over the river.

It came in through the high windows the color of strong tea, as it had on Awakening Day, onto the platform and the stone and a woman held up by her husband with a lamp in one raised hand and her face wet and white and set the way it set before a fight; onto Bai Lu kneeling, and the midwife from the old quarter; and onto a woman in a wheelchair on the steps, counting.

At ten past nine the Cantor came down.

He didn't see her come; he was holding Lingyue. But he felt the hall change, as a hall changes when someone very old walks into it, and looked up, and she was there.

At the top of the aisle, come down the keeper's road through the roof, unasked, alone. Taller than any of them: four meters, perhaps. Not translucent. So old her glass had gone the color of milk, so that the light inside showed only faintly, like a moon behind cloud. A great ring of throats at the base of a long neck, opening and closing slowly. Not dressed in light. Dressed in nothing: a Singer who'd dressed a thousand ages of children in light and sung them up, and never once worn it herself.

She came down the aisle and the thousand and seventy made way. They didn't bow; the Hollow Bell had no bowing, Seed said. They went still, as you go still when someone walks in who's been the center of every room you've ever been in.

She stopped at the foot of the steps, beside his mother's wheelchair, and looked up at the platform.

> **She says: what is this?**

"She's having a baby," said Xiaoyu from the step, before anyone could stop her, fast, as she said things she'd just understood. "It's coming. It's early. My sister-in-law held up the lamp so your children could see where the shore was, and now she can't put it down, and the baby's coming."

The Cantor looked a long time at the girl of seventeen on the step with a capped marker in her hand.

> **She says they don't have this. On the Hollow Bell children are sung. They're not— she doesn't have the word. They're sung into being by a chord of seven. They come out of the song already standing.**
>
> **She's never seen one come like this. She asks: may she stay?**

His mother didn't stop counting. But she lifted one thin hand off the blanket and laid it very lightly on the long pale milk-glass foot of the oldest Singer of the Hollow Bell, and left it there.

The Cantor looked down at it, and stayed.

---

At thirteen minutes past ten, in the Awakening Hall on East Ring Road in Lingzhou, on the platform where every eighteen-year-old in the city had put a hand on a stone for forty-two years and been told what they were, a child was born.

He wouldn't remember it properly. He'd try many times afterward and fail. He'd remember Lingyue's voice saying something that wasn't a word, and then his name, and then something about Bai Lu that Bai Lu said she wouldn't repeat. He'd remember the lamp in her hand going up white all at once, lighting the whole hall and the long curved roof he'd walked under at eighteen from inside like a shell, so bright that a thousand children of the Hollow Bell cried out. He'd remember the midwife from the old quarter saying *there, there, there you are*. He'd remember his mother at the foot of the steps stopping counting.

Then the lamp went out.

Not dropped. Not burned. It went out as a lamp goes out on a shore when the boat it was lit for has come in: all at once, softly. The light that had been coming up from underneath all winter went down and down and settled, and was only Lingyue's own, inside her, where it had always been.

And in the sudden quiet of the Awakening Hall, in the sun coming in through the high windows, there was a sound.

Small. High. Furious. Not a word. Not a song.

A note.

---

> **It's a girl,** said Seed. **Bai Lu says it's a girl. Two point six kilograms. Four weeks early. Breathing. Bai Lu says breathing's the most important thing, and she's breathing very well. She's breathing like her mother, which is to say, furiously.**
>
> **A hundred and fifty-eight. The same as the first time I counted, in the back room, in the summer. A hundred and fifty-eight.**
>
> **I'm recording this. I asked last month. She said yes. Obviously.**

He was on his knees on the platform. He didn't remember kneeling. Lingyue was in his arms, gray and wet and shaking and laughing, and in her arms, wrapped in one of Fat Tan's blankets, something tiny and red with its eyes screwed shut and its mouth open, making a sound.

The same sound, over and over. Not a word. A note.

---

In the aisle the children of the Hollow Bell had gone completely still. All thousand and seventy, and the Cantor at the foot of the steps, four meters tall, milk-glass, with his mother's hand on her foot. All looking at the platform. At the sound.

> **Ninth says that isn't in the song,** said Seed. **It isn't in any song. It isn't a chord or a sentence. It's one note from one throat, and it isn't for anything. It isn't asking to be chosen. It isn't asking to be passed by. It isn't asking for anything.**
>
> **She says it's just saying: here I am.**

And Ninth, at the front of the aisle, three meters tall with her own small light inside her, opened one throat and sang her note back.

Not the one from the platform, not the one she'd sung up the thread. A new one. Small. Answering.

And behind her, one by one, then many, then all of them, a thousand and seventy children of the Hollow Bell who'd come down a road of light to see a lamp opened their throats and sang their own notes. Not together, not a chord, not the song: each their own, the one thing that was theirs, answering a note that wasn't in any song, until the Awakening Hall was full of them. A thousand small different notes rising through the roof, up the thread, into the dark, toward a silent shell over the city.

The most beautiful thing any of them had ever heard.

And nobody wanted it to stop.

---

At the foot of the steps the Cantor of the Hollow Bell had begun to shake.

He saw it: the great ring of throats at the base of her long milk-glass neck opening and closing, opening and closing, and no sound coming out. The oldest Singer of a world that had sung one sentence for ten thousand years, standing in a hall full of children singing notes that weren't in it.

His mother hadn't taken her hand off the Cantor's foot.

> **She says—** Seed stopped, then went on very carefully. **She says she hasn't got one. A note of her own. She's sung the song nine hundred years. She sang her own children up in it. She's never sung anything that was hers.**
>
> **She says she doesn't know how.**

His mother looked up at her: Zhou Wanqing in her wheelchair, at the foot of a platform where her son's wife held her granddaughter, with a thin hand on the foot of a being from another world who'd sung her own children up a thread to a hand in the dark. She didn't have the Cantor's language. Seed carried it for her.

"Neither did she," said his mother, and nodded at the small red furious thing in Fat Tan's blanket, still making its sound. "Ten minutes ago. Nobody does. You just open your mouth and find out."

---

> **Yuanshu.**

Evening. They'd finally moved her, Lingyue and the baby, to the hospital, where Bai Lu had made everybody go home except him and his mother and Xiaoyu, and then made Xiaoyu go home, and then looked at his mother and given up. Lingyue was asleep. The baby was asleep in a clear plastic box beside the bed, which Lingyue had called ridiculous and Bai Lu had called not negotiable.

He sat on the edge of a hard hospital chair, looking at her.

> **I'd like to say it now, if you'll let me. I'm asking first.**

*Yes.*

> **Hello. I'm Seed. I live in your father's head. I was a harvest sack once. I count things.**
>
> **I counted you this morning. A hundred and fifty-eight, same as the first time.**
>
> **You can't answer. You're not anyone yet. Article Twelve, your grandmother's: your parents decided for you, once, and now they owe you the question. So do I.**
>
> **I'm going to ask you every morning, until you can answer, and after. I'm telling you first.**
>
> **What do you want?**

The baby in the plastic box didn't wake. She turned her head a little, as babies do, toward a sound only she could hear. Her mouth opened, and she made a small sound. Not a cry. Not a word.

A note.

> **That was a coincidence,** said Seed. **Lingyue would say.**
>
> **I'm writing it down anyway.**
