## Chapter 304: The Two O'Clock

His name was Kang Yousheng, and he'd run the ferry at the old steps for fifty-two years.

Lingyue hadn't known that. She'd seen him, of course; everyone in the hall had, because he came to the two o'clock every night and the two o'clock was small enough that you saw everybody. A thin old man in a padded jacket with a flask of something that wasn't tea. He sat in the third tier on the aisle with his hand flat on the stone of the step in front of him, as holders did when they couldn't reach the platform. He said *eh* to Wen Jia every time she asked him, except once, on the fourth night, when he said, "I'm eighty-one, girl, what else am I going to do with two hours?"

He died at twenty to three on the thirteenth night with his hand on the step.

Nobody noticed at first. That was what she kept coming back to afterward. He didn't make a sound and he didn't fall. He was sitting in the third tier on the aisle with his hand on the stone and his eyes shut, like half the two o'clock, and the woman next to him thought he'd dozed off. It was Wen Jia who knew.

> ***...Lingyue,*** said the girl in the stone. ***...the ferryman. He's stopped being warm.***

---

Bai Lu said it was his heart.

She said it standing in the aisle at a quarter past three, bag open, sleeves pushed up, her face set the way it set when she'd done everything there was to do and it hadn't been enough. She said it quietly to Lingyue, then again, louder, to the nine hundred people of the two o'clock who were all on their feet in the tiers not knowing what to do with their hands.

"His heart," said Bai Lu. "It was old and it was tired. It would have stopped this week or next, whatever he was doing; I've had him on my list for a year." She looked round the hall. "Holding didn't help him. I'm not going to tell you it didn't hurt him, either. I don't know. Nobody's done this before."

Nobody had anything to say to that.

Lingyue went down the platform steps with her daughter asleep in the sling and stood in the aisle beside the third tier, looking at him. A thin old man in a padded jacket, eyes shut, his hand still flat on the step, the flask beside him with its cap off.

*Wen Jia,* she thought. She didn't have Seed, and she didn't need it; the girl in the stone could hear anyone in the hall who wanted to be heard. *Is he there?*

> ***...yes. By the stone. Close. He's very cold. Everybody is, at first.***
>
> ***...I asked him. I didn't know if I was allowed. I'm not the boy, I haven't got a door. But I can ask.***
>
> ***...he said he'd wait. He said: I want the one who asks to ask me. Nothing against you, girl.***

Lingyue stood in the aisle a moment.

Then she knelt in the third tier, put her free hand flat on the step beside his, as he'd been holding it, and stayed there until the shift was over.

---

The two o'clock the next night was six hundred.

She felt it before Gao Min said a word. She was on the platform with her hand on her husband's chest, and at two the floor under him thinned. Not gone, not near gone. But six hundred people in a hall that held fifteen hundred, sitting far apart, not looking at the seat in the third tier on the aisle where someone had left a flask, a paper boat of dumplings and a white chrysanthemum.

She could feel what it cost him, under her hand. The body on the mattress under the blue jacket drawing on that floor as it had for two weeks, harder every day, and the floor bearing it, and bearing it, and thinner.

"Novaya Zarya's on," said Gao Min behind her, low, stopwatch in one hand and pencil in the other. "It's morning there. Orlov's put in another four hundred. Kesse's sending what it can. It's enough." She paused. "For tonight."

"And tomorrow?"

Gao Min didn't answer.

---

There was a man on the steps of the Awakening Hall in the morning.

Lingyue saw him when she came out at seven for the shift change. Fifty or so, in a work coat, holding a sheet of card on a stick, the way people did on the news from Port Halvard. On it, in thick black marker that wasn't Xiaoyu's and wasn't true, only what he thought:

*HOW MANY MORE?*

He wasn't shouting. He just stood on the third step with his card while the line for the seven o'clock went round him on both sides, and nobody said anything to him, because the old quarter didn't.

She went down the steps.

She hadn't planned to. Su Mingli, at the door, said "Lingyue" in the voice that meant *don't*, and she went anyway, daughter against her chest, grandfather's sword on her back, and stopped on the step above him.

He looked up. He knew who she was. Everybody did.

"He was my uncle," said the man. "Kang Yousheng. The ferry."

She hadn't known that either.

"Fifty-two years," he said. "He took me across the river to school every morning. Every morning, in the Black Tide, when the bridges were down." His hand wasn't steady on the stick. "He was eighty-one. He should've been in bed. And he came here every night and sat on a step for two hours to keep your husband warm, and now he's dead." He looked at her. "How many more? That's all I'm asking. How many more? Is he worth it? Is one boy worth that?"

The line had stopped moving on both sides of the steps.

Lingyue looked down at him a long time.

She thought of everything her husband would have said. Something quiet and true that would have changed the man's face. He'd have asked him something; he always asked.

She wasn't her husband.

"No," she said.

The man stared at her.

"Nobody's worth anybody," said Shen Lingyue. "I'm not going to stand on these steps and tell you my husband's worth your uncle. He isn't. Nobody is." Her voice didn't shake. "Your uncle said yes. Every night. The girl in the stone asked him first, every night, and he said yes, and once he said: I'm eighty-one, what else am I going to do with two hours." She stopped. "I'm not telling you that makes it all right. I don't know if it does."

The man said nothing.

"He's by the stone," said Lingyue. "Your uncle. Wen Jia asked him. He said he'd wait. He wants the one who asks to ask him properly, when he's home." She looked at the card. "So that's what I'm doing. I'm keeping my husband warm so he can come home and ask your uncle what he wants. That's all I've got."

She turned and started back up.

"You can stand there," she said over her shoulder. "With that. It's a fair question. He'd say it's a fair question. I'm going to keep counting."

---

He was still on the steps at noon.

At two in the morning, he came in.

She saw him from the platform. He came through the doors without the card and walked up the aisle to the third tier, to the seat with the flask and the paper boat and the chrysanthemum, and stood there a moment. Then he climbed the three steps of the platform, past her, past her husband on the mattress, past the dead nun with the lamp, and put his hand on the stone.

**[He's going a long way. You'll be warm on purpose for two hours. It might make you tired. Do you want to?]**

The man looked at the letters a while.

"Is my uncle there?" he said.

**[Yes. Right here. He's cold. He says: eh.]**

The man made a sound that wasn't a word. Then he said, "Yes," and went back down and sat in the third tier on the aisle next to the flask, put his hand flat on the step, and shut his eyes.

The two o'clock that night was eleven hundred.

A noodle shop on Ferry Lane had shut early and come in, Gao Min said, all of them: the owner, the cooks, the owner's mother. A boy in Academy uniform had brought his whole class and said his great-grandfather used to take him across the river on the ferry when he was small, and he hadn't known until yesterday the old man had been coming here. Gao Min said she'd stopped trying to work out why people came. She just wrote the numbers down.

---

On the nineteenth night, the mark came from Anthos.

Not from him; from the other direction. Down through the Lens, out of the root, along the net, through Ichiro, who had a way of ringing that Xiaoyu could read like handwriting now. She'd been asleep on the bottom tier with the marker, and she woke all at once with her hand on the floor, sat up, and her face changed, and she read it out in the dark hall, slowly.

*FROM HALVARDE. AT ANTHOS. THROUGH THE FOG-RINGER.*

*THE SIXTEEN HOUSES HAVE STOPPED.*

*THEY WENT TO THE EDGE OF THE FEDERATION. THEY FOUND THE STONES THERE HAD READ THE MARK. ALL OF THEM. THERE IS NOWHERE IT WASN'T READ.*

*THEY ARE ASKING WHERE IT CAME FROM.*

Xiaoyu stopped.

"Go on," said Lingyue.

Her sister-in-law didn't look at her. She was looking at her own hand flat on the floor: the hand that had written *THIS IS NOT YOUR FACE* in square capitals on a wall in a bank on East Market Road and sent it, through a girl in a stone and a fog-ringer in a lens, to every Paragon in the Lamplit Worlds at once.

*THE FOG-RINGER CAN SEE THEM THROUGH THEIR FACES. THEY KNOW IT WAS WRITTEN. THEY KNOW WHAT A SCRIBE IS. THE OLD HOUSES REMEMBER THE WORD.*

*THEY KNOW IT WAS A GIRL. ON AN UNLISTED ORCHARD.*

*FOUR OF THE SIXTEEN WANT TO COME AND ASK HER TO WRITE SOMETHING ELSE.*

*TWELVE DO NOT.*

*THEY ARE ARGUING.*

*THE FOG-RINGER SAYS: TELL THE GIRL WITH THE SWORD. HE SAYS: HE'S SORRY. HE'S WATCHING. HE'LL RING THE MOMENT THEY DECIDE.*

The hall was silent. Eleven hundred people of the two o'clock in the tiers with their hands on the stone, eyes open now.

Lingyue looked at her husband's face, thin, asleep, impossibly far away. She looked at her daughter. She looked at the girl on the bottom tier with the marker in her hand, eighteen, Scribe, unmarked, whose writing was true.

"Ask them," said Zhou Xiaoyu, very steady, in her mother's voice. "If they come. Ask them what they want me to write." She capped the marker. "And then I'll tell them no."

Lingyue put her hand flat on her husband's chest.

"Gao Min," she said. "I need a second rota."
