## Chapter 301: The First Orchard

He went down the thread with Old Seven, and the Collector waited on the road.

It said it would. It didn't go down threads, it said; it had never needed to, because the wants had always come up to it. It would wait on the carry road where the old thread joined, like a grandmother waiting at the top of the cliff path while the young ones run down to the beach.

> *I have waited longer than this for less,* it said. *Go and see. Then tell me.*

So they went down the gray old thread, out of the carry road and away from everything, fast at first, then slower, then very slow, at the pace the drowned answered anything. Old Seven beside him, cold and steady. Seed counting.

> **This thread's older than any stone on Blue Star,** said Seed. **Older than the Federation's stones. And it was made by hand. Not by the picker, not by the Collector. By them: the ones who made the picker. This is one of the first.**

---

The stone stood on a hill.

He came down the thread into it as the man in the gray coat had gone into the stone below the House of Anthos, as Wen Jia had gone into the stone in Lingzhou: not into the rock but into the reading, the part of the stone that looked out at its world and read what grew there. He'd never been inside one. He'd touched plenty. He'd stood in front of one and felt a girl in it asking. He'd never been where she was.

It was like standing at the window of a dark house, looking out at a field at night.

The stone looked out because it couldn't help it; that was what it was for. And through it, as through glass, he saw what it saw. A low gray hill under a moonless sky crowded with stars he didn't know. A plain going away flat and pale and empty on every side to a bare horizon. A wind he couldn't feel and the stone could, moving over the plain and the hill and the stone itself as it had moved, Seed said, every night for longer than Seed had a number for.

Nothing breathing.

The stone read it anyway. Every year, on the morning it had been made to read on, it read the whole of its world for what ripened, found nothing, marked it, and sent the mark up the gray thread into the carry road.

> **It's been doing that since the last child,** said Seed. **I can see the marks in it, back and back. Every year the same: nothing, nothing, nothing. Before that, a long stretch of very few. One child. Two. None. One. Getting smaller, like a fire going out. And before that, a great many.**

*It picked them all,* he thought.

> **It picked everything bright, every year, for as long as anything here was bright. And then nothing was.**

---

Old Seven turned first.

He felt the cold ember beside him turn in the reading, as it turned on the north wall when something came out of the north. Not toward the plain. Down, into the hill.

> **He says they're here,** said Seed.

He'd known. He'd known on the carry road when the Collector said there was nobody. He was a necromancer. There was always somebody.

He looked down. The hill was full of them.

Not graves. Nothing on this world had been buried the way Blue Star buried things, in rows, under stones. They had lain down where they were when they stopped, and the wind had covered them, and the long time had pressed them into the hill until they were part of it. Embers so dim he'd never have seen them without the stone, gray on gray, cold as the bottom of the deep water. In the hill and round it and under the plain as far as the stone could read, which was further than he could take in. Millions.

And every one of them turned, very slightly, toward the stone on the hill, as the dead of Blue Star turned toward the north wall.

> **They're the ones who were left,** said Seed. **The dim ones. The ones the stone marked low and the hands didn't take, every year, for generations. They stayed and had children. The stone read their children. The bright ones were picked; the dim ones stayed and had children. Until there weren't any more.**
>
> **They're the last people of this world. Every generation that was left behind. And nobody has ever asked them anything.**

---

He didn't know how to begin, and that surprised him. He'd been asking the dead since a cemetery at three in the morning, and he'd always known the first word.

He didn't know their language. He didn't know what they'd called the hill. He didn't know whether they'd ever had a word for *ask*.

> **I don't know their language either,** said Seed. **But every one of them was read by this stone, and this stone was made by the same hands as the stones of Blue Star. The reading's the same. I can ask the way the stone reads: not in words, in the shape of a question. The way Wen Jia asks the babies before they can talk.**

*Then ask.*

> **You ask. I'll carry it.**

So he asked, down through the stone and the reading into the hill. Not *do you want to get up*; that was for soldiers. Something older, that came before getting up.

*Hello. I'm here. What do you want?*

---

Nothing happened for a long time.

He'd expected that. The drowned had taken minutes; the cloud in the deep water had taken hours. These had lain in a hill under a wind for longer than there had been a Blue Star, nobody had ever spoken to them, and the stone standing over them had only ever told them what they were.

He waited. Old Seven waited beside him, facing down into the hill. Seed counted. Far away, at the end of a thread gone very thin, fifteen hundred people in a hall on East Ring Road were being warm on purpose, and he could just feel them under everything, like a floor.

In the fourth hour, one of them answered.

Not with a word. With a turning. One ember close to the stone, almost underneath it, very small, turning toward the question as a sleeper turns toward a voice in the next room.

> **A child,** said Seed. **No. Old, very old. But she was read here as a child, and the stone remembers her that way. She was the last one it ever read.**

He looked at her: a gray ember in a hill, so faint she was hardly there. The last child of the first orchard, read on a morning long ago on this hill by this stone, marked, and not picked, because nothing in her was bright enough. She'd grown up on an empty plain among the last of the dim ones, grown old, and lain down as close to the stone as she could get.

> **She's asking something,** said Seed. **The shape of a question, the way the stone reads. She's asking: what am I.**

He went still.

> **It's all she ever had. The stone told her what she was. Every child on this world was told, every year, and it was the only question anybody ever answered for them. She thinks you're the stone. She thinks you've come to tell her again.**

*No,* he thought, down into the hill, as gently as he'd ever thought anything. *I'm not the stone. I'm not going to tell you what you are. Nobody's ever going to do that to you again. I came to ask.*

The ember didn't move for a while. Then, slowly, a different shape came back up through the hill, and Seed held it a moment before passing it on.

> **She says: ask what?**

---

So he told her, through the stone and Seed, in the shape of a question with no words in it. What he was. Where he'd come from. That on his world the stones had stopped telling and started asking, and that a girl who'd never been asked anything sat in one now and asked children what they wanted to become.

He told her the hands had stopped. That a man in an orange jacket had held a mark up to the face of the picker, and it had read something true and opened every hand it had.

He told her the house was empty.

He felt that one go into the hill. Not into her only; into all of them, millions of gray embers, generations of the left-behind lying under a wind on an empty plain, the news going through them slowly as news goes through a village at night. *The house is empty. It stopped. The hands are open. Nobody's taking anybody any more.*

They didn't do anything. They were too old to do anything. But the hill changed, the way a room changes when everyone in it lets out a held breath at once.

---

Then he asked her again. *What do you want?*

She didn't know. He felt her not knowing. Nobody on her world had ever had to answer it. On an empty plain among the dim ones there'd been nothing to want except not to be picked, and she hadn't been.

Then the Collector spoke. Not to him: down the gray thread from the carry road, quietly, as it had once spoken to his sister.

> *I have something of hers.*
>
> *She was the last one. On the last reading morning, when the hands came and there was nothing to take, I came after them, as I came after the hands everywhere. And I found a child on a hill beside a stone, read and not taken, and I asked her what she wanted.*
>
> *She told me, and I kept it. She was very small, and it was a very small want. I have kept it longer than almost anything.*
>
> *May I give it back?*

He looked at the ember in the hill.

*Someone's asking if they can give you something,* he thought, down to her. *Something you said a long time ago, when you were small. They kept it. They'd like to give it back. Do you want it?*

The answer came up through the hill very slowly.

> **She says yes,** said Seed.

---

It came down the thread like a seed dropping.

He felt it go past him, small and warm the way the Collector was warm, older than anything he'd ever touched, nearly as old as the thread, so faint it was hardly a want at all. It went down out of the carry road, through the stone, into the hill, found the gray ember lying as close to the stone as she could get, and went into her.

And she lit.

Not bright. Nothing on that world would be bright again; the picker had seen to that. But she lit as an ember lights when you breathe on it: a warm gray going faintly gold at the edges, for the first time in longer than he had a number for.

> **I've got it,** said Seed. **It wasn't a word. It was the shape of a want, the way she'd have said it at six on a hill to the warm thing that came after the hands. It was: to grow up.**

He didn't move.

> **And she did,** said Seed. **She grew up and grew old and lived her whole life on an empty plain and lay down by the stone. She did it without the want. It was kept; she never knew she'd wanted it. She did it anyway. And now she's got it back, afterward.**

The ember in the hill was turning toward him. What came up through the stone this time didn't need Seed to carry it; he felt it himself. Not words. A feeling so old and small and plain it went straight through him and out the other side.

*Oh,* said the last child of the first orchard. *I did. I did that.*

---

Old Seven hadn't moved.

> **He says ask her the other thing,** said Seed. **You've got a door. Ask her if she wants it.**

He didn't open it. He'd learned that in a cemetery and on a beach and in the deep water: you didn't open the door and then ask. You asked, and if they said yes you stood in it with your back to the light and didn't turn round.

*There's a door,* he thought. *I carry it. It goes somewhere I don't know. Nobody who's gone through has ever come back to say what's on the other side. But nobody I've asked has ever been sorry.*

*You can go through, now you've got your want back. Or you can stay here on the hill by the stone. Either one's yours. What do you want?*

The ember turned in the hill, gray and faintly gold.

> **She says the others first,** said Seed. **She was the last one read. She'd like to be the last one through. She says: ask them.**

---

He was there three days.

He didn't know it at the time; Seed told him afterward. Three days in the reading of a stone on an empty world, with the Collector waiting on the carry road above, and fifteen hundred people at a time rowing a body in a hall on East Ring Road, and a mark from home taking, by the end, four hours to reach them.

He didn't ask them all. He couldn't have; there were too many, and they were too old and slow and dim, and he was one boy in a stone. He asked the first nine. They asked their nine. As the dead of Blue Star did it, and the drowned, and the cloud in the deep water: each asking the one beside them in the shape of a question with no words, in the dark, in a hill, under a wind, generation by generation, until all the left-behind of the first orchard had been asked by each other, for the first time, what they wanted.

Most of them wanted the door.

He stood in it with his back to the light for three days while they went through, one at a time, millions, slowly, as the oldest dead did everything. He didn't turn round. Old Seven stood beside him facing down into the hill and didn't turn round either.

> **Death Law: 59.1 to 59.4 percent,** said Seed. **The wall's closer. I can feel it ahead of us like a cliff edge in the dark. It isn't dangerous yet. It's just there.**

On the third evening, when the hill was nearly empty and only a few thousand were left who'd said *not yet* or *stay* or *ask again*, which was allowed, the last child of the first orchard came to the door.

She stopped in it. He could feel her just behind him, a small dim ember with gold at the edges.

> **She says thank you,** said Seed. **She says tell the warm thing it can keep the next one; she won't mind.** A pause. **No. She's changed her mind. Tell it not to. Tell it to give them all back.**

Then she went through.

---

He came back up the gray thread to the carry road at the end of the third day, slowly, like someone climbing a cliff path from the beach at dusk.

The Collector was waiting where it had said it would be. Seed said it hadn't moved at all in three days, which for the Collector was no time, though Seed thought it had felt long to it anyway.

> *I heard,* it said. *She said to give them all back.*
>
> *I have a great many.*
>
> *I think I will need help.*
