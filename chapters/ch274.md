## Chapter 274: The Old Chord

The Old Chord came down on the twentieth day, all seven of them, down the keeper's road as the very old come down stairs: slowly, one step at a time, with long pauses, as if each step were a decision.

He'd never seen anything so old. Not the Cantor, nine hundred years and milk-glass. Not Sister Kiyo, paper over bone, twelve hundred years under a gate. The Old Chord were older than either. Seven Singers five meters tall, so old their glass had gone not white but gray, the gray of the rings, of walker-thread, so that no light showed through them at all. Many-throated, but some of the throats in each great ring had closed, Seed said, and wouldn't open again. They'd sung the tithe up the thread before the Cantor had, for longer than the Lamplit had existed. They'd taught her the song.

They'd pushed eight hundred and ninety-seven children off a beach in the dark toward a mouth that wasn't there. Forty-one had burned out in the thread before they saw the lamp.

They came down through the roof of the Awakening Hall onto the platform before the warm black stone, and stood there.

The hall was full, as it had been every day for twenty days: the thousand and seventy children of the Hollow Bell in the aisle with their own small lights inside them, the holders, the Candle's children, the city. And at the foot of the steps, in her wheelchair, his mother, and beside her four meters of milk-glass: the Cantor.

Nobody spoke.

---

> **They're asking for the boy who refused the tithe,** said Seed. **In the old sentence. It's the only one they have. Ichiro says they're bending it as far as it'll go.**
>
> **They say: you who would not take.**

He climbed the steps. Not quickly. He stood on the platform before seven gray Singers taller than anything in the hall, staff in hand, and said nothing, and waited.

> **They say: we pushed them. The eight hundred and ninety-seven, down the thread into your stone. Because the song said the tithe must be given whole, and you would not take it, and we thought you were testing us.**
>
> **They say: forty-one burned in the thread before they saw the lamp. We heard them burn. We heard them go dark. We heard the boy at the bottom of the thread ask them what they wanted, and heard them say chosen, and then heard one of them say: I saw the lamp.**
>
> **They say: we have never heard one of the chosen say anything but chosen.**

The seven gray Singers didn't move.

> **They say: we have sung the tithe up the thread for longer than there have been Lamplit. We chose the brightest. We dressed them in light. We sang them to the hand, every age, thousands of ages. And the hand took them and passed us by, and the song said we had done well.**
>
> **They say: we never once heard one of them say: I saw the lamp.**
>
> **They say: we do not know what we have done.**

---

He didn't answer at once.

He stood on the platform and looked at seven beings older than anything he'd ever spoken to, gray as the rings that had come to prune everything Seed touched, standing before a stone that asked. He thought of the Cantor at the back-room window, bent, singing one wavering note to a child in a drawer. *Three of them were her own.* And the Old Chord had taught her the song.

*You did what you were taught,* he thought. *By the song. By a hand in the dark. For longer than there were people to teach anything else.*

*Forty-one children burned in a thread.*

Both were true. He didn't know what to do with both at once.

> **Nor do I,** said Seed. **I've run it. There's no number for it.**
>
> **I don't think you have to know what to do with it. I think you only have to ask them what they want.**

---

*What do you want?* he said, up through the staff into the old sentence, which Ichiro had bent as far as it would go to fit the question. *I'm telling you first. Whatever you say, I'll tell the hall exactly. I won't do anything to you. Nobody here will.*

The seven were quiet a long time.

Then the one in the center, the oldest and grayest, with half her throats closed, bent slowly, as a reed bends, toward the stone.

> **She says: the stone. The one that asks.**
>
> **On the Hollow Bell there are seven stones in the Hall of Throats. Black. Warm. They read the little lamps when they stop glowing, and mark how bright, and the brightest are chosen and dressed in light and sung up. The Old Chord have sat beside those stones and sung for longer than anyone remembers.**
>
> **She says they're very old. Some of their throats have closed. In a few hundred of your years they'll stop. When they stop, they'd like to go into the stones.**

He went very still.

> **The way the girl went into yours. The one who never got to touch one, who asks.**
>
> **They'd like to sit in the stones of the Hollow Bell where they sang the chosen up, and ask every little lamp that touches them, for as long as the stones last, what it wants to become. They sang them up for ten thousand years. They'd like to ask them for ten thousand more.**
>
> **She says it won't be enough. Nothing will be enough. The forty-one are dark.**
>
> **But it's what they want. You asked.**

---

The hall was silent.

At the foot of the steps the Cantor hadn't moved. Her great ring of throats opened and closed, and no sound came out.

Then, from the front of the aisle, Ninth: three meters tall, translucent, her own small light inside her, the one who would have been sung up ninth, whose age-mates had been pushed off a beach in the dark, forty-one of them burned before they saw the lamp. She came slowly up the aisle and the platform steps and stood before the oldest of the Old Chord, who'd taught the Cantor the song that had dressed her in light.

She said nothing. She looked up.

And sang her note.

Not the one from the first morning, nor the one she'd sung up the thread, nor the one she'd sung back to a newborn's first cry. A different one. Lower. Harder. A note which, Ichiro said afterward from the fog at Shiomi, would have been, if it were a bell, the bell the shore rings when a boat comes in late having done something wrong at sea.

The headman's bell. *You came in. The lamp was lit. That's enough for one night.*

Not forgiveness. Ichiro was very clear: there was no forgiveness in it. The fog-code didn't have forgiveness, and nor did the note. It only said: *you came in.*

The oldest of the Old Chord stood on the platform before the stone, five meters of gray glass with half her throats closed, and listened.

And then, from one of the throats that still opened, very small, very old, wavering, for the first time in longer than the Lamplit had existed: a note.

Not in the song.

---

> **Death Law: no change. Level: no change,** said Seed. **I'm telling you because you asked me to tell you the numbers, even today.**
>
> **There aren't any numbers for today.**

---

The Singers left on the twenty-fourth day.

Not all of them. He asked; the Cantor asked; Ninth went down the aisle of the Awakening Hall and asked every one of the thousand and seventy, one at a time, in their own notes, which weren't a language yet but were becoming one. Most wanted to go home: to the Hollow Bell, to the Hall of Throats with its seven black stones, to the mothers who'd sung them up and didn't know yet they could come back. To teach the little lamps, the Cantor said, that there was more than one song.

Two hundred and six wanted to stay.

They wanted a sky. They wanted to swim in the river by the old ferry steps, as the Candle's children did. They wanted to stand in a courtyard and not sing for a while. Ninth was one of them. She said she'd put a question down, as Hush had told her, and she'd like to stay where it was and see what grew.

The Old Chord went home, all seven, to sit beside the stones and sing, the Cantor said, until they stopped, and then go inside them and ask.

And the Cantor went with them.

She came to the back-room window on the last morning and bent, four meters of milk-glass, until her great ring of throats was level with the sill. Xiaodeng was awake in the drawer. The Cantor sang her note, the low slow wavering one, the lullaby from before the hand, and Xiaodeng looked up at her and made a sound back.

> **She says that on the Hollow Bell a little lamp glows for one day. This one glowed for nine hours before she came, and lit a hall and a thread, and a thousand of the Cantor's children found the shore by it.**
>
> **She says: tell her, when she's old enough to be asked. Tell her the Hollow Bell owes her a song. Her own. Not the old one. When she wants it, she has only to ask, and they'll sing it.**

---

At dusk the shell over the city sang.

Not the song. Not *take them, and pass us by*. Not any sentence at all. Eight hundred and sixty-four Singers who'd come down a road of light to see a lamp, and the Cantor, and the seven of the Old Chord, and the tens of thousands of the choir who'd sung one sentence ten thousand years and gone silent twenty-four days because they knew no other, opened their throats as the vast pale shell turned over Lingzhou and began slowly to rise. And each of them sang their own note.

Not together, not in a chord. Tens of thousands of different small notes from tens of thousands of throats, rising over the old quarter and the river and the north wall and the long curved roof of the Awakening Hall, none of them meaning anything or for anything. Each one saying only: *here I am.*

It wasn't beautiful the way the song had been. It was nothing like that. It was ragged and strange and full of notes that didn't go together; some of the old ones wavered and some of the young ones cracked.

Nobody on Blue Star wanted it to stop.

The shell rose into the dark, singing, smaller and smaller, until it was a point of pale light among the stars in the north, and then nothing. On the north wall three thousand people stood with their faces turned up long after it had gone, listening to a sound only Seed and Tam could still hear.

> **It's still singing,** said Seed. **It'll sing all the way home. It'll be ragged the whole way.**

---

Kwan found them three nights later.

She came down the stairs at two in the morning with the laptop held out in front of her like a tray of something hot, and he knew from her face, as he'd known on the night of the three gray rings, and the night of the gardener, and the night of the Candle.

He was on the vault steps with the staff. Xiaodeng was asleep upstairs in her drawer, Lingyue beside her. Xiaoyu had gone to bed at one after asking the Collector what it wanted and writing on the back of Gao Min's hand. Ninth was in the courtyard under the stars.

Kwan turned the laptop round.

The keeper's eye. Seed's map of the Shallows: the arm, the twelve gold dots of the fields, the gray husks of the pruned ones, the dark place of the branch with its small gold point. The keeper's small white ring, a third smaller.

And at the far edge of the Shallows, very far, where the arm began to curve away toward the deep water and the places Seed's map had nothing: crinkles.

Not one. Many. Small, like the surface of a pond when something's dropped into it from very high. In a line, coming out of the dark one after another, like lamps coming on along a street.

He didn't have to count them. Seed did.

> **Ninety.**
>
> **It's spring.**
