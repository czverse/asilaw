## Chapter 289: Four Thousand Seats

The Assembly of the Lamplit Worlds sat on the third morning, and it was the Awakening Hall.

He knew it the moment he walked in. Not by its size; it was a hundred times larger, a bowl of black stone so vast its far side was blue with distance, open at the top to the morning sky of Anthos. Not by its light; every one of its four thousand seats was lit in the colors of its House or world. Not by its people: Paragons in white and gold, Matriarchs in deep red, Fleet Captains in their Houses' colors, the Speakers of eight thousand five hundred and eighty-eight worlds, every one with a face on a fine chain at the throat, cut from the Lens, three gold marks, four.

It was the shape. Tiers curving up into the distance. An aisle going down. And at the bottom, in the center, on a round platform of black stone, a place to stand: the place where you stood to be told what you were.

He stood on it.

---

He wasn't alone. The night before, in the flagship's Lens chamber, where they'd all gathered because it was the only room on the ship nobody from the twenty-nine could reach without walking past a one-armed dead captain, he'd asked who would come. Everyone said yes before he'd finished asking.

So they stood together on the platform at the bottom of the Assembly.

Old Seven at the front, one-armed, two meters ten, in rusted armor, spear grounded in his one hand, facing out into four thousand seats. Behind him in a line, each holding the one in front, sixty shapes of gray ash: the Third Battalion of the Eastern Line, who'd come down from the flagship in a ship the size of a bus because the keeper couldn't lay a road out here, and had walked into the Assembly in a line and halted and stood at ease. Lingyue, Xiaodeng in the sling, her grandfather's sword on her back, a lamp held in her hand. Tam and Seri, barefoot, holding hands. Bai Lu with her bag, who'd said she wasn't letting a month-old baby into a room full of Paragons without a doctor. Iselde, without a face.

And beside him at the edge of the platform, the First of the Ladder.

Not in white with gold at the collar. Not in white at all. In a plain gray coat of the kind the C's wore in the arrays, which Halvarde had handed him that morning without a word. With a cracked black disc in his pocket. Standing on the platform where he'd been read at twelve, beside a boy whose stone had gone black.

The Assembly went silent when he walked in.

Seed said that had never happened. In nine thousand years, Seed said, the Assembly had never gone silent for anything.

---

The Speaker sat at the front of the lowest tier, above the platform, in a seat of white stone: Aurelle Teller. Very old, older than Corvane, older than Severin, with white hair and a face like carved bone and a disc at her throat with four gold marks. Dark. Seed said so. Every Paragon's face had darkened on the ninth night, when a fog-ringer sat down in the Lens, and none had recovered. The light that had moved in the stone like water had gone out of every one.

She looked down at the platform.

"Dacian Severin," she said, with a grade; Seed carried it. "Formerly SSS. Formerly First."

"Aurelle," said Severin, without one.

The Speaker didn't move. But something in the four thousand seats did: a sound, not a word. The sound four thousand people make when a man they've knelt to all their lives speaks to the Speaker without her grade, in the Assembly, in front of them.

"You will answer to the Assembly," said Aurelle Teller. "For the Lens. For the cores. For the Ladder."

"Yes," said the man in the gray coat. "I'm here to." He looked up at her. "But first I'd like to ask the Assembly something."

---

He did it badly.

Seed said so afterward, not unkindly. The First of the Ladder asked the Assembly a question the way a man who's never been asked anything asks his first: too fast, then too slow, with the grade creeping back into his sentences and being taken out again one word at a time while four thousand people watched him do it.

"Ninety-two days ago," said Dacian Severin, "I went to an orchard in the Shallows to take an instrument and restore the Ladder. The instrument was a boy. And a thing in his head. And a girl in a stone. And a fog-ringer. And a question."

He stopped.

"On the ninth night the fog-ringer asked me what I wanted. In the Lens. In front of everything the Lens has ever been cut into. You heard him, every one of you. Your faces went dark because the root was asking, not reading."

Silence.

"He asked you too," said the former First of the Ladder. "All of you. What you wanted. And you answered, alone, in your cabins and your Houses and your beds. And someone heard."

He looked up at four thousand seats.

"I'd like to know what you said."

---

Nobody answered.

Four thousand seats of Paragons and Matriarchs and Fleet Captains and the Speakers of eight thousand five hundred and eighty-eight worlds, and nobody spoke. The bowl of black stone was so silent he could hear the wind high over its open top in the morning sky.

> **They don't know how,** said Seed. **They've never answered a question in the Assembly. The Assembly doesn't ask; it decrees. Every word ever spoken in this room had a grade in it.**
>
> **They answered alone on the ninth night because nobody could hear them. They don't know how to answer in front of each other.**

Then from the fourth tier, from a seat burning deep red, a woman stood up.

Sixty-one, tall, gray hair cut short, a long deep-red coat with gold at the collar, and at her throat on a fine chain a black disc with three gold marks, dark.

"Corvane Varr-Anthos," she said. Her voice carried to every seat; the bowl was built so. "Matriarch of the House of Anthos."

She didn't give her grade.

The sound went through the four thousand seats again, louder.

"I would like to be asked," said the Matriarch. "Here. In front of the Assembly. What I want." She looked down at the platform: not at Severin, at the boy. "I have an answer. I have been practicing it for ninety-one days."

---

He looked up at her: the tall woman in red in the fourth tier, who'd come down a road of light into a courtyard on Blue Star and been asked by a woman in a wheelchair whether she'd asked her daughter anything. Who'd sent the same clumsy mark down a stone to a room full of children learning to wear shoes for ninety-one days without missing one. Who'd told him first, before anyone, about the Lens.

"Matriarch," said Zhou Yuanshu, in the Federation's language, without the grade. "I'm going to ask you something. First I'll tell you what I'll do with your answer."

She waited.

"Whatever you say, I'll say it back, exactly. So the whole Assembly hears it twice: once from you and once from someone who was listening." He gripped the staff. "That's all I'll do. Nobody here has to do anything with it. You can say no. You can say ask me tomorrow."

The Matriarch stood in the fourth tier and said nothing for a long moment.

"What do you want?" said Yuanshu.

---

"I want to open the arrays of the House of Anthos," said Corvane Varr-Anthos.

She said it steadily, hands at her sides. Her voice carried to every seat.

"All of them. Every ship. Every cell. Asked first. And the children who come out, I want them asked what they want, and if they want to go home, I want them taken home rowed, and if they do not, I want them kept in the House of Anthos as children of the House, not as cores, with the name of the House, which they have earned more than anyone born to it."

The sound in the four thousand seats wasn't a sound any more. It was something else.

"And I want the stone on Anthos," said the Matriarch, "the one in the reading-hall below the House, where every child of Anthos has been read for nine thousand years, where my daughter Maelis was read and marked E and given to the fold, to ask."

She didn't stop.

"Nobody is in it. I cannot go into it. I am alive." Her voice didn't change. "So until someone can, until someone chooses to, I want to stand beside it. On every reading morning. Every child. Before the stone reads them, I want to ask them myself what they want to become. And if they say they do not want to touch it, I want to be the one who says: then don't."

She looked down at the platform.

"I said yes for my daughter once," said Corvane Varr-Anthos. "Without asking. On the morning. In the box. I will spend the rest of my life asking other people's."

She sat down.

---

He said it back, all of it, exactly, as he'd told her he would, in the Federation's language without the grade, standing on the platform at the bottom of the Assembly so that every seat heard it twice. Once from her, once from someone who'd been listening.

When he finished, nobody moved.

Then from the ninth tier, from a seat burning iron-gray, a man stood up. A Fleet Captain, forty or so, in the gray of the House of Iron, a disc with two gold marks at his throat, dark.

"Haskel Iron," he said, without his grade. His voice was shaking. "Fleet Captain. The Iron Tower." He swallowed. "My brother is in the third array of my ship. I put him there. He was an F. I was an S. I signed the order." He looked down at the platform. "On the ninth night the fog-ringer asked me what I wanted, and I said: to see my brother. And nobody heard it but me and him."

He stood in the ninth tier with his hands shaking.

"I would like it heard," said Haskel Iron. "Twice."

---

They came for nine hours.

Not all of them; not most. Of four thousand seats, Seed counted three hundred and eleven who stood. One at a time, in the Assembly, in front of one another, without their grades, each said what they'd said on the ninth night when a fog-ringer in a stone asked them and only they had heard.

*To see my brother.*

*I do not know. I have never not known anything. I would like it heard that I do not know.*

*To stop.*

*To have been asked. When I was twelve.*

*To be first.*

That too. Eleven times; Seed counted. Eleven Paragons and Fleet Captains of the twenty-nine Houses stood in the Assembly of the Lamplit Worlds and said, in front of everyone, without their grades, steadily, that what they wanted was to be first. And he said it back to each of them, exactly, so it was heard twice.

> **The Collector said that was the easiest want it ever kept,** said Seed. **It's very hard to say out loud in a room. I can see their faces.**
>
> **I think it's the first time any of them have heard it.**

And every time, at the end, he said the same thing to each one: *Thank you. Nobody here has to do anything with it.*

---

At the end of the ninth hour the Speaker of the Assembly stood.

Aurelle Teller, in white, four gold marks on a dark disc at her throat, rising from the seat of white stone at the front of the lowest tier. She hadn't moved for nine hours. She'd sat above the platform with her hands folded and watched three hundred and eleven people stand one at a time and say what they wanted without their grades, and a boy from an orchard in the Shallows say it back to each of them.

She looked down at him.

"You have turned my Assembly into your stone," she said, with a grade; Seed carried it.

Then, slowly, deliberately, as a very old woman works off a ring she's worn so long her finger has grown round it, she said it again. Without.

"You have turned my Assembly into your stone."

"No," said Yuanshu. "It was always a stone. Somebody just came in and sat down."

The Speaker looked at him a long time.

"The twenty-nine Houses ask the Assembly to break the Lens," said Aurelle Teller, "and restore the Ladder, and choose a new First." She didn't look away from him. "The Assembly will decide tomorrow."

She paused.

"The Speaker would like to know what the ungraded one wants. Before the Assembly decides."

---

He stood on the platform at the bottom of the bowl of black stone, under four thousand seats, with the staff in his hand.

He thought of the north wall on the ninth night over Blue Star, when a fog-ringer in a stone had asked eight billion people at once what they wanted, and he'd stood in his old place and not known, for the first time since he was eighteen. And of the step of the groundskeeper's hut at the edge of the War Memorial Cemetery at dusk, when Seed asked him and he'd known. Two wants.

*To go and ask them. And to come home.*

He'd done the first. He was doing it.

"I want to send a mark," said Zhou Yuanshu.

The Speaker's face didn't change.

"Through the Lens. Tomorrow, before the Assembly decides. To every face in the Federation: every disc on every chain round every throat that was cut from the root." He looked up at four thousand seats, four thousand dark discs on fine chains. "Not a question. A mark, the kind the stones used to send. Except this one would say something true."

"What would it say?"

"I don't know yet," said Yuanshu. "My sister's writing it."

---

> **I'm sending it now,** said Seed. **To Xiaoyu, through the Lens. Ichiro's tying it. He says it'll be clumsy because he's excited.**
>
> **Down the net, through the knot in the deep water, down Wen Jia's thread, into our stone. It says:**
>
> **THE ASSEMBLY SITS TOMORROW. EVERY FACE IN THE FEDERATION WAS CUT FROM THE LENS. ICHIRO CAN SEND A MARK TO ALL OF THEM AT ONCE.**
>
> **WHAT SHOULD IT SAY? IT HAS TO BE TRUE. YOURS ARE.**
>
> **— Y.**

And very far away, on a world in the Shallows, in the Awakening Hall on East Ring Road at two in the morning Lingzhou time, a girl of seventeen, nearly eighteen, who'd just asked the Collector what it wanted and been told *tomorrow*, watched letters form in the air over a warm black stone in a hand that wasn't the stone's, and read them. She sat down on the platform floor before it, beside the body of a nun holding a lamp, with a marker in her hand, uncapped.

And for a long time didn't write anything.
