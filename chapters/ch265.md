## Chapter 265: Again

The boy who came off the road first was called Tam, and on the third day he asked to be taken to the stone.

He asked Gao Min. Everyone asked Gao Min. She'd made herself the person the Candle's children asked, as she'd made herself the person the holders asked, by sitting three days at a table at the foot of the War Memorial Cemetery with a ledger and writing down every name and every age-inside and every place they'd been taken from, and never once a grade. When a child tried to give her one she put her pencil down and said in her flat careful voice, *we don't write that here*, and waited until they gave her something else.

Tam had given her his name, and the name of a world, Corraine, one of the fuel orchards on the inner worlds, and the number nineteen, which was how many years he'd been in the wall.

On the third day he came back to her table and said, in the Federation's language, which Seed was teaching Gao Min a word at a time:

> **He says: the stone. The one that asks. Can I see it?**
>
> **On Corraine the stone read him at twelve and said F, and then they took him to the ship. He says the stone was the last thing he saw before the wall. He'd like to see one that asks, before he decides whether he wants to touch it.**

---

They went on the fourth morning.

Not all nine thousand five hundred and fifty-nine. Gao Min said that would frighten the stone, and Wen Jia, when asked, said through the staff that it wouldn't frighten her, but it might frighten the children to see so many of themselves in one place again, all in a line. So they went in groups. Tam's first: forty of them. Most still barefoot, because shoes were strange. In city clothes too big for them. Holding hands, because that was how they'd come down the road.

The Number Nine took them. Wen Hao wouldn't let anyone else. He stopped outside the Ossuary at 7:12 and opened the doors and counted to ten, and forty children of twelve who were nineteen and thirty and forty-four inside climbed onto his bus and sat down very carefully, as people sit who've sat on nothing but the inside of a wall for years. He drove them up East Ring Road in the morning light, slowly, past the trees, and stopped outside the Awakening Hall, opened the doors, and counted to ten again, out loud, so the children could hear.

Tam asked him why. Seed translated.

"My sister's in there," said the driver of the Number Nine. "She asks the kids what they want to be. I count to ten so she knows it's me."

Tam looked at him in the mirror.

> **He says: is your sister a core?**

"She was in a sack," said Wen Hao. "Thirty years. Then a basement. Then a boy's head. Now a stone." He met the boy's eyes in the mirror. "She chose the stone. Nobody put her there."

---

The hall was quiet.

It was never empty now. The holders had stopped needing to hold, but some still came in the mornings and stood in a short line up the aisle with their hands on one another's shoulders, because they'd got used to it. They made room when the forty came in. Nobody told them to.

The three lines of light still hung over the platform. Under them stood the black stone with its crack and its downward-pointing runes and the blue jacket over its shoulder.

The children stopped in the aisle.

He watched them stop: forty twelve-year-olds in clothes too big for them, holding hands at the bottom of the aisle, looking up at a black stone ten meters high. The last thing they'd seen before the walls. Seed said every one of them was shaking, and most didn't know it.

Tam let go of the hand he was holding and walked up the aisle alone.

He stopped at the foot of the platform steps and didn't climb them. He stood and looked: at the runes turned down, at the jacket, at the crack.

> **He says it's the same. The stone on Corraine. The same black, the same runes. He'd know it anywhere.**
>
> **He says it's warm. The one on Corraine was warm too. But the runes were pointing at him. These are pointing down.**

"There's someone in it," said Yuanshu. He'd come up the side aisle and stopped a little way off. "Her name's Wen Jia. She was seventeen when she was taken. She never got to touch one. Now she's in it. She asks."

Tam looked at the runes.

> **He says: can she hear me?**

> ***...yes,*** said Wen Jia through the staff, to the hall. ***...I can hear you. Hello. You're the boy who came off the road first. The bus driver told me. He tells me everything.***

Tam didn't move. Seed said he'd stopped breathing.

> ***...you don't have to touch me. Lots of people don't. There's a woman in a stone on the other side of the world who counts the ones who don't, and she says it's more every month, and she says that's good.***
>
> ***...but if you want to, I'll ask you something. I'll tell you first: whatever you say, I'll open that. Not what I read. What you say. And if you don't know, I'll say come back when you do.***
>
> ***...that's all. That's all I do.***

---

He stood at the foot of the steps. The thirty-nine at the bottom of the aisle didn't move, nor the holders, nor Gao Min on the bottom tier with her ledger.

Then Tam climbed the three steps.

Slowly, as the children from the road walked. He stopped before the stone, under the three lines of light, and looked at it from very close.

And then he said quietly to it, in the Federation's language, what Xiaoyu had said on Awakening Day, which he couldn't have known, because nobody had told him:

> **He says: hello. I'm Tam, from Corraine. I'm going to put my hand on you. I'm telling you first.**

And he did.

---

**[What do you want to become?]**

The letters formed under Xiaoyu's three lines in the hand that wasn't the stone's. Seed had taught Wen Jia the Federation script in a night, and she wrote in that.

Tam looked up at it, and for a very long time said nothing. His hand stayed flat on the stone. The letters hung there.

> **He says on Corraine it said F. It didn't ask. It said F, and then a man in gold came and said the honor, and his mother cried and took the money, and they put him in the ship.**
>
> **He was in the wall nineteen years, in the first array, next to a girl from Halloran who sang. Not out loud; you can't, in the wall. Inside. She sang inside for nineteen years and he listened.**
>
> **He says she's still up there in the Candle. She said yes, to carry it home. She wants to see her mother.**

He stood with his hand on the stone.

> **He says he doesn't know what he wants to become. He was going to be a fisherman on Corraine, before. That's what his father was. He doesn't know if he wants that now. He doesn't know what anything is here.**
>
> **He says he's only ever been one thing.**

**[Then come back when you know. I will be here.]**

Tam looked at the letters a moment.

> **He says: no. Wait.**

The letters waited.

> **He says he knows one thing. He wants to be able to hear the ones in the walls. The way the boy did. The way the thing in his head did. Not to take them out; he doesn't know how. Just to hear them, so somebody does. So nobody's ever in a wall again with nobody listening.**
>
> **He says: is that a becoming?**

The stone was quiet a moment.

> ***...yes,*** said Wen Jia. ***...that's a becoming.***

**[Chosen.]**

**[Talent: — not marked —]**

---

He felt it open, very small: a little, as the old knot said. Not a Law on the tree in the stone. Something the boy had said, which Wen Jia had opened.

> **It's like mine,** said Seed, quietly. **A little. The listening part, not the counting. She's given him a little of what I do: the part that hears the ones nobody hears.**
>
> **I didn't know that could be given.**

Tam took his hand off the stone.

He stood there a moment. Then he turned his head slowly, as if he'd heard something a long way off, and looked up. Not at the letters: past them, at the roof, and past the roof at the sky, and past the sky to the north, to the small white ring and the crinkle beside it, two hundred and twenty meters long, with thirty-two thousand and forty people in its walls burning low in turns, breathing.

His face opened like a door.

> **He says: I can hear her. The girl from Halloran, in the first array. She's singing. She's still singing.**
>
> **She's singing because she's going home.**

---

At the bottom of the aisle the other thirty-nine had let go of one another's hands.

Not all at once. One, then another; a girl, then two boys, then more, walking up the aisle one at a time toward the platform in their too-big clothes, barefoot on the polished floor, to stand in a line at the foot of the steps, one behind another, as the holders stood, each with hands on the shoulders in front.

Not because they had to. Because, a girl told Gao Min afterward through Seed, that was how you stood when you wanted to do something and were frightened. You held on to the one in front.

Gao Min wrote that down in her new ledger, in the column headed *What they said.*

They went up one at a time, all forty. Some touched it and some didn't. Some said *I don't know*, and the stone said *come back when you do*. Some said things nobody had ever said to a stone. *I want to swim. I want to grow things. I want to be a mother. I want to be bright on purpose, just once, for something good, and then stop.* A girl of twelve who was forty-four inside put her hand on it and said through Seed, very clearly: *I want to be graded again, so I can see it's wrong.* The stone was quiet a long time, and then wrote:

**[I do not grade. But I will tell you what you are, if you want. You are someone who came down a road of light and asked.]**

And the girl stood with her hand on the stone and cried and said *yes, that, that's what I wanted*, and the stone wrote **[Chosen]** and nothing else, and opened nothing, because she hadn't asked for anything to be opened. She'd asked to be told.

---

That night in the banking hall Xiaoyu wrote at the top of a new column, on the last of the white plaster round the corner of the vault door:

*THE CANDLE'S CHILDREN. 9,559. CAME DOWN THE ROAD.*

*TAM, FROM CORRAINE. 19 YEARS IN THE WALL. CAME OFF THE ROAD FIRST. WENT UP THE STEPS FIRST. CHOSE: TO HEAR THE ONES IN THE WALLS.*

And under it, smaller, because Gao Min had read it to her out of the ledger and she'd said it had to go on the wall:

*THAT IS HOW YOU STAND WHEN YOU WANT TO DO SOMETHING AND ARE FRIGHTENED. YOU HOLD ON TO THE ONE IN FRONT.*
