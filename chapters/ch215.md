## Chapter 215: The Hut at the Edge of the Cemetery

The vote closed on the thirty-eighth night. The keeper was allowed to stay, and Yuanshu went to the cemetery.

Sixty-six percent. Okafor read it out in the crater, in the rain, to the whole of Blue Star, and then read out the second question, which Cassia Lowell had asked to have added to the ballot under Article Eleven and which had passed by more: *Blue Star does not consent to the collection of the vessel called Seed.* Seventy-four percent. When the Council confirmed it, General Han's hand went up first. Lowell's was second.

> **Seventy-four percent of eight billion people say they don't consent to me being collected,** said Seed. **I don't know what to do with that. I've counted a lot of things. I've never been counted like that before.**

He didn't answer, because he had nothing useful to say either. At dusk he walked out of the Ossuary with the staff, Old Seven a pace behind, and went east across the river and through the old quarter and past the tram yards, to where it had started.

---

The War Memorial Cemetery never changed, and that surprised him every time. The long green slope down to the river, the little gray stones in their rows, the cypresses, the iron railings. Stone No. 7 near the top. The stone gate on the hill above, where Iron Wall Zhao stood facing outward. The stone beside it marked QUIET, over a director who had held a switch in the round chamber to the end.

And by the back fence under the cypresses, the hut.

It had been the groundskeeper's: one brick room, a tin roof, a door that didn't shut. He'd sat on its step at a quarter to three on the first night with a training spear across his knees and the groundskeeper's cat watching him from the roof, waiting for the mist to come down over the stones and not yet knowing what he was going to do. He'd been back a handful of times since. It wasn't the sort of place you went back to often.

Lingyue was sitting on the step.

---

He stopped at the corner of the hut. She didn't get up. She sat on the brick step in her Academy jacket with her grandfather's sword across her knees, looking down over the rows of stones toward the river in the last of the light.

"Xiaoyu told me you'd come here," she said.

"I didn't tell Xiaoyu."

"You didn't have to. She says you always come here when something's too big. After the voyage. After the wall. After the Western Pass. She says you think nobody's noticed." She turned the sword over on her knees. "Everybody's noticed."

He sat down beside her. The step was narrow, so they ended up close whether they meant to or not.

The river went by at the foot of the slope, gray. A tram bell rang somewhere in the old quarter. Old Seven had wandered up to stand by stone No. 7, facing out, the way he sometimes did beside the hole he'd come out of.

"Seventy-four percent," said Lingyue.

"Yes."

"Eight billion people said nobody gets to take Seed." She kept her eyes on the river. "I voted twice. I did it at the Academy and then again at the Ossuary, because Fat Tan didn't recognize me with my hair down."

"That's illegal."

"So arrest me," said Shen Lingyue.

> **She's lying,** said Seed. **She voted once, at the Academy. I checked the Ossuary booth logs. She said it to make you laugh.**
>
> **You didn't laugh. I'm noting that.**

He didn't laugh. He sat on the step of the hut where it had all started, next to the girl who'd stood on the awakening platform ahead of him and not even looked at her own letters, and couldn't think of a single thing to say.

"Grandfather told me," she said, after a while.

His spine went stiff.

"He said he asked you whether I was happy. On the steps of the main hall. He said you told him to ask me, and then answered anyway." Her thumb found the worn place on the hilt. "Tired and frightened and angry about three things at once, and that's what happy looks like on her."

"I did say that."

"It's true." She didn't look up. "He said something else. He said: when the boy asks her the other question, I'd like to be told before the cameras." A pause. "He wouldn't tell me what the other question was. He said I'd know."

---

The light was nearly gone. The cypresses had turned black against a sky shading from gray to dark blue. Across the river, the lamps on the far bank were coming on one at a time.

Of course he had thought about it. On the island, flat on his back with her shoulder against his. On the wall at midnight, the night of the forty days, with her head on his shoulder. On the training ground, with her lying on cracked stone laughing until she cried. He'd thought about it in this hut, and on the jetty at Sandu, and in a field above the sky with six lamps pinning him in place and his whole life going up their beams with her face somewhere in the middle of it.

He'd never asked. Not because he didn't know how to ask; he'd asked fifty thousand dead and two hundred million cousins and a keeper over the world and a dying old woman on a jetty. He didn't know how to ask *her*. Because he had never before been afraid of the answer.

> **You're afraid of the answer,** said Seed. **That's new. Two years in your head and you've been afraid of plenty, but never an answer. Answers were the one thing that never scared you.**
>
> **I'm not going to say anything else.**

---

"Lingyue."

She didn't look round.

"I'm going to ask you something. You don't have to answer. I'll tell you first what I'll do with the answer."

"I know how you ask."

"I know you know." His hands on the staff weren't steady, and he didn't try to make them steady. "If you say yes, I'll go up Mount Qingyun and tell your grandfather before anyone else, before any cameras. Then I'll tell my mother and she'll pretend she didn't see it coming. Xiaoyu will scream. Qin Hao will write it down." He took a breath. "And then I'll stand next to you for as long as you'll have me. Not because anyone asked me to. Because I want to."

Her hands had gone still on the sword.

"If you say no, I'll still stand next to you. On the wall, in the field, wherever. I'll never ask twice. Nothing changes except that I'll know." He looked at the stones going down into the dark. "And if you say you don't know, I'll wait as long as it takes. I'm good at waiting. Monks taught me."

He stopped. He couldn't find the rest. He had never had to say the question part out loud before, to anyone, because it had always been the same question and it had always been the easy part.

"*What do you want,*" he said. "That's how it goes, isn't it."

"No," said Shen Lingyue. "Not this time."

She turned and looked at him. In the last of the light, on the step of a groundskeeper's hut at the edge of a cemetery, her grandfather's sword across her knees and a faint white scar on her lip from the day the sky came down on her and she stood on her own light and held.

"This time ask the real one," she said. "Out loud. The whole thing. I want to hear you say it."

---

He said it.

He wouldn't remember the words afterward. He remembered his voice cracking in the middle. He remembered Old Seven turning his skull up by stone No. 7. He remembered that by then every lamp on the far bank was lit, a long string of small lights along the water.

He remembered that she didn't answer straight away. That she looked at him for so long he had one cold second of thinking he'd misread everything, the way Seed had once misread the water.

Then she hit him on the arm. Hard. The way she had since they were eighteen.

"Yes," she said. "Obviously. You took two *years.*"

She put her grandfather's sword down on the brick step beside her as carefully as if it were made of glass, and put both arms round his neck, and hung on, and didn't say anything else at all.

> **I'm not recording this,** said Seed.
>
> **I'm noting that I'm not recording this.**
>
> **I'm also noting that Old Seven has come down from stone No. 7 and is standing by the cypresses with his back to you, facing out, the way he stands at a door when the people inside need not to be watched. His flame is very bright.**
>
> **Xiaoyu has just texted asking if anything's happened. I haven't answered.**
>
> **She's texted again.**
>
> **And again. This one is just question marks.**

---

They walked back through the old quarter at ten, the long way, along the river, Old Seven a pace behind.

On the bridge she stopped.

"Your grandfather," said Yuanshu.

"Tomorrow. First thing. Before the cameras." She didn't let go of his hand. "He'll pretend to be stern. He'll say three hundred years of Shens have never married a necromancer. Then he'll say he's glad. Then he'll ask what you want, to see what you say."

"What should I say?"

She looked at him, on a bridge in Lingzhou at night with the old quarter's lamps behind her, and beyond the city the long dark of the north where a small white ring hung low over the end of the world, and beyond that, thirty days out and coming, three gray rings that used to be keepers.

"The truth," she said. "You're good at it."

> **Thirty days,** said Seed. **You asked me to always tell you the numbers. Even tonight.**
>
> **And I've had an idea. I'm sorry. I had it while you were asking her and I couldn't stop it. I was watching you be afraid, and I thought: he's asking something he's never asked, and the answer could change what he is.**
>
> **The rings were keepers. Nobody ever asked them anything.**
>
> **I'm a sack. I was made to count and hold and wait to be carried. Somebody asked me, and it changed what I am.**
>
> **I think I know how to ask a ring.**

Lingyue, who couldn't hear any of it but could read his face perfectly well by now, said, "It's had an idea, hasn't it," and when he nodded, she sighed and said, "Fine. Tell me on the way home. And then tomorrow you're telling my grandfather, idea or no idea."
