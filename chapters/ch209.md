## Chapter 209: The Shore

The field was gold again when they got back to it, and the keeper would not stop talking.

They saw it from a long way out. On the eleventh night by Bai Lu's clock, at the leading edge of what was left of the island (a kilometer and a half of glowing ground with fifty thousand dead packed onto it like commuters on the last train), Old Seven raised his skull and looked ahead, and there it was. Not a single lit window across a valley any more. A city's worth of light: a broad soft gold glow so wide and warm that every other light near it looked dim.

> ***...THEY ARE HERE. ALL OF THEM.***
>
> ***...I ASKED THEM THIS MORNING. AND YESTERDAY. AND THE DAY BEFORE. MOST STILL SAY: WE DO NOT KNOW YET. ASK TOMORROW.***
>
> ***...SOME OF THEM ASK ME THINGS. ONE ASKED WHAT I WANTED.***
>
> ***...I DID NOT KNOW WHAT TO SAY. I SAID: TO BE ASKED. SOMETIMES.***
>
> ***...IT SAID IT WOULD.***

The Elder, at the middle of the island with the gold thread running out of her, was smiling. He had never seen her smile and hadn't known her face could do it.

"It has been like this for nine days," she said. "It tells me every column. Every answer. It does not stop." She looked toward the light. "My people made it out of a yes, under a red sun, a very long time ago. We thought we had built something that keeps." Her long hands pressed together. "We did not know it could be happy."

"It sounds like my aunt after a wedding," said Dabao, and the Elder considered this seriously and said she thought that was probably right.

---

They came in through the far door at dawn by Bai Lu's clock.

What was left of the island slid up against the edge of the field and stopped, and the two edges met with a long low noise he felt in his back teeth and closed into one ground again, as if they had never been apart.

> ***...THERE. I HAVE IT BACK. A LITTLE SMALLER.***
>
> ***...IT WILL GROW. PERHAPS.***

The field was full.

He walked out across it on legs that still didn't feel entirely like his, Lingyue beside him and very pointedly not helping. Gold columns everywhere, in every direction, filling the old rings on the ground and the spaces between and the haze at the edges. Not in rows. In clusters, the way people stand in a hall they've just arrived in before they've decided where to sit. Cousins of the world that asks, small and pale and a little like Hush. Things from the walls of the Mouth he had no names for: something like a tree, something like a drift of fine gold dust, something with a great many hands folded neatly in front of it. All of them peering out. Curious.

At the near edge, where four faint rings lay side by side on the ground, the hundred and seventy-five who had stayed when the field was empty stood in a tight cluster, like the first guests at a party when the room suddenly fills up. They turned, all of them at once, to watch the dead of Blue Star go by.

---

The keeper laid the roads at sunrise over Blue Star: six of them, gold, wide enough for nine abreast, down out of the small white ring above the end of the world to every place the dead had come from.

The Western Legion went first, to the Western Pass. He watched from the top of the road as twenty-five thousand dead soldiers marched down out of the sky toward a mountain of graves in the snow, a short broad skeleton at the front with a pick on his shoulder, counting. Sixteen were missing, to the gleaners. Sergeant Ma had said their names at the rim every night of the voyage. He'd say them on the mountain too.

At the bottom, on the first terrace, beside a gray stone in row two hundred and twelve, an old man with one arm stood in a greatcoat with his cap on. When his soldiers came down out of the sky he didn't say anything. He took his cap off and held it against his chest.

---

The road to Sector Nine came down last.

He went down it behind Old Seven, Lingyue beside him, the Spear Corps and the First Legion behind, the living scattered among the dead as they had been on the island. Bai Lu with her bag and the clock. Dabao with his shield. The Mo twins with their one pack. Kwan with three instrument cases and a new list. Qin Hao with his bandaged arms and his notebook. Adjutant Xu with his cane. The Elder, the gold thread running through her into the road.

Liang Fu, with his weight off his right leg.

And in Old Seven's hand, uncurled now, every leg out and every eye open, looking down at Blue Star rising toward them through the morning: Hush.

---

They were all there.

Nobody had organized it. The whole city was on the north wall in the gray dawn, from the river gate to Crossing Six, the way it had been the morning the roads went up. Su Mingli at the top of the stairs. Orlov. General Han in dark green with his cap under his arm. Captain Lin Wen up in the watchtower above Crossing Six. Sister Ning with her candle. Fat Tan, crying without any shame at all. Principal Yue. Hendrik Maas, who had once again driven a bus from the station for reasons nobody had asked him to explain.

At the foot of the road, where it met the Sector Nine battlement: his mother in her wheelchair, blanket over her knees, a microphone in her lap.

And next to the wheelchair, not behind it, in a hoodie two sizes too big and with a black marker uncapped in her hand: Xiaoyu.

She'd been there all night. Her eyes were red and she had ink all over her fingers. She didn't run to him or hit him on the arm. She stayed where she was at the bottom of the road, exactly as she'd said she would, so he'd know which shore was his.

---

Old Seven reached the stone first.

He stepped off the end of the road onto the battlement and stopped in front of Xiaoyu and crouched, though she was seventeen now and nearly Lingyue's height and had called a whole city by name in the dark. He held out his other hand.

Hush looked at the girl with the marker. The girl with the marker looked at Hush. On the wall, three thousand people held their breath.

> ***...you're the small loud one.***

"Yes," said Xiaoyu. Her voice was rough. "I write it on the wall. Every month. Everything you said."

> ***...I know. The keeper told us, in the fire. It said a girl in a city was writing it on a wall in capitals with the seconds underneath.***
>
> ***...I liked that you put the seconds.***

Xiaoyu's chin had started to shake.

Hush uncurled one thin limb. In it, soft at the corners, the red faded almost pink, was the card.

> ***...I said I still had it. Every month. So someone would know I was still here.***
>
> ***...I am here now. I don't need to have it any more.***

It held the card out. Not to Old Seven. To her.

> ***...will you put it on the wall? At the end, where the seconds go?***

Xiaoyu took the queen of hearts between ink-stained fingers as if it might break, and found she couldn't say anything. She nodded, twice. Then she sat down on the stone, very suddenly, in front of a crouching dead soldier, and put her face in her hands and howled.

Hush climbed out of Old Seven's palm onto her knee and sat there, small and uncurled and freezing cold, in the gray dawn on the north wall of Lingzhou, on the knee of a girl who had written down every word it ever said.

---

Yuanshu knelt by his mother's chair.

She didn't speak at first. She looked at him for a while, at the softness still at the edges of his eyes, at the blood dried in the creases of his ears that nobody had had time to wash out, at whatever else had been there since a door at the bottom of the Death Law had stood open for nineteen hours and never quite shut again.

"You're different," said Zhou Wanqing.

"Yes."

"Tell me what you wanted. The whole time. You said you would."

He thought about it. Not quickly; he'd learned that much.

"I wanted them to be asked," he said. "All of them. The ones in the walls, the ones in the columns, the ones in the ground. I didn't want anybody left in the dark without somebody asking what they wanted." He paused. "And I wanted to come home. And I wanted Lingyue to come home. And I wanted to sit on the vault steps and let Xiaoyu shout at me."

"That's a lot of wants."

"I know."

"Good," said his mother. "Keep them. Don't let anything take them. Not up there. Not anywhere."

She picked up the microphone, pressed the button, and said in a quiet, sharp, slightly hoarse voice, out of every speaker along the north wall of Lingzhou:

"They're home."

All along the wall, in the dawn, the city let out its breath. Somewhere near Crossing Six somebody started clapping and then stopped, embarrassed, and then somebody else started, and this time it caught.
