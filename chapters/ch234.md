## Chapter 234: Grading

Gao Min had nine boxes in the boot of her car and wouldn't let anyone else carry them.

She brought them in one at a time at one in the morning, past Old Seven at the bronze door, who turned his skull to watch each one go by, and set them in a row on the long table under the lamp. Gray cardboard, Bureau seals, handwritten labels on the ends in a small square hand that hadn't changed in forty years: *LINGZHOU AWAKENINGS, YEARS 1–5. YEARS 6–10.* All the way to *YEARS 41–42 (INC. Z.Y.)*.

"It's on computer too," she said. "I don't trust the computer."

She took the lids off. Ledgers, hundreds of them, bound in gray cloth. Every page ruled in pencil, every line a name, a date, a Class and a Talent.

Fat Tan, who'd come down the stairs in his slippers to see who was making the noise, looked at the boxes for a while and then went and made tea without being asked, which was how Fat Tan said *this is serious*. Kwan sat down beside Gao Min with the laptop. Xiaoyu perched on the bottom rung of the stepladder with a marker uncapped in her hand, waiting to be told what to write.

Yuanshu sat on the vault steps with the staff, and Seed went up the gold thread to the keeper with the knot.

---

It took four hours.

Not because the cousins were slow. Reading wasn't answering. They didn't have to decide anything or want anything or say *ask again tomorrow*; they only had to look at a shape and say what it meant. They'd burned low for ten million years in the Low Fire and then in the Mouth, and they knew the shapes that fire made from the inside better than anything in the Shallows.

But the knot was very old. Older than the REQUEST. Older, the keeper said, coming down the thread at half past three in its rough new voice, than anything the cousins had ever read.

> ***...THEY SAY IT IS NOT WRITTEN FOR A HARVEST. NOT LIKE THE SACK. NOT LIKE THE RINGS.***
>
> ***...THEY SAY IT IS WRITTEN FOR AN ORCHARD.***

At the long table Gao Min stopped turning a page.

> **I'll read it to you the way they read it to me,** said Seed. **All the way through. Then I'll stop.**

> **READ WHAT RIPENS.**
>
> **A MIND IS RIPE WHEN IT HAS GROWN ITS OWN SHAPE AND NOT YET SET. READ IT THEN. NOT BEFORE. NOT AFTER.**
>
> **FIND WHICH WAY IT WILL BURN. OPEN IT THAT WAY. A LITTLE. THAT IT BURN BRIGHTER THAN IT WOULD.**
>
> **MARK HOW BRIGHT.**
>
> **SEND THE MARK UP THE THREAD.**
>
> **WHAT IS OPENED WILL FEED. WHAT FEEDS WILL BURN. WHAT BURNS WILL BRIGHTEN. TEND THE ORCHARD BY THIS.**
>
> **WHEN THE ORCHARD IS BRIGHT, THE PICKER COMES.**

Nobody spoke. Fat Tan stood in the doorway of the back room holding a tray of tea and didn't come in.

Gao Min put a finger on the open ledger as if to keep her place. "Read it again. The part about marking."

> **MARK HOW BRIGHT.**

"That's a Talent," she said. "F to SSS. That's the mark."

---

She had a graph.

She'd made it twenty years ago for a paper nobody read, and made it again by hand every year since on squared paper, and kept it folded in the lid of the box marked *YEARS 41–42*. She unfolded it on the table: six sheets taped end to end, a pencil line running left to right across forty-two years.

"Mean Talent grade. Lingzhou, every awakening, every year. Weighted. F is one, SSS is seven."

The line went up. Not steeply and not in jumps. Slowly, year after year, like the pencil marks parents make on a kitchen doorframe, a hair higher every Awakening Day than the one before. In year one it started below three. In year forty-two it was above four.

"Everybody knows Talents are going up," said Gao Min. "It's in the papers every summer. The Bureau says it's better nutrition. Better training. Better schools. Cultivation culture." She tapped the line. "I never believed it. The curve's too smooth. Nutrition isn't smooth. Schools aren't smooth. There was a famine in year nineteen and the line didn't even notice." She ran her fingernail along it. "Something's been turning it up a little every year. Like a dial."

> **Like an orchard,** said Seed. **Tended a little more every season.**
>
> **Every Class is a Law opened a little. Every level is a kill. Every kill makes the Law burn brighter in whoever made it. *What is opened will feed. What feeds will burn. What burns will brighten.* That's what levelling is.**

Yuanshu sat on the vault steps and didn't move.

*Every kill.*

He had more than three thousand of them in the ledger. Wolves in the first rift. Beasts on the north wall in the Black Tide. Walkers. A Wolf King. Every one had come up through the Death Law into him and made something brighter. He'd felt it every time: a warmth, a level, something opening a little wider.

He'd thought it was his.

---

"So SSS means *eat first*," said Xiaoyu.

She said it from the stepladder into the quiet, fast, the way she said things the instant she understood them. Then she went white and clapped a hand over her mouth and looked at the stairs.

Lingyue was on the stairs.

She'd come down without anyone hearing, in a gray sweater and bare feet, using her grandfather's sword as a cane, the scabbard tip clicking on each step. She'd stopped halfway with one hand on the rail and was looking down at the graph and the ledgers and the small square woman in the Bureau coat.

"Lingyue—" said Xiaoyu.

"No, you're right," said Shen Lingyue. "That's what it means."

She came the rest of the way down. Click. Click. To the table. Gao Min, without being asked, turned the ledger back two years to the right summer and the right morning and put her finger on a line in her own square hand.

*SHEN LINGYUE. LIGHT SWORDSWOMAN. SSS. DAWNBREAKER.*

Lingyue looked at it a long while.

"First SSS in Lingzhou in eleven years," she said. "Front page of every paper. My grandfather had it framed. It's in the hall of the Shen house, in gold." She nearly laughed. "Dawnbreaker. I thought it was a name. It's a label on a box in a shop." She looked at the line. "Best fruit in the orchard. Pick me first."

"Lingyue—"

"Don't," she said, without looking at Yuanshu. "I worked it out on the stairs. I'm not upset. I'm—" Her knuckles had gone white on the sword. "On the wall, when I burned, it was the best thing I ever felt. I told you that. I told you I understood the Sixth." She put her finger on the ledger beside Gao Min's. "It knew. When I was eighteen. It read me and opened me that way on purpose, a little further than I'd have gone, so that one day I'd stand on a wall and want to burn like that. And it wrote down how much I'd want it, and it sent it up."

Nobody said anything.

"It made me like it," said Shen Lingyue quietly. "Being bright. It made me so I'd like it."

---

Gao Min didn't look away and didn't touch her. She wasn't that kind of woman and Lingyue wasn't that kind of girl, and both of them knew it. But she took the pencil from the spine of her notebook, and in the margin beside the line that said *SSS* she wrote, lightly enough to be rubbed out: *Mark of the Stele. Not a measurement of the person.*

"I'm going to do that next to every line," she said. "Forty-two years. It'll take me the rest of my life and I don't care." She put the pencil back. "I'd like to start with you. If that's all right."

Lingyue looked at the small gray words in the margin.

"Yes," she said. "Start with me."

Fat Tan came in at last and put a cup of tea by her hand, and then, after some thought, a second one by Gao Min's, and a plate of sesame biscuits in the middle of the forty-two years where everyone could reach.

---

> **There's one more part,** said Seed. **Everyone can hear. But I'd like to say it to Yuanshu.**

He knew already. He'd known since the hall, since the thread going up out of the crown of the tree in the stone. He'd sat on the vault steps four hours knowing.

*Go on.*

> **SEND THE MARK UP THE THREAD.**
>
> **On the first morning you put your hand on the Stele and it read you. And it read me. A counting-vessel, a harvest tool, loose in a boy. Something it had no mark for. So it went black, all eleven, because they're one tree, and all eleven sent up what they had. Not a mark. They didn't have one. They sent what they saw.**
>
> **A vessel. Loose. In a mind. On Blue Star.**
>
> **I think that's how the scout knew where to look. I think that's why the REQUEST says IT IS THE CAUSE. The rings, Kirovsk, the reader, the tree: it came because of the first morning.**
>
> **Not because of you. Because of me. Because your mother built me in a basement out of something she didn't know was a harvest tool, and put me in her son, and I woke when he touched the stone.**
>
> **It's mine. I want you to know I know that. I've known since the hall.**

He sat on the steps a long time.

Then Old Seven left the bronze door.

He came as he always came, slowly, with his spear, across the marble between the boxes and the people, past the long table and the graph, and stopped in front of the boy on the step. He didn't crouch. He looked at the place a little above Yuanshu's eyes.

> **He says the Black Tide was thirty-three years ago,** said Seed. **The scout came down in the Black Tide. Specimen Zero. It marked the city and said something would come back for what it marked. That was before the boy was born. And the Steles fell forty-two years ago, before the Black Tide.**
>
> **He says I'm a scout and I know how this works. A scout doesn't bring the enemy. The enemy's already coming. The scout is the first one to see it. I saw it, on the first morning. I looked back. Nothing had ever looked back before.**
>
> **He says that isn't a cause. It's a report.**

Seed was quiet for a while.

> **He says that's an order. I asked him. He says yes.**

---

At four in the morning Gao Min folded the graph along its old creases, put it back in the lid of the box, and sat with her hands flat on the table.

"Thirty-two days," she said.

Nobody needed telling of what.

"Eleven halls. Everyone on Blue Star who turns eighteen this year. They'll line up alphabetically and put their hands on the stone, and it'll read them and open them a little further than they'd have gone and mark how bright and send it up." She looked down the table at the ledgers, at the woman with the sword, at the boy on the step. "And we'll print it on their identity cards. And schools will take them or not by it. And people will marry them or not by it. And in forty years someone like me will draw a line on a graph and it'll be a hair higher." She paused. "Or we don't. And they don't awaken. And a beast comes out of a rift and there's nobody with a Class to stand in front of it."

She got up, with the noise people her age make getting up.

"That's not a question for the Bureau," said Gao Min. "That's not even a question for Concordia." She looked at Yuanshu. "It's your article. The one where one answer acts for everybody. Eleven, twelve, whatever it is."

"Eleven."

"Then ask them. All of them. And tell them first: the knot, the thread, the graph, the orchard. Everything." She picked up the first of the nine boxes. "I'll go on the television with you. I've got forty-two years of tables. People believe tables."

From the stepladder, marker in hand, Xiaoyu said: "What do I write?"

They all looked at her.

"On the wall," said his sister. "Somebody has to write it down. What do I write?"

Yuanshu looked at the long white wall. ARTICLE TWELVE, the red *no*, *is this what they did to us*, the branch asked every morning with no answer yet.

"Write what it said," he said. "The knot. All of it, exactly. Big."

She climbed the ladder and wrote it at the top of a new column in square black capitals, every line from READ WHAT RIPENS to the end. Under the last line she stopped with the marker in the air.

Then she added one more, smaller. Not the knot's. Hers.

*NOT YET.*
