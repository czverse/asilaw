## Chapter 290: This Is Not Your Face

She wrote eleven things and crossed out all of them.

She told him afterward, not in a mark but in person, months later, on the vault steps with the marker in her hand, because some things weren't for marks. She'd sat on the platform floor of the Awakening Hall in front of the warm stone from two in the morning until a quarter to five with a sheet of ledger paper on her knee and the body of a twelve-hundred-year-old nun beside her holding a lamp, and written eleven things, and crossed every one out.

*YOU ARE NOT YOUR GRADE.* Crossed out. It was true, she said, but the kind of true you argue with.

*THE LADDER IS GONE.* Crossed out. True, but not hers to say.

*WHAT DO YOU WANT?* Crossed out. Ichiro had already asked that, on the ninth night. Everyone.

*SOMEONE CAN HEAR YOU.* Crossed out. That was Seri's, and only true for people in walls.

Seven more. Crossed out.

At a quarter to five Wen Jia spoke from the knot. Not through a staff; the staff was on Anthos with her brother. In letters, in the air over the stone, in the hand that wasn't the stone's, small, so only a girl sitting on the platform floor could read them.

**[You're doing the face.]**

Xiaoyu looked up.

**[Your brother's face. The step face. Six hours.]**

**[Your mother's coming. I told the bus driver. He's bringing her.]**

---

Zhou Wanqing came into the Awakening Hall at five in the morning in her wheelchair, pushed by the driver of the Number Nine, who'd gone to the Ossuary in the dark unasked and knocked on the bronze door and told Fat Tan *the stone says the girl needs her mother*, and Fat Tan hadn't argued.

His mother said nothing for a while. She sat at the foot of the platform steps and looked at her daughter on the floor in front of the stone with eleven things crossed out on a sheet of ledger paper.

"What's it for?" she said at last.

"Every face in the Federation," said Xiaoyu. "The discs. Round everybody's throat. They were all cut from the Lens. Ichiro can send a mark to all of them at once. Billions. My brother says it has to be true. Mine are." Her voice cracked a little. "I don't know what to say to billions of people. I've only ever written names."

Her mother looked at her.

"What did you want to be told," said Zhou Wanqing, "when you were fifteen? The year before the stone. When you couldn't sleep for fear it would say *Scribe, Talent D*, and everyone would go *oh*."

Xiaoyu didn't answer.

"You told me. At breakfast, eleven months ago. You said: what if I put my hand on it and it says what I am, forever, because a rock said so." She folded her thin hands on the blanket. "What did you want someone to tell you?"

Xiaoyu sat on the platform floor with the marker in her hand.

"That it wasn't me," she said slowly. "Whatever it said. That it was just a rock."

"And?"

"And that I wasn't anything yet." She looked at the paper. "Until I said."

Her mother was quiet a moment.

"I said that once," said Zhou Wanqing. "On a slope in the cemetery, to a boy who came down a road of light and asked if being an F was all right here."

"I know," said Xiaoyu. "I wrote it down."

---

She wrote it at ten past five.

Not on the paper: on the platform floor before the warm black stone with the blue jacket on its shoulder and its runes pointing down, in square capitals, as she'd written THE HOUSE IS EMPTY and SOMEONE CAN HEAR YOU SINGING. Big. So it was written. So nobody could say afterward it wasn't there.

Two lines.

*THIS IS NOT YOUR FACE.*

*NOBODY IS ANYTHING UNTIL THEY SAY WHAT THEY WANT TO BE.*

She sat back on her heels and looked at it.

Then under it, smaller, because she said a mark should say who'd sent it, as Iselde's had and the house-is-empty one hadn't, which had always bothered her:

*— ZHOU XIAOYU. SCRIBE. NOT MARKED. WHO WAS THERE.*

---

**[That's it,]** wrote Wen Jia, in the air over the stone, and then again: **[That's it. That's the one.]**

> ***...I'm going to tie it now. Properly, the way the small one with the legs showed me.***
>
> ***...it's going a long way. Farther than anything I've ever sent. Up my thread into the net, through the knot in the deep water, down into the Lens. And then out of the Lens into everything it was ever cut into. Billions.***
>
> ***...I'm a bit scared.***

"Me too," said Xiaoyu.

> ***...good,*** said the girl in the stone. ***...you're supposed to be. That means it's real.***

She tied it, and sent it.

---

On Anthos it was dawn.

He was on the flagship's observation deck at the window, with the burning world below and the bowl of black stone on the terminator, open to the sky, where the Assembly would sit in four hours. He hadn't slept. Lingyue was asleep against the window with Xiaodeng in the sling. Old Seven was in the doorway. In the bow of the ship, in a disc of black stone the width of a courtyard, a lamp burned and a fog-ringer waited.

> **It's coming,** said Seed. **I can feel it in the Lens. Ichiro has it. He says: oh. He says: oh, she's good. She's better than me.**
>
> **He's going to send it. He's asking you first.**

*Yes,* he thought. *Send it.*

> **He says: right then. Everybody. Look at your face.**

---

He didn't feel it go; it was too big. It went out of the Lens as light leaves a lamp, not travelling, just there everywhere at once: into every disc of black stone ever cut from the root, on every fine chain round every throat, on eight thousand five hundred and eighty-eight worlds.

He saw some of it. Seed showed him what it could, through the Lens and the faces, as it had once seen through the cells of the Candle. Glimpses: a thousand, a million, too many to hold, so it held a few and let the rest go by.

A Paragon of the House of Teller on a terrace above a burning city, hand at her throat, looking down at the black disc in her palm, in which for the first time since the ninth night light was moving. Not the old light, not like water. Two lines in square capitals that weren't her language and that she could read anyway.

A C in the yards at Anthos, a man of fifty with grease on his hands, standing in a corridor with his face in his palm, reading it; then lifting the chain off over his head and setting the disc on a shelf, carefully, as you set down something you might want later, and walking away from it.

A girl of eleven on a world Seed had no name for, in a reading-hall, the day before her reading, crying, holding up the small blank disc her parents had bought for the morning, the one that would be cut from the stone and marked tomorrow, which had something written in it now that nobody had cut there.

A docker on Corraine. A Fleet Captain of the House of Iron alone in the ninth tier of the empty Assembly at dawn, a disc with two gold marks in his hand and his shoulders shaking.

And a woman of sixty-one in deep red, standing beside a black stone in a reading-hall below the House of Anthos, where she'd come at dawn because tomorrow was a reading morning and she'd said in the Assembly she'd stand there. Her daughter beside her, twelve in the body and twenty-three inside, holding her hand. Both looking down at the discs in their palms: the Matriarch's with its three gold marks; Maelis's, which had been Iselde's, crossed out before the Assembly, given to her sister, kept in a box.

Both saying the same two lines. True.

*THIS IS NOT YOUR FACE.*

*NOBODY IS ANYTHING UNTIL THEY SAY WHAT THEY WANT TO BE.*

---

> **Yuanshu,** said Seed, **it wasn't a question. Ichiro asked them on the ninth night; this wasn't that. This was a mark, the kind the stones used to send. Except it didn't say how bright. It didn't say F or E or SSS, or where on the Ladder.**
>
> **It said the thing at their throat wasn't them. And it was true. When they read it, they knew it, the way the one who picks knew the house was empty.**
>
> **I'm counting. I can't count it.**
>
> **I think nine thousand years of faces have just found out they aren't faces.**

---

The Assembly of the Lamplit Worlds sat at nine.

He walked into the bowl of black stone with the others: Old Seven one-armed at the front, the Third Battalion in a line, Lingyue with the baby and the sword and the lamp, Tam and Seri, Bai Lu, Iselde, the First of the Ladder in a C's gray coat. Down the aisle to the platform at the bottom, the place where you stood to be told what you were.

The four thousand seats were full, and not one of them was burning.

He stopped in the aisle.

Four thousand seats that yesterday had every one been lit in the colors of its House or world. Dark. Not switched off, not hidden: turned down, as a lamp is turned down by someone who's decided they don't need it all the way up to be seen. Four thousand people sitting in the bowl of black stone in the morning light that fell through its open top, in the colors they'd been born to, not burning.

And at four thousand throats, on four thousand fine chains, four thousand discs of black stone with light moving in them. Two lines.

Some of the chains weren't round throats any more. Some were in hands. Some lay on the seats beside their owners. Some weren't there at all.

---

The Speaker stood.

Aurelle Teller, in white, at the front of the lowest tier, in her seat of white stone. Very old. At her throat on its fine chain, a disc with four gold marks and light in it, moving.

She didn't sit down again. She stood above the platform and looked down at the boy at the foot of the aisle and the man in the gray coat beside him.

"The twenty-nine Houses asked the Assembly to break the Lens, restore the Ladder, and choose a new First," she said, without a grade. "The Assembly will now decide."

She looked up at four thousand seats, turned down.

"The Speaker is not going to tell you what you are," said Aurelle Teller. "A girl on an orchard in the Shallows who is not marked says nobody is anything until they say what they want to be. The Speaker read it in her face this morning, and it was true." She paused. "So the Speaker is going to ask."

She turned to the first tier.

"House of Anthos. What do you want?"

And in the fourth tier Corvane Varr-Anthos stood up in deep red, her daughter's crossed-out disc in one hand and her own in the other, and said so every seat could hear:

"Ask every child first."

---

It took eleven hours.

Not a vote. The Assembly had never voted; it decreed, by precedence, in the order of the Ladder. Today there was no order and no Ladder. There was a Speaker of great age standing in a bowl of black stone without her grade, asking every House and every world, one at a time, in no order but the order they happened to be sitting in, what it wanted.

And every one answered, out loud, in front of the others.

He said none of them back; nobody asked him to. He stood on the platform with the staff in his hand and listened, as a fog-ringer in a stone had told him listening was half of talking.

Three thousand six hundred and twelve said *ask every child first*, or something that meant it. *Open the arrays. Let the stones ask. Let the cores out. Let them go home rowed.*

Three hundred and sixty-one said *I don't know*, and the Speaker said *then the Assembly will ask you again tomorrow*, and wrote it down.

Twenty-seven said *restore the Ladder*.

---

At eight in the evening Aurelle Teller sat down for the first time in eleven hours, slowly, as old people sit when their knees decide for them.

"The Assembly has been asked," she said. "The Assembly has answered."

She looked down at the platform.

"The Lens will not be broken. There are two people in it. They were asked, and they said yes. If anyone wants them out, they may ask them.

"Every child on every world of the Federation will be asked what they want to become before any stone reads them. Until someone chooses to sit in each stone and ask, a living person chosen by that world will stand beside it and ask first.

"The arrays of every House that answered will be opened. Asked first. Rowed home.

"And there will be no First."

She paused.

"The Speaker would like it noted that she has been Speaker of the Assembly for a hundred and nine years and has never once been asked what she wants."

The bowl of black stone was silent.

"The Speaker would like to be asked," said the oldest Paragon of the Lamplit Worlds. "Tomorrow."

---

The twenty-seven didn't stay.

They stood when she finished, all at once, in the tiers where the twenty-nine Houses sat: eleven Paragons and sixteen Fleet Captains, the ones who'd said in front of everyone, yesterday and again today, that what they wanted was to be first. They stood in the bowl of black stone with their faces at their throats, lit, two lines in each, true, which they'd read at dawn and couldn't unread.

And they walked out. Not in a line, not holding one another's shoulders. One at a time, up the aisles, in their colors, burning, the only ones in the Assembly still burning, out through the doors at the top of the bowl into the Anthos evening.

> **They're going to their ships,** said Seed. **Twenty-seven of the twenty-nine. The C's on those ships are talking. Tam can hear them.**
>
> **They're going to the Lens.**

At the front of the platform Old Seven turned his skull. Not toward the doors: up, through the bowl's open top, at the sky over Anthos, where a cathedral that had been the Severin flagship hung in orbit with a disc of black stone in its bow, and a lamp in the disc, and a fog-ringer.

> **He says they're going to try to break it,** said Seed. **They read the mark. They know it's true. They can't unread it. So they're going to try to break the thing it came through.**
>
> **He says that's what you do when you can't unread something. You try to break the stone.**
