Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 244Stakes

czverse11 min read

By the third morning Gao Min had a chart on the wall of the banking hall, and nobody liked looking at it.

Eleven lines, one for each stone, drawn by hand on squared paper taped together, as she drew everything. She pinned it beside the long white wall where Xiaoyu could see it from the stepladder, and every morning at nine, when the Bureau offices in ten other cities rang with their figures, she added a pencil point to each line and joined it to the last.

Port Halvard: four centimeters the first night. Seven the second. Eleven the third.

Novaya Zarya: three, five, eight.

Kesse, Port Abiri, Yamato, the rest: all going up. Not together, not at the same rate, but all of them, every night, as the Talent line on her old graph had gone up a hair a year. Except that this wasn't years.

Lingzhou: two, three, five.

"It's accelerating," said Gao Min, to the chart, pencil in hand. "Not linearly. Every night it's pulling harder than the night before." She drew the point. "At this rate Port Halvard comes out of the ground in nineteen days."

"And Lingzhou?"

"Thirty-one." She put the pencil behind her ear. "And the thing itself gets here in fifty-seven."

Nobody needed it spelled out. The stones would be out of the ground and up the threads long before whatever was pulling them arrived, with eleven people from the bottom of a well sitting inside them.

Roman Sterling went home that morning.

He didn't fly. It wasn't an emergency until it was, he said, and when it was he'd fly and Concordia could fine him. He took the train to the coast with his canvas bag and his raincoat buttoned to the throat, but before he went he came down to the vault steps where Yuanshu sat with the staff.

"If you use the twelfth again, ring me. I'll say it on the radio."

"I will."

"If you don't use it," said the King of Force, "ring me anyway. I'd like to hear what you did instead." He looked across at the chart, at the line marked PORT HALVARD climbing faster than the rest. "That one's mine. Callum Ward was from my city. His mother lives four streets from the hall. I'm going to go and stand next to it."

At the bronze door Old Seven stood aside for him without being asked.

They argued for two days about cutting the threads.

Qin Hao proposed it on the first afternoon at the long table, his notebook open and his healed pink hands flat on either side of it, in the voice he used when he'd thought about something a long time and decided he was right.

"My fire burns what binds. That's what it does. Fat Tan's ropes. The pressing on the branch. The threads at Kirovsk." He nodded at the diagram on Kwan's laptop: eleven pale lines going up off the stone. "It's pulling them up by the threads. So burn the threads. I'll do Lingzhou myself. I'll teach Orlov's people. Su Mingli can fold me to the rest." He shut the notebook. "No thread, no pull. The stones stay in the ground."

From the stairs Lingyue said: "Yes."

He looked at her. She was looking at the chart.

"It's the thing that works," she said. "Somebody has to say it, and it's always me. Fine."

I've run it, said Seed. It works, in most runs. The threads burn, the stones stop rising, the pulling stops.

And then the one who picks stands in the dark at the end of eleven cut threads and can't see anything, because it only sees bright and the stones were the only bright it had. And then it keeps walking.

It knows the way now. It's walked most of it. It doesn't need the thread to find Blue Star. It needs the thread to see Blue Star. Without it, it comes here blind.

"So it's blind," said Qin Hao. "Good."

I don't know if that's good. Something very large, with many hands, that can't see, walking into a world it came to look at.

It'll put its hands down to feel what's there.

Wen Jia settled it.

Not in the banking hall: in the Awakening Hall at one in the morning on the second night, after the Collector had come and gone, warm and patient, and had said to Xiaoyu when she asked what it wanted: I looked further. There is a shape in the empty place. I do not know what it is yet. Ask me again tomorrow. After Xiaoyu had written it on the back of Gao Min's hand. After the hall had gone quiet.

Yuanshu asked her through the staff from the foot of the steps, with the warm stone in front of him and the blue jacket over its shoulder, and the floor round its base cracked now in a line you could fit a fingernail into.

If we burn the threads, what happens to you?

...we stop being able to hear each other.

It was a while before she said more.

...the threads aren't only what it pulls on. They're how we talk. All eleven. They go up and they join somewhere in the dark, and that's how I know the old man at Port Abiri is holding, and the girl in Yamato, and the one who counts the no's. That's how we went in, the night you put the captain's hand on me. Sideways, along them.

...if you burn them we're eleven people in eleven rocks who can't hear each other any more.

...I was in a sack for thirty years where I couldn't hear anyone. I can do it. I'm just telling you.

The hall was quiet.

...and the other thing. It's pulling because it can't see. If you cut the thread, it still can't see, so it'll come down here to look instead of up the thread.

...I'd rather it pulled on me than walked into the hall.

In the morning Qin Hao closed his notebook.

"Fine. Not the threads." He didn't look happy about it. "Then what?"

Nobody answered.

They'd been at the long table since six. Kwan with three diagrams. Gao Min with the chart. Su Mingli, who'd come in at seven with her arms folded to say she could hold one stone down with gravity for about four hours before she needed a day to recover, and there were eleven stones and fifty-seven days, and would somebody please do the arithmetic for her because she was too tired. Old Seven at the bronze door. Lingyue on the stairs with the flame in her hand, peach-sized now, which she kept looking at as if it belonged to somebody else.

And at the end of the table Xiaoyu, with Shi Lei, who'd come at eight carrying the plain new steel spear the Defense Corps had issued him on Awakening Day, which he hadn't put down since. He'd sat without being asked and hadn't said a word for three hours.

He said one now.

"It opens what you say."

They all looked at him. He went red, as he did, and held on to his spear.

"The stone. It used to read you and open what it found. Now it asks, and it opens what you say. That's what happened to me. I said what I wanted and it opened that." He looked at Xiaoyu. "It opened Scribe in her because she said Scribe. Seed said it's not on the tree. It made it up because she said it." He swallowed. "So what if somebody says they want to hold it down?"

The table went silent.

Say that again, said Seed.

"What if somebody puts their hand on it," said Shi Lei slowly, "and it asks what do you want to become, and they say: someone who holds you down. So you stay." He looked at the chart. "And it opens that, in them. And they hold it."

I've never run that. I'm running it now.

Seed went quiet a long time. Yuanshu could feel the hum of it above his eyes, like a city at night with every light on.

The yeses inside can't hold the stones down, because they're in them. You can't push down a chair you're sitting in.

But somebody standing outside with a hand on it, who's asked to be opened toward holding... The stone opens a little of what you say. That's what the old knot said: OPEN IT THAT WAY, A LITTLE. Wen Jia took the grading out. She didn't take the opening out.

A little holding, in one person, with a hand on a stone. It's not much. It's like one dead soldier with one thread of pale fire against a branch the size of a mountain range.

But there were fifty thousand of them.

"How many people to hold one stone?" said Gao Min.

I don't know. Nobody's ever been opened toward holding. I don't know how much holding there is in one person. But the pull grows every night, so whatever the number is tonight, it'll be bigger tomorrow.

Su Mingli unfolded her arms.

"Then test it," said the King of Gravity. "Tonight, before anybody puts it on television." She looked at the boy with the spear. "You first. It was your idea."

They went at midnight, before the Collector was due.

Shi Lei climbed the platform with his spear in one hand and stopped in front of the warm black stone. The crack round its base was wider than it had been that morning. Gao Min had a laser level on a tripod at the foot of the steps, trained on a pencil mark she'd made on the stone's face at six, and a notebook open on her knee.

"Hello," said Shi Lei. "It's me again. Shi Lei. I'm going to touch you."

He put his hand on it.

[What do you want to become?]

The letters formed under Xiaoyu's three lines, which were still hanging there.

"Someone who holds you down," said Shi Lei. "So you stay. So it can't take you."

Nothing happened. Then, slowly, in the hand that wasn't the Stele's:

[That is not a becoming. That is a doing. Do you want to be it, or do you want to do it?]

Shi Lei stared at it and went red.

...sorry, said Wen Jia through the staff, a little embarrassed. ...it's the stone. It's very fussy about grammar. I'm still working on it.

At the foot of the steps Gao Min made a noise that in anybody else would have been a laugh.

Shi Lei looked at his hand on the stone, at the spear in his other hand, at the front row, where his father wasn't sitting tonight because his father was on nights at the coal yard, because somebody had to be.

"Be it," he said. "I want to be someone who holds things down. So they stay. So nobody takes them." He kept his hand where it was. "That's what I wanted for the coal yard anyway. I just didn't have the word."

[Chosen.]

Everybody felt it from the foot of the steps.

Not light: weight. For a moment all the air in the Awakening Hall grew a little heavier at once, and then settled. The boy on the platform went still and set his feet, as you set them in spear forms at five in the morning before the first thrust, and leaned. Not on the stone. Into it. Down.

There, said Seed. I can see it. Very small. A little holding opened in him, going down his arm into the stone, down the stone into the floor.

Gao Min was watching the laser.

For a while she said nothing. Then she wrote a figure in pencil, checked the laser, and wrote it again.

"Point eight of a millimeter," she said. "Down." She looked up at the boy. "It went down."

By three in the morning there were forty-one.

Nobody called them. Xiaoyu sent one message at a quarter past twelve to her year, the ones who'd awakened with her and the ones who'd said I don't know and the ones who'd said not this year: Shi Lei held the stone down. It asks you what you want. If you want to hold it, come. And they came, on bicycles and on foot and in pajamas, with parents in coats behind them. Forty-one of them, in through the side door with Gao Min's keys and up the three steps one at a time.

Some had been opened already on Awakening Day toward something else: healer, flier, something that makes things grow. The stone asked them anyway. They said also this, and it opened that too, a little.

Some had said I don't know. They knew now.

And a thin girl with glasses, who'd walked up the steps on Awakening Day and down again without touching it and said not this year, went up and put her hand on it and said, when it asked: "I still don't want to be anything. But I want you to stay." The stone was quiet a long moment. Then it wrote, in the hand that wasn't its own:

[That is allowed.]

And she held it.

At ten to one the Collector came, warm and patient, and rested on the hall: on forty-one children with their hands on a black stone, and on Xiaoyu at the foot of the steps. She asked it what it wanted, and it was quiet a long while, looking at the children.

...I am still looking.

...but I would like to watch this. If that is allowed.

"It's allowed," said Xiaoyu. "Watching's free."

At three Gao Min checked the laser.

"Lingzhou. Net change since midnight: zero." She wrote it down, looked at it, crossed it out, and wrote it again, bigger. "It stopped."

At 5:40 the Number Nine left the depot on East Ring Road.

It had no number in the window. Wen Hao had taken it out and put up instead a sheet of cardboard lettered in thick black marker, in square capitals that weren't Xiaoyu's but were trying to be:

TO THE HALL. FREE.

It stopped at every stop and was full by the second.

At 5:52 outside the Awakening Hall both doors opened with a hiss, and Wen Hao counted to ten out loud, and then didn't shut them. He sat with his steady hands on the wheel while forty people got off his bus and climbed the steps of the hall in the gray light, a busload of them, a cleaner and two nurses and a man from the fish market and a schoolteacher and a grandmother with a stick, to put their hands on a black stone with a blue jacket on its shoulder and tell it they wanted it to stay.

On the radio at Kwan's belt, from Port Halvard, a heavy accented voice, breathless, as if its owner had been walking fast:

"This is Sterling. I'm at my stone. There's a queue round the block. There's a woman at the front who says she's Callum Ward's mother." A pause. "She says she wants to hold it down so the thing that took her son can't take anything else. She asked me if she was allowed." Another pause. "I'm a Force King. I've been deciding things for people for fifty years. I told her to ask the stone."

A last pause.

"I'm putting that on the record."
