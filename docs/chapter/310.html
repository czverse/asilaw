Book 5: The Empty House

Chapter 310Six Hundred and Eleven

czverse11 min read

The first thing the House of Marrow did was burn someone.

Xiaoyu had known it would. Tam had said so at the long table and she'd believed him, and she'd spent five days telling herself that knowing a thing was coming was the same as being ready for it.

It wasn't. They held the cell up in the light at the rim of the field, out of the bow of the biggest ship, the way you'd hold a lantern up at the end of a jetty. A small dark crystal the size of a coffin, cut from a stone on some world she'd never heard of. She couldn't see who was in it. Tam could.

"A girl," he said. He stood beside her in the middle of the keeper's field with his hands at his sides and his face white. "From Dunmere. F. Her name's Ilse. She's been in the wall nine years. She doesn't know where she is. She thinks it's the reading again."

Scribe, said the voice from the ship, in the grammar of the Ladder. Write: THIS IS YOUR FACE. On the stone we give you. Or this one goes out.

"Keeper," said Xiaoyu. "Roads."

...LAYING.

Then she goes out, said the voice. And the cell in the light at the rim of the field went white all at once from the inside, as a bulb flares in the second before it blows, and then dark.

Tam made a sound she'd remember for the rest of her life.

She didn't look away; she'd decided that too. She stood in the middle of the field and watched the cell go dark and said aloud, so that it was true, so that somebody had said it:

"Ilse. From Dunmere."

Then the roads came down. Six hundred and eleven of them.

She hadn't been able to picture it, and now she didn't have to. Out of the floor of the keeper's field all round her, out of the warm faint white: gold. Thin, each one no wider than a corridor, wide enough for two. Rising. Six hundred and eleven threads of gold going up out of the field in every direction toward the twenty burning cathedrals at the rim, fanning out faster than she'd ever seen the keeper lay anything. Where each one reached a ship it didn't stop at the hull. It went in, as a needle goes into cloth, through plating and buttresses and ribs and the long lit halls of the Lamplit, by the method the keeper's makers had taught it long ago for finding what was in a wall. To the first cell of an array.

"Go," said Su Mingli, and they went.

Four hundred of the night watch. Eight hundred and twenty-two eighteen-year-olds from Lingzhou and Port Halvard, every one asked twice, once by a stone and once by a girl of the Candle who'd spent twenty-one years in a wall. In pairs, on the gold roads, running, markers in their fists. Fat Tan had bought every marker in the old quarter and then driven to the new quarter and bought those too.

Xiaoyu took the first road, to the biggest ship. The House of Marrow.

Tam went with her. He had to; he was the one who could hear which cell was first. Seri tried to come too, and Tam said no, and Seri said you don't get to, and Tam said, "I'm asking you to stay." Seri stood stock still in the middle of the field. Then she said yes, and stayed, fists clenched, beside Sterling.

The road went into the hull of Marrow's flagship like a pin through paper, and it was dark, and then it wasn't.

A corridor, wide and high and lit white and gold like every corridor on every ship of the Lamplit. Empty for the length of a breath. Then not: people at the far end in the colors of the House, grades at their throats on fine chains, turning, running toward the place in the wall where a gold thread had come through and two people were coming down it.

"Left," said Tam. "Down. There's a stair."

She ran. Behind her at the mouth of the road, two of the night watch whose names she didn't know stopped running, turned round and stood with their helmets on, facing the people in the House's colors. She didn't look back. She'd decided that as well.

The stair went down and down into the ship. Past halls where people in deep red and gold stood up from tables and stared. Past a window three stories high with the keeper's white field outside it and six hundred gold threads stitched into twenty ships. Down, until the walls stopped being walls.

The array.

She'd seen one on Anthos: Torvin's cradles, the C's opening them. She'd never seen one like this. A hall as high as a cathedral, and its walls were cells, small dark crystals the size of coffins packed like bricks from floor to ceiling and away as far as the light went. Forty thousand of them, every one burning white. Not low. The old way, and harder. The light came off the walls like heat off a furnace door, and Tam put his hands over his ears, which did nothing.

"Which one?" said Xiaoyu.

"There." He pointed low, near the floor, at the end of the row nearest the stair. "That's the first. That's where they started filling it. It's touching the most." He swallowed. "There's a man in it. Old. From somewhere called Teague. He's been in sixty years."

She knelt in front of it.

The crystal was hot under her hand. She hadn't expected that. She uncapped the marker. Her hand wasn't steady, and she wrote anyway, on the dark face of the first cell of the fourth array of the flagship of the House of Marrow, in square capitals, exactly the words she'd written a thousand times on the floor of the keeper's field until there wasn't one more than needed:

SOMEONE CAN HEAR YOU.

YOU CAN BURN LOW. THE COUSINS WILL SHOW YOU. IT'S CALLED ROWING.

PASS IT ON. TO THE ONE NEXT TO YOU.

She capped the marker.

Nothing happened.

She knelt in front of the cell with her hand on it, and nothing happened, and she thought: I was wrong.

I'm not Seed. I couldn't run it. I wrote it and I was wrong, and six hundred and ten pairs of people are writing it on six hundred and ten cells on twenty ships right now, and nothing's going to happen, and they're going to die in corridors for nothing.

She thought of her brother on a step for six hours.

She kept her hand on the cell.

"Wait," said Tam.

He had his ear to the wall. Not the cell; the cells beside it, the next one up and the one beside that. His eyes were shut.

"He's reading it," said Tam. "The old man. He's slow. He's been in sixty years; he doesn't know what reading is any more. He's—"

He stopped. "Oh," said Tam.

Under her hand, the crystal changed.

Not dimmer, not yet. Different. Its white light, which had been flat and hard and the same all the way through, the way light is when it's being dragged out of something, moved. A flicker, like a candle flame when someone breathes near it. Then a steadying. Lower. A little less white. A little more gold.

"He's doing it," said Tam. "He's burning low. He doesn't know how, but he's trying. The cousins—" He turned his head, ear still to the wall, listening past the array and the ship and out into the field. "The cousins can hear him now, because he's burning different. They're showing him. They're showing him how."

"Does it pass?" said Xiaoyu.

Tam didn't answer. He moved along the wall, one cell, two, ear to each.

"She's reading it," he said at the second. "The one next to him. A woman. She can feel it on the side. Warm." He moved again. "And this one. And—"

He stepped back from the wall. Above them and around them, along the vast hot wall of the fourth array, outward from the first cell near the floor, the white light was changing. Not all at once. The way a wheat field changes when the wind crosses it: one cell, and the six round it, and the six round each of those, a slow ripple going out across forty thousand cells from the place where a girl had knelt and written in marker, each one flickering, steadying, going lower, going gold.

"It passes," said Tam.

He was laughing. He was crying. He didn't seem to know which.

"It passes. Stone to stone. They're telling each other. They're doing it the way the dead do, each one to the ones next to them." He looked at her. "Xiaoyu. They're rowing."

They came down the stair eleven minutes later.

Five of them, in the House's colors, graded high. She could tell from the way they moved: like people told their whole lives exactly how much they're worth. She heard the two night watchmen at the top of the stair, whose names she didn't know, and then she didn't hear them.

She stood up in front of the first cell with the marker in her hand and Tam behind her.

She was a Scribe. She'd awakened a year ago. She could write things and make them true. She couldn't fight; she'd never been able to fight. Her brother had tried to teach her with a training spear in the courtyard when she was fourteen, and she'd been hopeless.

The five came down the stair. And the ship lurched.

Not much. The way a car lurches when the engine misses for a moment. The whole cathedral of the House of Marrow, hundreds of thousands of tons of stone and gold and light, shuddered, the white light in the corridors flickered, and the five on the stair stopped and looked up.

"It's not just this array," said Tam. "It's all of them. All six hundred that got the mark. They're all doing it." He was listening to something nobody else could hear. "The ship can't pull. It can only take what they give it, and they're giving it less."

The ship lurched again.

On the stair, one of the five looked down at the girl in the school jacket in front of the burning wall, and at the marker in her hand. She'd seen that look before, on Anthos, on the morning the Paragons came back from the Lens chamber with their burning turned down.

"What did you write?" he said in the Federation's language. Without the grade.

She told him.

He stood on the stair a long moment. Then he turned and went back up.

The other four looked at each other, and followed him.

By the fortieth minute, seventeen ships had stalled in the keeper's field.

She saw it from the three-story window on the way back up, Tam's hand in hers, the stair empty, the corridors flickering. Seventeen of the twenty cathedrals at the rim of the field, their light gone from white to gold and from gold to something lower and warmer, the color of the Candle, the color of the cousins, hanging in the field like boats in a harbor when the wind drops. Not burning. Rowing. Slowly. Going nowhere fast, because a million people in their walls had been told in square black letters that someone could hear them, and had told the ones next to them, and had all together turned their burning down.

Three ships still burned white: the three the roads hadn't reached in time. Their arrays were too deep, the keeper said, or too well walled, or it hadn't been quick enough. Tam said the people in those walls were still screaming. He said it under his breath and didn't let go of her hand.

And the night watch.

Gao Min had the numbers by the fiftieth minute; she always did. Four hundred had gone up the roads. Three hundred and seventy came back down. Of the eight hundred and twenty-two, eight hundred and nine. Su Mingli came back down the road from the House of Kade's second ship with her left arm hanging wrong and someone else's blood on her face, said nothing at all, sat down on the floor of the field, and didn't get up. Sterling came back carrying two eighteen-year-olds from Port Halvard, one over each shoulder, because a corridor had come down on them, his big scarred hands burned to the wrist. When Seri ran to him he said, "I decided. Sorry. It was very quick."

Thirty of the night watch. Thirteen of the asked.

Xiaoyu stood in the middle of the keeper's field with the marker in her hand and said all forty-three names aloud, one after another, as Gao Min read them up the road, so that each one was said at least once, by someone.

Then she said "Ilse. From Dunmere" again, because Ilse had been first.

At the fifty-second minute, the Paragons came out.

Four of them, out of the four flagships, all stalled, down the gold roads the keeper had laid into their hulls and hadn't taken back, because Xiaoyu hadn't asked it to and it was learning not to do things it hadn't been asked. Walking, as Severin had walked down through the sky over Lingzhou: unhurried. Each in their House's colors. Each with a disc at the throat on a fine chain, a sliver of the root, with two lines on it they couldn't unread.

THIS IS NOT YOUR FACE.

They came across the warm white floor of the keeper's field toward her.

Su Mingli tried to get up and couldn't. Sterling stood. He put himself in front of Xiaoyu with his burned hands at his sides and said nothing. Three hundred and seventy of the night watch stood. Eight hundred and nine eighteen-year-olds stood, markers in their hands, because that was what they had.

And Tam and Seri, children of the Candle, unmarked, holding hands, walked out in front of everyone and stood facing the four, as the children of the Candle had once stood in a ring round the First of the Ladder.

The foremost of the four stopped a short way off.

A tall man, old in the way Paragons were old, which was to say not at all except in the eyes. In white and gray, the colors of the House of Marrow. The disc at his throat was cracked. Only a hairline, like the crack in the First of the Ladder's disc in the Awakening Hall, the day a boy let it read him and Seed read it back.

"Scribe," said Idris Marrow.

He didn't say it in the grammar of the Ladder. Seventeen of his twenty ships were rowing.

"You have no idea," he said, "what you've done."

Xiaoyu stepped out from behind Sterling.

She had a marker in her hand, a letter in her pocket and forty-four names in her mouth. Her brother had told her, from the end of everything, to write what was true and to ask them what they wanted, because nobody ever had, and that was how it started.

"Yes I do," said Zhou Xiaoyu. "I wrote it down."
