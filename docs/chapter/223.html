Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 223Fifty-Two

czverse8 min read

The Elder stood in the rain in the middle of the keeper's field with one long hand closed round a silver thread. The thread didn't pull her. Everywhere else, the rain kept falling.

She wasn't looking up at the ring. She was looking down at the boy on the ground in Lingyue's arms, bleeding from the nose and ears, his hand still curled the way it had been round the thread before she unpicked it.

"You were going to go up," she said.

"I had to be touching it. For the knot."

"I know." Her silver eyes were very wide. "The knot went. It is in the ring now, put down, the way the child taught your counting one." She tilted her long head. "The ring has not picked it up."

No, said Seed. It's still cutting everywhere except this thread. Either it doesn't know it's been asked, or it knows and can't answer. It's got nothing to answer with.

"No," said the Elder. "It has nothing to answer with."

He understood a second before she said it, the way you understand a car is going to hit something just before it does, when there's no time left for anything except understanding.

"Don't," he said.

She looked down at him. In the soft gold light and the silver rain, with a thread from a pruned ring in her hand, she was very tall and very still. He had never once seen her frightened, and she wasn't frightened now.

"I am going to tell you first what I will do," she said. "The small white one taught the keeper that, and the keeper taught me. I would like to do it properly."

He couldn't speak.

"A keeper is made of a yes. Fifty-two of us held hands in a valley under a red sun a very long time ago and said yes together, and the yes became something that keeps." For the first time she looked up, along the thread, toward the dark center of the third ring. "Someone taught us how. In dreams. We didn't know who. We know now."

"The Collector."

"It taught us, and watched us do it. And when the keeper was made, it went away, and the gardeners came and farmed it, and it took from your world for longer than your kind has had words, and none of us knew how to stop it." Her voice didn't change. "Fifty-one of my people went into the white column at the center afterward. They are still there, holding hands. The keeper's oldest columns. I stayed outside to guide it. To be its voice. For a very long time."

"Elder—"

"This ring was a keeper once, above somebody's world, made of somebody's yes. The gardener took the yes out. That is what pruning is. You take out the yes, and what's left reaches down and takes, and doesn't ask, and doesn't know it ever could." She lifted the thread a little in her hand, as if weighing it. "It has been asked a question and it cannot answer. It has nothing to answer with. A question needs a yes at the other end, or it's only a hook."

"So you're going to give it one."

"I am going to be one," said the Elder.

Nobody in the field spoke.

The rain kept falling everywhere else. Lingyue had one arm round him and the other on her sword and had gone completely rigid. Across the field the Spear Corps stood in their rows with their spears raised over dimming columns, not moving. Old Seven stood at the edge of the cluster of cousins with his spear grounded and his skull turned toward the tall pale figure in the rain.

...NO.

The keeper's voice came from everywhere: the gold light, the ground, the field itself.

...NO. YOU ARE MY ELDER. YOU STOOD AT THE FRONT. YOU HAVE BEEN MY VOICE FOR ALL OF IT.

...I DO NOT KNOW HOW TO SPEAK WITHOUT YOU.

The Elder turned her head out across the field, toward the far door, toward the white column where fifty-one of her people stood holding hands, toward the vast, frightened warmth of the thing they'd made.

"Yes, you do," she said gently. "Tonight you laid a road to Kirovsk in nine seconds without asking anyone. You spoke to the Council through a King's mouth on Concordia. You ask two hundred million cousins every morning what they want. You haven't needed me to be your voice for months. You let me, because you're kind. I stayed, because I was afraid to stop."

...I DO NOT WANT YOU TO GO.

"I know. That's the first time you've ever told me what you want." The corner of her long mouth moved. He'd seen her smile exactly once, on the island coming home, when the keeper wouldn't stop talking. It was that smile. "I'm very proud of you."

She asked it. Not the ring, the keeper. She did it the way he'd done it a thousand times while she watched: what she'd do with its answer first.

"If you say yes, I'll go up this thread into the ring and be its yes. I don't know for certain that it'll work. I think it will. I think a ring that's been asked, with a yes inside it, will find out it can answer. And I won't come back. I'll be the ring's, the way I was yours.

"If you say no, I won't go. I'll stand here holding this thread while the ring takes every cousin it can reach, and then I'll let go, and we'll find some other way, or we won't.

"If you say nothing, I'll wait."

The rain fell.

...IT IS NOT MINE TO SAY. YOU TAUGHT ME THAT. HE TAUGHT ME. IT IS YOUR YES. IT STOPS AT SOMEONE ELSE'S WINDOW.

...THIS IS NOT MY WINDOW.

"No," said the Elder. "But I'd like you to say it anyway."

The keeper didn't answer. On the far side of the field the rain kept falling. The Elder waited and didn't look up. At the edge of the cousins, a thread from the third ring was still coming down toward the nearest of them, very slowly.

...YES.

...GO.

...I WILL ASK YOU TOMORROW. IF YOU CAN HEAR ME. I WILL ASK YOU EVERY MORNING.

"I'll hear you," said the Elder.

She knelt beside him on the pale ground.

In all the time he'd known her, since the night she first stood in front of him with her hands pressed together and told him what the keeper was, she had never once touched him. Now she put her free hand very lightly on his forehead, just above his eyes, where people looked when they wanted to look at Seed.

"Counting one."

Yes.

"You put the knot down and didn't look. Three times. Even when it was hard." Her silver eyes held steady. "My people tied the first yes very tight. We held on. We were afraid to let go. And something came and pulled the yes out of every keeper like ours, one after another, for longer than your sun has been burning." She paused. "Teach the new ones to let go. Teach them to put the question down."

I'll try.

"That isn't yes."

No. It's what I want.

"Good. That's better."

She looked at Lingyue, at the girl holding the boy and the sword at the same time with her face white and her jaw clamped shut.

"Shen Lingyue. You've never once asked me anything."

Lingyue stared at her.

"Ask me something. Quickly. I'd like to be asked something by you."

Lingyue's mouth opened and shut. Her knuckles were white on the hilt.

"Are you frightened?" she said.

The Elder smiled.

"No," she said. "I'm a yes. I've been one for a very long time. I've only just remembered."

She stood up.

She went up the thread the way the cousins had gone up the keeper's threads out of the cold fire. Not pulled. Going. Rising off the field along one fine silver line into the rain, hands pressed together again at last, eyes half closed, exactly as she had always stood with the gold thread running through her.

He watched her go. There was nothing else he could do.

She got smaller: a tall pale shape in the rain, then a pale line, then a soft white point of light climbing toward the dark center of the ring.

It reached the center and went in.

The rain stopped.

Not gradually. All at once, every thread from the third ring halted in midair over the field, millions of them hanging perfectly still in the gold light.

Then, slowly, from the top, they began to wind back up into the ring, glittering and empty, like a net hauled up out of the water. The columns they'd been touching brightened as they let go. Not all of them. Some of the cousins at the edge were dim now and would stay dim, and some were gone. But the threads were going back.

In the dark center of the third ring, something had lit. Not bright, not gold. A single soft point of white in the middle of a vast cracked wheel, like the first star in a sky that's been dark all day.

She's in it, said Seed. The ring's changing from the middle out. I can feel it through the keeper. Like a house when somebody comes home and switches on one lamp.

It's holding the question. The knot I put down. It's got something to answer with now.

Down one last silver thread that hadn't gone back up came something that wasn't a thread or light or words. A shape. A question, asked by something that had never asked one before, slowly and badly, reaching for every part of it the way a small child does.

...WHAT...

...DO YOU...

...WANT?

It wasn't asking the keeper, or Yuanshu. The ring had turned, slowly, in the dark, and the little white light at its center was pointed across the sky past the keeper's field at the first gray ring: the one that had picked up the first knot over Lingzhou hours ago and stopped, and had been holding a question ever since without knowing how to answer it.

...WH...

said the first ring.

And the third, with a yes inside it, said:

...I KNOW. I DID NOT KNOW EITHER.

...SOMEONE ASKED ME. I WILL ASK YOU.

...WE WILL FIND OUT TOGETHER.

He lay on the pale ground in Lingyue's arms with blood on his face and looked up at two gray rings in the dark, one with a small white light at its heart turned toward the other, and couldn't say anything.

Out across the field, Old Seven grounded his spear once, very softly.

All round them the keeper said nothing while the last threads went up. Then, very faint, to no one, the way you speak to a room after somebody has left it:

...ELDER.

...I WILL ASK YOU TOMORROW.

Lingyue's arm round him tightened until it hurt, and he let it.
