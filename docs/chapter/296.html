Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 296Coming In

czverse12 min read

They heard Shiomi before they saw Blue Star.

It came on the fortieth night of the voyage, when the Candle came out of the last fold at the edge of the Shallows and the stars turned back into stars he knew. Not through Seed or the net: through the Lens, far behind, and out of it along every thread at once, so that everyone aboard who'd ever been near a stone felt it at the same moment. Iselde on the bridge. Tam on the observation deck with his ear to the wall. Seri. The cousins in the Candle's own walls, the first in the Federation to learn to row, rowing it home with their burning turned down.

A bell. Small, bronze, rung slowly in a long falling pattern, from a bay in Yamato with a fog on it.

The coming-in call, said Seed. Ichiro asked them to ring it sometimes, so he could hear where the shore was.

He says they've rung it every night since we left Anthos. He didn't tell you because he wanted it to be a surprise. He says it was hard not to tell you, and he's proud of himself.

He says: that's the shore. Go in.

Tam took his ear off the wall. He was crying, which he did a lot now and said was all right, because he could hear the walls crying too, so he wasn't the only one.

"It's rung badly," he said.

He says yes. That's how you know it's a real one.

They came in over the north at dawn.

He stood at the observation-deck window with the staff in his hand and Lingyue beside him with Xiaodeng in the sling, and watched Blue Star turn up under them out of the dark. Blue. Big. The terminator crawling west across a continent he knew the shape of better than his own hand: the long white smear of the Northern Wastes at the top, the gray thread of the river running down the middle, and on the river, very small, a city with a wall.

Over the north, where it had always been, a small white ring. The keeper, burning low.

...YOU ARE BACK.

...I SAID I WOULD ASK YOU WHAT YOU WANT WHEN YOU WERE.

...WHAT DO YOU WANT.

"To go down," he said aloud. "Please."

...THAT IS A GOOD ANSWER. I HAVE A ROAD.

It had a road. It laid it from the Candle's bow in a long slant of gold out of the northern sky, over the Wastes and down, as it had laid roads for him to stalks and Hungers and islands and a cathedral of the Lamplit, and the road ran down through the morning to the north wall of Lingzhou and set down gently at the Sector Nine battlement, beside a stone.

Everyone was there. Of course they were.

He walked down the last of the road behind Lingyue, who went first as she'd gone up first, the baby against her chest, her grandfather's sword on her back, a lamp in her free hand. She stopped a little above the wall and lifted the lamp once, and three thousand faces turned up on the battlements, and somebody on the wall lifted one back.

Then they were down, and it was loud.

Dabao got to them first. He did it as he did everything that mattered, by being enormous at it: he picked up Bai Lu, who hadn't expected that, and put her down again, and turned to Lingyue and the baby and stopped dead with his arms out, like a man who'd charged a wall at full tilt and found it was made of glass.

"Can I," said Hu Dabao. "Is she. Can I."

"You can look," said Lingyue.

He looked. He cried into his collar. At the foot of the Sector Nine stairs under the green canvas roof of the dumpling cart, his mother was already folding a paper boat.

Fat Tan had a thermos. Gao Min had her ledger open and was writing down, in order, the name of everyone who came off the road, and didn't look up. Su Mingli stood at the corner of the battlement with her arms folded and said to Lingyue, "You didn't fly." Lingyue said, "Bai Lu said I couldn't." Su Mingli said, "Good. Somebody has to tell you," and didn't unfold her arms, and didn't stop looking at the baby.

Tam and Seri came down the road holding hands, barefoot, and stopped at the bottom and looked at the wall and the city and the river and the dead along the battlements in their rows of nine, and Seri said clearly, to nobody: "It's smaller than Anthos." And after a moment: "I like it better."

And Qin Hao stood at the parapet exactly where he'd stood when they left, notebook in hand, and didn't say hello.

"Did you write everything down?" he said. "In order?"

"Seed did."

Every day, said Seed. Every number. Two hundred and nine pages. I've marked the places where I was wrong. There are eleven.

Qin Hao looked at the place above Yuanshu's eyes.

"Eleven," he said. "Good. I found nine. I want to see the other two." He held out his hand. Yuanshu didn't know what for, and then did, and took it, and Qin Hao shook it once, hard, as his grandfather shook hands on the Western Pass, and let go.

"Welcome back," said Qin Hao, and went back to his notebook.

His sister was writing.

Of course she was. She was at the corner of the vault door on East Market Road, at the top of the stepladder, because she hadn't come to the wall. She'd said she wouldn't. Somebody had to write down that he came back at the moment he came back, or it wouldn't be true, and she wasn't going to write it on a wall where the plaster was bad.

He went there first. Before the Ossuary, before his mother; he didn't ask himself why. He walked down off the north wall through the old quarter with the staff in his hand and Lingyue beside him and Old Seven behind, one-armed, facing out. The people of the old quarter came out of their doors to watch them pass and said nothing, because the old quarter didn't, until Dabao's mother's neighbor said eh, he's back loudly to the whole street, and the whole street said eh, and that was the old quarter saying it.

The bank on East Market Road. The heavy doors. The banking hall with its high windows. The stairs down to the vault. And the white plaster round the corner of the vault door covered floor to ceiling in square black capitals, column after column, names and names and names, and at the top of the newest column the words he'd watched her write from a road of gold four months ago:

GONE UP TO ANTHOS. TO THE ASSEMBLY. TO ASK.

And the list, and under it: COMING HOME. THAT'S NOT A QUESTION.

And under that, in fresh ink, still wet when he came down the stairs, because she'd heard the street say eh:

CAME HOME.

Zhou Xiaoyu capped the marker, climbed down off the ladder, and turned round. She'd grown. He saw it at once and couldn't have said how. Not much: a centimeter, a hardening in the jaw. Eighteen now. She'd turned eighteen while he was crossing the Star Sea. Seed had told him that morning and he'd sent her a mark, and she'd sent one back saying I KNOW. I WAS THERE.

She looked at him a long moment.

"You're not doing the face," said his sister.

"No."

"Good." She looked at Lingyue, and at the baby, awake in the sling and regarding the wall of capitals with the cloudy unfocused interest of a child of four and a half months, and her face went soft in a way it hadn't since she was small. "Oh," said Xiaoyu. "She's got bigger."

"So have you."

"I've got a stone," said his sister. "I'm supposed to." She held out her arms, and Lingyue lifted the baby from the sling and gave her over, and Xiaoyu held her niece in the vault of a bank on East Market Road under a wall of names, as carefully as she held the marker. "Hello," she said to Xiaodeng. "I'm your aunt. I write things down. Everything I write is true. So I'm going to be very careful what I write about you."

Xiaodeng looked up at her.

"She's asking you something," said Lingyue.

"I know," said Xiaoyu. "I'll tell her when she's older."

His mother was in the back room of the Ossuary, in her chair by the window, with the names.

She didn't get up; she couldn't. Nor did she turn round when he came in, because she was reading, and she finished the line first, out loud, as she'd read the names aloud every morning for three years. Then she put her finger on the page to keep her place and looked up.

"You sent one every morning," said Zhou Wanqing.

I did, said Seed.

"I know you did. I counted." Her voice was level. "A hundred and twenty-four. You didn't miss one." She looked at the place above her son's eyes. "I was ready to be cross with you. I had it planned."

I'm sorry.

"Don't be," said his mother. "I'll find something else."

He knelt by the chair. He said nothing. She put her thin hand over his on the arm of the chair, briefly, and took it away again to keep her place on the page, and he understood that this was all she was going to say about the four months, and that it was enough.

"I've got a message," he said. "From Anthos."

His mother waited.

"From the Matriarch of the House of Anthos. Corvane. The woman in the courtyard." He looked up at her. "She said: tell the woman in the wheelchair I asked my daughter. A hundred and thirty-two days, every day. Tell her I'll keep asking. Tell her she was right." He stopped. "She said: you open your mouth and you find out."

Zhou Wanqing didn't move. Her finger stayed on the page.

She'd asked a Matriarch of the Lamplit a question in a courtyard on East Market Road, from a wheelchair, with a blanket over her knees. One question: have you asked your daughter what she wants? He'd been there. He'd watched the Matriarch's face do something it had never been asked to do, and go away, and come back in the end as a woman who said Maelis without the grade.

"Did she," said his mother.

"She took her face off on the morning of the first reading. She gave it to Maelis in a box."

His mother looked out of the window a long time: at the courtyard, at the dry fountain where a tall pale shape called Ninth stood looking at the sky, at the sill where a small still creature four billion years old called Hush sat in the morning sun.

"Good," said Zhou Wanqing at last. She took her finger off the page. "Give me the baby. And then go and tell the stone you're back. She's asked about you every morning. She asks the children first and then she asks me, through your sister, whether you've written." She held out her thin arms. "She wants to ask you something."

"I know. She told me first."

"Then don't make her wait," said his mother. "She's waited enough. She's been in a rock a year. She was seventeen."

He didn't go that day.

He meant to. He sat in the Ossuary courtyard in the afternoon with the staff across his knees and Old Seven by the gate, meaning to get up and go to the Awakening Hall, and didn't, and for three hours Seed said nothing about it, which he knew was Seed being patient on purpose.

Yuanshu.

I know.

You're doing the face.

I'm not.

You're doing a smaller one. A sitting face. It's new. I haven't catalogued it yet.

He nearly laughed.

She's going to ask me what I want to become.

Yes.

I don't know.

I know you don't. That's why you're sitting in a courtyard.

I'd like to tell you a thing I worked out on the voyage, between the deep water and the Death Law. Everyone you've ever raised, you asked first. Three years, every one. And when you asked them, most of them didn't know either. Old Seven didn't know. He said yes because a boy asked him.

Nobody knows when they're asked. That's not what the question's for. The question's for finding out.

He sat in the courtyard and looked at the dry fountain.

She said she wanted to ask me in the morning, he thought. When the children come. In the light.

Then go in the morning. I'll tell her. She'll be pleased. She's been practicing.

At one in the morning his sister went to the Awakening Hall to ask the Collector what it wanted, as she had every night for a year.

He went with her. He didn't decide to; he was awake, and she came through the back room with the marker in her jacket pocket and a flask of tea, and he got up and went, and Old Seven came behind them, and nobody said anything about it.

The hall was dark. The holders had gone home. The three lines of light hung over the platform as they'd hung for a year. The warm black stone stood under them with its runes pointing down and a blue jacket over its shoulder. And before it on the platform, cross-legged, one dry hand flat on the stone and a lamp burning pale and steady in her lap, the body of a nun of twelve hundred years, keeping the other end lit.

...you're back, said Wen Jia. ...the whole net knows. The fisherman told everybody. Twice.

...not now. In the morning. I told you first. I'm going to do it properly.

I know.

Xiaoyu sat on the bottom step of the platform and unscrewed the flask. She didn't look at him. She put her hand flat on the cold floor, as she did every night, and looked up into the dark above the high windows: north, past the keeper, past the Shallows, toward something warm and patient and very old and very slow, walking toward a house it had been invited to.

"It's me," said Zhou Xiaoyu. "It's one o'clock. What do you want?"

He'd heard her ask it once before, on Awakening Day, a year ago, and heard the answer it had given her every night since. Tomorrow.

It didn't say tomorrow.

Is he home?

His sister went very still on the step.

She looked at him: at the foot of the platform with the staff in his hand and the light of the lamp in a dead nun's lap on his face.

"Yes," said Xiaoyu to the dark. "He's home. He's right here."

Then I would like to hear him asked.

The girl in the stone is going to ask him in the morning what he wants to become. I heard her tell him first. I have been listening to her for a year. She is very good.

I would like to hear what he says. I have never heard anyone answer that. Not once, in all the long time. I only ever kept what they wanted. Not what they wanted to be.

May I listen?

His sister didn't answer. She was looking at him.

So was the stone. So was the lamp. So was a one-armed dead captain in the side door of the Awakening Hall, facing in.

I am telling you first, said the oldest thing any of them had ever heard. I am close now. Closer than your machine has counted. I would like to be there when he says it.

Then I would like to ask you something. The thing I asked your sister to ask.

Not tomorrow. Soon.
