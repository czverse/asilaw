Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 230The Cut

czverse11 min read

Shen Lingyue threw the light uphill.

She didn't shine it or bend it. She gave it weight, the way she'd learned to when there had been no sun to bend and nothing to spend but her own Law, and then she held her hand out over the parapet palm up and let a little of herself fall the wrong way: up into the sky, like dropping a stone into a well.

It went up a long way, slowly, a small pale spark rising over the north wall into the bent dark where the stars were sliding.

Then it touched something, and stopped, and spread.

Afterward people tried to describe it and couldn't, and most of them gave up and said: you had to be on the wall.

The spark touched the underside of the dark and ran outward like a drop of ink on wet paper, except it was light, and it was running over something that had been invisible a breath earlier. As it spread, the thing was simply there.

Thread. Silver-gray, the color of the rings on two people's hands. Thread pressed on thread pressed on thread, a branch as wide as the city, slanting down out of the north from somewhere beyond the end of the world. Its underside was ridged and grooved like bark. Along it, packed close, hung small dark rings the size of houses, pale and gray like old fruit, and he didn't need to ask what they were. Keepers. Pruned. Growing on it.

And at the end, over the wall and the city and three thousand upturned faces, the tip.

It didn't come to a point. It opened. Above the old quarter the branch spread into a fan, a crown, a huge soft frayed thing like the end of an unravelled rope, and from every strand of it something finer still hung down toward the city: silk, hair, almost nothing at all, millions of strands dangling out of the sky over Lingzhou in the spreading light like willow roots over a river.

Touching them. Softly, the way the end of a vine feels along a wall for the next handhold.

On the wall beside him a soldier with a rifle swayed and sat down hard against the parapet with a look of enormous surprise. A girl on her father's shoulders went limp. Down the battlement, three people, nine, thirty, folding over like grain in a wind.

It's reading them, said Seed. Not taking yet. Tasting. Looking for the brightest. It'll start taking in minutes.

Lingyue.

"I know," said his wife.

She climbed up onto the parapet.

He had known she would. They'd planned it in the banking hall over nine of the seventeen nights, and he'd argued against it every single night, and every single night she'd listened and said yes, and I'm still doing it. On the ninth night he'd stopped arguing, because she was right and they both knew it.

A plant grows toward light. So give it one.

She stood on the parapet with her grandfather's sword in her right hand and her left hand open, and this time she didn't throw the light or hold anything down with it or bend it.

She lit.

All of it. Everything the Light Law had, everything a Lord at Level 66 could find in herself, in one breath: not falling, not careful, not running downhill. Out. Her whole body, the sword, her hair, the red silk ribbon she'd tied into it that morning because it had come with the dress from Mount Qingyun. At one in the morning, Shen Lingyue stood on the north wall of Lingzhou and burned like a star that had come down to have a look.

Three thousand people looked away, because they had to.

He didn't. He couldn't. He stood in his old place gripping the staff in both hands and watched his wife burn, and thought very clearly of the Sixth: for one day, we would like to be very bright. And of the eyes of the Mouth. And of what had come along the arm to drink them.

The tip turned.

Everyone who could see through the glare saw it. The great frayed crown over the old quarter lifted, slowly, its millions of hanging strands drawing up off people's heads, out of the hair of the girl on her father's shoulders, off the swaying soldiers, and the whole soft fan of it swung toward one point.

A woman on a wall, burning.

The soldier against the parapet blinked and put a hand to his head. The girl woke up and started to cry. Down the battlement, thirty people, then nine, then three, sitting up, standing, alive, staring at each other in the white light like people who'd been dreaming of falling.

And the tip of the gardener's branch came down toward Lingyue, very low now, very close, like a hand.

Now.

Adjutant Xu carried it.

He'd been sitting on the battlement in his chair since midnight, cane across his knees, at the top of the Asking Lattice: a knot of nine with a knot of nine under each, down into the cemetery, out to the Western Pass, under the sea off Kesse, up a gold road into the north. He hadn't moved. He'd been waiting for one word.

He lifted his cane and brought it down once on the stone.

It went down the Lattice faster than anything Seed had ever measured. Nine, eighty-one, seven hundred and twenty-nine, twelve rounds in one heartbeat. Every dead soldier in the Lattice heard an old adjutant's cane hit the Sector Nine battlement, and every one of them was already where they'd been told to be.

On the branch.

They had climbed it while it read the city, up the gold roads the keeper had laid out of the north in the dark: fifty thousand dead of Blue Star, banked and cold and ash-gray, walking up long shining slants of gold into the night toward a thing that grew toward light and couldn't see anything that wasn't lit. The First Legion in their nines. The Western Legion behind a sergeant with a pick. The Spear Corps. Grid Nine. The Watch of the Shelf, up from the bottom of the sea, Nana Ampofo leading them with water still running off her coat. Up onto the underside of the branch, to a ring of bark a kilometer behind the frayed crown, where they stood in a circle right round it, upside down, waiting.

The cane hit the stone, and Yuanshu gave them the fire.

He didn't have to think. He'd done it a thousand times: down through the staff into the Death Law, all forty-five point six percent of it, and out through the bone of the staff and the bones of the dead like water through a riverbed. Pale fire, her light and his Law wound together. He couldn't reach her now; she was burning, and her light was going up, not down. He didn't need to. He had two years of her inside him, every time she'd laid her hand on the staff and lit it.

He sent it down the Lattice behind the old man's cane, and in one heartbeat fifty thousand rusted weapons on the underside of the gardener's branch went pale.

And cut.

One thread each. That was all. Fifty thousand dead, each with a pale-burning spear or sword or pick or shovel or rusty length of anchor chain, each cutting through one thread of the pressed bark in front of them. It wasn't enough. It could never have been enough. The branch was millions of threads thick.

But Qin Hao was there.

He'd stood at Yuanshu's right since midnight with white fire banked in his new pink hands, and when the cane hit the stone he opened them and let go. Not at the branch: into the pale fire, down the Lattice behind it, white riding pale, the fire that burns what binds following the fire that cuts. Wherever fifty thousand dead had cut one thread, white fire went into the cut and found whatever held that thread to the next one, and the next, and burned it. All the pressing. All the binding. Everything that made the branch a branch, in a ring a kilometer behind the tip.

The thread came apart. Not cut. Unbound, unravelling like rope when someone picks out the twist.

The branch screamed.

Nobody heard it with their ears. They heard it the way you hear a tree come down a long way off in a forest: in the chest, the gut, the soles of your feet. A long, slow, enormous creak. The whole sky over Lingzhou groaned like a ship's hull.

And the branch began to pull back.

It's withdrawing, said Seed. Along the arm, like a hand off a stove. If it pulls back before the cut goes through, the tip goes with it.

Su Mingli.

The King of Gravity hadn't moved from the corner of the parapet all night. Her arms were still folded.

She unfolded them.

He'd seen her fold space before: a forty-thousand-tonne bridge across a city. He'd never seen this. She didn't fold anything. She reached up with one hand, palm out, toward the great bent branch over the north wall, and closed her fingers.

Gravity took the tip.

All of it: the vast frayed crown over the old quarter, pinned, dragged straight down toward the city as if the sky had tipped. The branch behind the cut hauled back along the arm, and hauled, and the tip didn't come, because it was in the fist of a King who'd told everyone she wasn't staying.

Two thousand kilometers north, where the long body of the branch passed low over Kirovsk on its way down to Lingzhou, Marshal Kirill Orlov stood in the square where a ring had fallen as sand and laid his bare hand flat on the frozen ground. Ice came up out of the snow. Not a wall. A pillar, white and blue, climbing kilometers into the dark until it reached the underside of the branch where it passed overhead and froze fast to it.

Now the branch couldn't pull back at all. Its tip was in a fist over Lingzhou, its body was frozen to a pillar over Kirovsk, and between them, a kilometer behind the crown, fifty thousand dead hung upside down in a ring of white fire, cutting.

From the radio on Yuanshu's belt, through the static, a man's voice said one word in Northern.

He says: hurry.

At the eleventh minute it found the Lattice.

Everybody in the Lattice felt it. The tip was turned toward Lingyue, drinking her light from a hundred meters, and she was going gray at the edges like a fire with not much left to burn. But pale fire was light too: fifty thousand thin pale lights on the underside of the branch, and the Lattice that carried them, every knot lit pale from the top down. A road of light running from the branch down into the dead, nine by nine by nine.

To the top.

The tip found it the way a root finds water. Half its strands still drinking at Lingyue, the other half swinging slowly round toward the brightest line in the sky: the line that led down into the dead. All of them. Fifty thousand on the branch, and the cemetery, and the Western Pass, and the sea, and the keeper's field, and the staff in his hands with ninety million names in it.

And at the top of that line, where everything ran into one knot of nine, an old man in a chair with a cane.

He says: I'm tying it off, said Seed.

No—

He says: boy. I'm an adjutant. I carry messages. That's what I'm for.

He says the message is: it stops here.

The old man shut his hollow eyes, and Yuanshu felt him do it.

He took the top knot of the Asking Lattice, the one every other knot ran up to, the one that was him, and pulled it tight. Not cut. Tied, the way you tie off a vein: so the pale fire could still run down and nothing could run back up. Nothing could follow the light down into the dead.

Nothing past him.

The tip's strands came down out of the sky onto the Sector Nine battlement and found the end of the road, and the end of the road was an old man in a chair, and they went into him.

He didn't make a sound. He sat there with his cane across his knees and silver threads in his gray hair and his shoulders and his chest, like willow roots going into a riverbank, and the pale light went out of him from the top down, up the threads and into the dark.

He says—

Seed stopped, and started again.

He says: Old Seven still owes me nineteen dumplings. Tell him I haven't forgotten.

He says: message delivered.

The chair on the battlement was empty.

The cut went through at the sixteenth minute.

Everyone felt it. The groan in the sky stopped dead, like a held breath let out, and Su Mingli staggered, because the thing she'd been holding up with one hand wasn't attached to anything any more. The tip of the gardener's branch came free over the old quarter and began, very slowly, to fall.

And the branch was open.

It hung over the north wall, behind the falling crown, cut clean through. The cut face was a kilometer across, and it wasn't silver-gray. Under the pressed bark it was gold: pale, wet, living, shining like the inside of a green stick when you snap it. And it was drinking. You could feel it. Everything the gardener had, every branch, every pruned keeper hanging off it, the whole tree reaching back along the arm into the dark, was pouring into the wound, exactly as Kwan had said. The newest place. The open place.

The place where it drinks.

One hour, said Seed. Then it heals.

On the parapet, Lingyue's light went out. She stood there a moment in the sudden dark, gray to the lips, the sword hanging from her hand, the red ribbon gone from her hair. Then her knees went, and he caught her.

"Did it work?" she said into his shoulder.

"The cut did."

"Then go," said Shen Lingyue. "Go on. I'm fine. Go."

He looked up.

Down out of the north, along the gold thread from the small white ring, something was coming: wide and pale gold and slow. A third of a keeper's field, given, coming down the sky toward the open cut like a sheet of light being laid over a wound.

At its leading edge, on the gold, small and pale and many-legged, every leg out and every eye open: Hush.

Beside it, two meters ten, with a finger and a thumb on his spear, walking up the gold in the dark the way he'd walked up the stalk and out of the cemetery: Old Seven.

They'd already gone.

Fifty-one minutes, said Seed. Yuanshu. It's time to decide.
