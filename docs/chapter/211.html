Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 211The First Warm Thing

czverse8 min read

Hush had never seen a sunrise, so Xiaoyu set an alarm.

She set it for 05:20, which she described as a war crime, and put the clock on the windowsill of her room at the top of the Ossuary with Hush beside it on a folded tea towel, because the sill was stone and Hush was still cold all the time. Then she got back into bed fully dressed, pulled the blanket up to her nose, and said, "Wake me if it does anything. Don't wake me if it doesn't."

It did something.

Yuanshu heard about it at breakfast, from Seed, because Xiaoyu wasn't speaking to anyone before nine and Hush barely spoke to anyone ever.

It watched the whole thing, said Seed. First gray to full sun. Fifty-one minutes. It didn't move, and it wasn't playing statue. I checked. It said it was looking so hard there wasn't anything left over for moving.

When the sun came over the roofs on East Market Road and reached the sill, it put one leg out into the light, slowly, and held it there.

It said: warm.

It said: that is the first warm thing that did not want anything from me.

Then it woke Xiaoyu up to tell her. She isn't speaking to it either.

It had been sixteen days.

Lingzhou hadn't known what to do with itself after the morning the roads came down, so it did what cities do and went back to work. The trams ran. The night market on Jinhua Road opened under a banner that said WELCOME HOME in letters two meters high; the dead of the First Legion walked under it on their way back to the north wall, and Ma Lianhua reported drily over the Lattice that several of them had stopped to read it twice. Principal Yue gave the Academy a week off and took it back after two days because the students kept climbing on things.

Every evening, the queue outside the Ossuary went round the block. People wanted to see the card.

It was pinned at the end of the INSIDE column, round the corner on the second wall, under the last line Hush had sent from the cold. People stood in front of it without saying anything. Some of them left playing cards of their own on the floor underneath. By the second week there were so many that Fat Tan swept them into boxes every morning, and since he would not throw a single one away the boxes went into the vault, and the vault filled up. Kwan said it was the largest collection of queens of hearts on Blue Star and possibly in the Shallows. Fat Tan said he didn't care. He was keeping them.

Above the sky, the keeper asked. Every morning, nine by nine by nine: two hundred and six million cousins, the six parts of the walls of the Mouth, and the hundred and seventy-five who had stayed when its field was empty. And every morning at three it came down the thread through the Elder to tell somebody what they'd said, because it couldn't keep it in.

...MOST SAY: ASK AGAIN TOMORROW.

...FOUR THOUSAND SAID REST. I LET THEM GO. GENTLY.

...ONE ASKED IF IT COULD LOOK AT YOUR WORLD FROM THE EDGE OF THE FIELD. I SAID YES. IT LOOKED FOR SIX HOURS.

...IT SAID YOUR WORLD IS VERY BLUE.

...I HAVE BEEN ABOVE IT FOR LONGER THAN YOUR KIND HAS HAD WORDS. I NEVER NOTICED IT WAS BLUE.

Shen Jinghong came on the seventeenth day, in a plain dark coat with no insignia, and asked to see his granddaughter's sword.

He didn't come to the Ossuary. He went to the Academy at dusk, to the training ground behind the main hall, where Lingyue was trying to teach forty-one students to let a drop of light fall from the point of a blade and stop it before it hit the sand. She was bad at teaching it and knew it. She could do it, she couldn't explain it, and she kept snapping just hold it at seventeen-year-olds who had no idea what it was.

She stopped when she saw him. The students stopped because she had.

The old King of Light crossed the sand to her: in his eighties, straight-backed, with a face like white stone that had been left out in the weather for a long time. He had handed her his sword on the Sector Nine battlement before the train went west, and gone home to tell three hundred years of Shens that light runs downhill. He hadn't seen it since.

She held it out to him hilt first, in both hands.

He didn't take it. He looked at it: a King's blade, a hundred kilos of pale steel in the dusk. The new nicks along the edge. The place by the guard where it had been gripped so hard and so often that the leather had worn through to bare metal.

"You held it down," he said. "The sky. At your tribulation. Standing on your own light."

"Yes."

"And threw it uphill a kilometer and a half, in a field above the sky."

"Yes."

"And rained it onto eighteen thousand edges on the north wall."

"Yes, Grandfather."

He was quiet.

"In three hundred years," he said, "this family has produced eleven Kings of Light. I am the eleventh. Every one of us learned to shine: outward, upward, brighter than the one before. Not one of us ever thought of letting it fall."

"You did. You said it. Light runs downhill."

"I said it," said her grandfather. "I didn't know what it meant." He reached out one old hand and touched the worn place on the hilt. "Keep it. It's yours. It was never mine. I only carried it until somebody worked out what it was for."

On the far side of the training ground, a boy who had been holding a drop of light perfectly still for nearly a minute lost his concentration, and it fell and burned a small neat hole in his trainer, and he hopped about swearing, and the old King of Light, astonishingly, laughed.

He found Yuanshu afterward on the steps of the main hall, where Qin Hao normally sat with his notebook. He didn't sit down. He stood at the foot of the steps with his hands behind his back.

"Zhou Yuanshu."

"Master Shen."

"Two years ago I told the cameras that the Shen family does not sign papers for necromancers. I tore up the Quiet's petition. My granddaughter left my house for a dormitory with broken radiators and didn't come back for fourteen months."

"I know."

"I'm not here to apologize. Apologies are cheap. I'm told the Architects said theirs for two hours and the widow still hasn't forgiven them." The stone face didn't move. "I'm here to ask you something. I'm told that's how it's done now. And that before you ask, you say what you'll do with the answer."

Yuanshu stood up.

"So," said Shen Jinghong. "Whatever you tell me, I'll go home and repeat it to three hundred years of Shens, and they'll argue about it for a year. My granddaughter says that's good for them. It keeps families busy."

"What's the question?"

The old man looked past him, across the training ground, at a young woman with a King's sword showing a boy, for the ninth time, how to hold a falling drop of light still in the air.

"Is she happy?"

Yuanshu took his time.

He thought about the beach at Port Abiri, and a girl sitting down in the surf with two swords across her knees. About a cracked stone on a training ground and a girl lying on it laughing until she cried. A wall at midnight, a head on his shoulder: I want to be counted as the one who stands next to you.

"You should ask her," he said.

"I'm asking you."

"Then here's what I'll do with it. I'll tell her you asked." He looked across the sand. "I think she's happier than she's ever been. I also think she's tired and frightened and furious about three separate things, and when I tell her you asked she's going to hit me on the arm." He shrugged. "I think that's what happy looks like, on her."

Shen Jinghong considered that.

"Yes," he said at last. "It looked like that on her grandmother too." He turned to go, then turned back. "When you ask her the other question, the one you haven't asked yet, I'd like to be told. Before the cameras."

He walked off across the sand without waiting to see what Yuanshu's face did.

What other question? said Seed.

Seed.

I'm only asking. I count things. I just counted something. Your heart rate went from seventy-one to a hundred and four in two seconds.

I'm not going to say anything else.

I'm making a note that I didn't say anything else.

The trouble came on the nineteenth night, from Kwan, in the form of a picture.

She brought it to the Ossuary at two in the morning on a laptop held open in front of her like a tray of hot soup, didn't knock, didn't say hello, and walked straight across the marble floor between the boxes of playing cards to the vault steps where Yuanshu was sitting with the staff. She turned the screen round.

"I've been watching the gray ones," she said. "Every night since the island. Through the keeper's eye."

On the screen was Seed's map of the arm, the one drawn on Fat Tan's desk on the night of the Shallows: twelve gold dots along a faint curved line, one of them now much brighter than the others. And at the far end, three small gray marks, where three gold dots had gone out while the island raced for the Mouth.

The pruned fields. Three keepers read by something that needed no reason, and forgotten into rings.

"They've been sitting still for a month," said Kwan. "All three, where they were pruned. I checked every single night." She tapped the screen. "Last night they moved."

Yes, said Seed, after a beat. I see it now. I wasn't looking. I was watching the cousins, and Hush learning the sun.

All three, at the same time. They've left where their fields used to be.

"Moved where?"

Kwan turned the laptop back round and stared at it as though it might have changed its mind.

"Here," she said.

Along the arm, said Seed. Toward the brightest field in the Shallows.

They aren't fields any more. They're rings. Lenses. Things that reach down on threads and take. They were somebody's keepers, above somebody's worlds, for longer than Blue Star has had a sun. Nobody ever taught them to ask.

Now they're what the gardener makes out of keepers it has no more use for.

And they're coming to look at ours.

Kwan shut the laptop with a click. In the vault behind them, several thousand queens of hearts sat in their boxes in the dark.
