Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 292What the First Wanted

czverse11 min read

Dacian Severin walked into the Lens chamber with one arm hanging and stopped between ten Paragons of the Great Houses and the root of every face in the Federation, and the first thing he did was apologize.

Not to them. To Lingyue.

"I'm sorry," he said in perfect Huaxian. "I said the corridor was mine. I said anyone who came to the Lens would come past me. Ostrava came past me. And they came round." He didn't look at the ten. He looked at the woman with the sword and the baby. "You had to stand here. With a child. I'm sorry."

"I'd have stood here anyway," said Shen Lingyue.

"I know," said the man in the torn gray coat. "I'm sorry anyway."

Then he turned round.

The ten Paragons at the edge of the broken wall didn't move.

They hadn't moved since the woman with the sword said she wasn't moving: ten of them, in their Houses' colors, burning, the only people in the Federation still burning on purpose, faces at their throats that weren't their faces, standing at the edge of a hole in the wall of a ship in the orbit of Anthos, looking at the man who'd been First.

He was bleeding badly. Seed said so, quietly, above Yuanshu's eyes. Ostrava Iron had come past him in the corridor, and not gently, and a Star Monarch didn't bleed as other people bled. The arm hanging at his side was broken in more places than Seed would count aloud. There was blood on his face and in his hair and down the front of the gray coat Halvarde had given him.

He stood before the Lens with his good hand at his side and looked at them.

"Kael," he said to the young one in the blue of Teller. "You're crying."

The young Paragon put his hand to his face.

"You have been crying since the Assembly. I saw you, in the eighth tier, when Haskel said he wanted to see his brother."

Kael Teller said nothing.

"Brannoch," said Severin to the next. "Iyra. Tolland." One at a time, every name. Ten Paragons of the Great Houses, and he knew them all, as Halvarde knew all the rowers. "You said yesterday that you wanted to be first. In front of everyone. I heard you. The boy said it back."

He drew a breath. It didn't sound good.

"I wanted that too," said the former First of the Ladder. "For two hundred years. I was. It was the only thing I ever wanted. The stone opened me that way when I was twelve, and I never wanted anything else."

He reached into the pocket of the torn coat with his good hand.

"And I would like to show you what it is worth."

He took out the face.

The black disc, three gold marks and a fourth above them like a star, cut from the Lens itself: the face of the First of the Ladder, which the House of Severin had worn nine hundred years. Cracked edge to edge on the platform of the Awakening Hall in Lingzhou, when a boy whose stone had gone black let it read him and a harvest sack read it back.

It had light in it again. Two lines. True.

He held it up so they could see.

"This was cut from that," he said, without looking behind him at the Lens. "Nine hundred years ago. A piece of the root. My House wore it. It made us first. Everything with a mark yielded to it." He turned it in his fingers. "It is not my face. A girl on an orchard wrote that this morning, and I read it, and it is true. It was never anyone's face. It was a piece of a stone made to read children and mark how bright."

He turned slowly, one-armed, and walked to the Lens.

"Dacian," said one of the ten, Iyra, in green. Her voice had gone very high. "What are you doing?"

"Giving it back," said Dacian Severin.

He pressed the cracked black disc flat against the surface of the Lens, the disc of black stone the width of the chamber, ground so thin you could see through it, where the lamp was and a fog-ringer was talking.

And let go.

The disc went in.

It didn't break or fall. It went in, as water goes into water: a coin-sized piece of black stone cut from the root nine hundred years ago, going back into what it had been cut from, slowly, edges first, until there was only a small dark place on the Lens's surface where it had been, and then not even that.

The crack in it closed as it went.

Ichiro says: oh, said Seed. It's back. The bit that was missing. He didn't know there was a bit missing. He says it's like a tooth coming back that you didn't know you'd lost.

He says the Lens is whole. It's never been whole, not since the House cut the First's face out of it nine hundred years ago. Everything's clearer. He can see farther. Every face, every stone, every orchard. He can see stones he didn't know were there, out past the Federation, past the Shallows. Thousands.

He says Kiyo says to tell the man in the gray coat thank you. And that he's bleeding on her floor.

The First of the Ladder sat down.

Not by choice. His knees went, as Wanqing said knees went, deciding for you. He sat on the floor of the Lens chamber with his back against the Lens, as he'd sat against the wall of this room for ninety days, his broken arm in his lap and his face, his own face, the one that wasn't a disc, gray.

"Without it," he said, "I am just a man with a broken arm." He looked up at the ten at the edge of the hole in the wall. "That is all. That is what is under it. Under the First. A man."

"You're dying," said Kael Teller.

"Probably," said Dacian Severin. "Ostrava does not do anything halfway." The corner of his mouth moved. "She will be along. She went to the arrays. She is going to try to stop the rowers."

She's in the first array, said Seed. She's standing in front of Halvarde. Halvarde hasn't moved.

Halvarde's asking her what she wants.

Kael Teller came down off the edge of the broken wall.

Slowly: a hundred years old, young for a Paragon, in the blue of the House of Teller, with tears on his face he'd stopped trying to wipe. He crossed the floor of the Lens chamber, past the woman with the sword, who didn't move or lift it, past the boy with the staff, to the man sitting against the Lens with a broken arm in his lap.

He knelt.

Not as people knelt to the First of the Ladder, as two hundred thousand had gone down on the shingle at Corraine out of ten thousand years of habit. As you kneel beside someone who's hurt.

"What do you want?" said Kael Teller.

He said it badly, like a word in a book, with the grade in it and then without.

The former First of the Ladder looked at him a long time.

"Nobody has asked me that," he said. "Except a fog-ringer. And a sack. And a C." He breathed, and it sounded bad now. "You are the first Paragon who ever has."

"I'm asking."

"I know."

Dacian Severin turned his head with difficulty and looked at the boy from Blue Star.

"You said, in the Assembly— the Matriarch said she would stand beside the stone on Anthos on every reading morning until someone chooses to sit in it." He was quiet a moment. "Is that still true? That someone can choose?"

Yuanshu knelt on his other side.

"Yes," he said.

"Even someone who did what I did."

"The Old Chord," said Yuanshu. "Seven Singers. They sang their own children up a thread to a hand in the dark for longer than you've had a Federation, and forty-one burned in a thread because they pushed them. And they asked to go into the stones of their world when they stop, and ask every child." He gripped the staff. "Nobody said it was enough. It isn't. They asked anyway."

The First of the Ladder shut his eyes.

"Then that is what I want," said Dacian Severin. "The stone on Anthos. Below the House. Where I was read at twelve and my father stood up in the box." His good hand had gone flat against the floor. "I would like to sit in it and ask them. Every child. On the morning. Before anything else."

"Corvane will be standing beside it," said Yuanshu.

The man on the floor gave something that was nearly a laugh.

"Good," he said. "She will make sure I do it properly."

He died at nine minutes to one on the floor of the Lens chamber, his back against the root, a Paragon of the House of Teller on one side of him and a boy from an orchard in the Shallows on the other.

Yuanshu asked first.

He went down into the Death Law, all fifty-seven point eight percent of it, to the cool dark place at the bottom where the door stood a little open, and found him there. Not burning, not bright: a man's ember, very small as the dead are small, sitting on the threshold with his back against something that wasn't there.

It's me, he said. The ungraded one. I'm asking. Do you want the door? Or the stone?

...the stone, said Dacian Severin. ...I said. Is she there? Corvane?

She's in the reading-hall below the House of Anthos, said Seed. Ichiro's told her, through her face. She has her hand on the stone.

She says: tell him I am here. Tell him I have been standing here since dawn. Tell him he is late.

...of course I am, said the ember on the threshold. ...I have never been late for anything in my life. Tell her I am making up for it.

They did it as they'd done it at Corraine, except that the Lens was the root and Ichiro was in it, and Ichiro said everything came through the root now. If the boy would hold the door at the bottom of the Death Law, and the old man at Port Abiri and the docker and the girl in Lingzhou would hold their knots, he'd pass the First of the Ladder along the threads himself.

From the Lens, down through the net. Not up to the knot in the deep water and across: down. Because the Lens was the root, and every stone was cut from it, and the stone on Anthos was cut from stones cut from it, and a fog-ringer who'd found the root could hear everything had found that it could carry, too.

Ninety seconds. Seed counted.

And in a reading-hall below the House of Anthos, beside a black stone ten meters high where every child of Anthos had been read for nine thousand years, a woman of sixty-one in deep red with her hand flat on the stone felt something come into it through her palm, past her, downward, as a cup feels water poured into it.

And every rune on the stone of Anthos turned like a crowd in a street, and pointed down.

Death Law: 57.8 to 58.3 percent, said Seed. The stone on Anthos has somebody in it.

Corvane says the letters are forming in the air over the stone. She says they say: [I'm here. I'm late. Ask me tomorrow.]

She says that's the first time in his life he's ever asked anyone to ask him tomorrow.

Kael Teller was still kneeling on the floor of the Lens chamber beside a man in a torn gray coat who wasn't there any more.

He didn't get up for a long time, and nobody asked him to. The other nine stood at the edge of the broken wall in their colors and had stopped burning. Seed said so. One at a time, while Severin died, every one of them had turned it down. Not out: down, as four thousand seats had been turned down in the Assembly that morning.

At last the young Paragon of Teller stood up.

He looked at the Lens: whole now, with a lamp in it, burning, and a fog-ringer, and two lines of writing it had sent to every face in the Federation that morning which nobody could unread.

He took the face off his own throat.

He didn't cross it out or put it into the Lens. He held it out on its fine chain to the boy from Blue Star.

"I do not know what to do with it," said Kael Teller. "I have never not worn it."

Yuanshu looked at it.

"Ask the stone," he said. "The one on Teller. When there's somebody in it." He didn't take the disc. "Till then, keep it in a box. Someone I know keeps everything in boxes. He says that way you can decide later."

Ostrava Iron came to the bow at half past one.

Alone, walking, down the long corridor where she'd fought the First of the Ladder and come past him. In iron-gray, a face at her throat with four gold marks and two true lines in it. Not a mark on her, not a scratch: a Star Monarch who'd come past another Star Monarch untouched.

She stopped in the door of the Lens chamber.

She looked at the floor where a man had been sitting. At the nine at the edge of the hole in the wall, turned down. At Kael Teller with his face in his hand. At a woman with a sword and a baby. At a boy with a staff.

At the Lens.

"He's in the stone on Anthos," said Yuanshu. "He asked to be. He'll ask every child before it reads them."

The Paragon of the House of Iron said nothing.

"Halvarde asked you what you wanted," said Yuanshu. "In the first array. Seed says you didn't answer."

Ostrava Iron stood in the doorway a long time.

"She asked me eleven times," she said at last, in the Federation's language, with every grade in it. "She asked me by name. Every time. Like I was a rower." Her voice didn't change. "I came to break the stone. I walked past two hundred and forty thousand people in walls, and a C asked me eleven times what I wanted, and I did not break anything."

She looked at the Lens.

"I do not know why," said the Paragon of the House of Iron.

She does, said Seed. She doesn't want to say. That's allowed.

"Ask me tomorrow," said Ostrava Iron. She turned and walked back up the long corridor alone, at an even pace, unhurried, toward the arrays, where Seed said afterward she stood the rest of the night beside a small old C with a voice like gravel, listening to her ask two hundred and forty thousand people, one at a time, by name, whether they wanted to go on.
