Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 245Lantern

czverse11 min read

"I want to go up," he said.

He said it on the vault steps on the fifth morning, with the staff across his knees and Gao Min's chart across the hall showing ten lines still climbing and one, Lingzhou, flat for two days. He said it to Seed, but out loud, so Lingyue on the stairs could hear, and Kwan at the long table, and Old Seven at the bronze door, because he'd learned long ago that if he only said things to Seed, nobody could tell him he was wrong.

"Up the thread. To Callum Ward."

I know, said Seed. I've known since the bell. You've got the face.

"He's on the end of a thread being used to see by. Burning. Since the hammer, which is thirty-four days." His hands tightened on the staff. "Nobody's asked him anything. Not the stone, not the thing that's got him, not us." He looked at the chart. "I want to ask him what he wants."

"And if he says get me out?" said Lingyue.

"Then I'll find out how."

"And if he says leave me?"

He didn't answer straight away.

"Then I'll tell his mother. She's in the queue at Port Halvard with her hand on the stone; Sterling said so. Somebody should tell her what he said, whatever it is."

Lingyue looked at him a long moment, then at the flame in her hand, peach-sized, pale and steady.

"Not alone," she said.

"It's not a place you can come."

"I know it's not a place I can come." She stood. "I'm going to sit next to you while you go, with my hand on you. If anything up there touches you that I don't like, I'll feel it and pull you back." She came down the stairs. "I'm bigger than a grain of rice now. I can pull."

They did it in the Awakening Hall at noon, because that was where the thread was.

Not the Lingzhou thread; Port Halvard's. But all eleven ran up out of the stones and joined somewhere in the dark under the world, and Wen Jia said she could pass him along sideways, as the other ten had gone in. She said it the way she said everything, briskly, as if it were obvious.

...I'll take you to the old man's thread first, Port Abiri, then across to the dock worker's at Port Halvard. He knows you're coming. He says he'll hold your hand on the way up. He says that's what dockers do.

...don't let go of the knot. Not the thread. The knot. The thread's what it pulls on.

The hall hadn't been empty since the first night. Holders stood in a long line from the platform steps up the aisle and out through the side door into the sun on East Ring Road, every age there was, and every few minutes one of them climbed the three steps and put a hand on the warm stone and said what they wanted, and the stone corrected their grammar if it had to and opened a little holding in them, and they stood with their hand on it for as long as they could manage. An hour, two. Then the next. Gao Min sat on a chair at the foot of the platform with a ledger on a board across her knees and wrote down every one.

The line made room for him without being asked. He sat on the platform with his back to the stone, under the blue jacket, the staff across his knees. Lingyue sat beside him and laid her hand flat on his chest over his heart, the flame in her other hand.

Old Seven stood at the foot of the steps, facing out.

Yuanshu shut his eyes.

Seed took him up.

Not down into the Death Law, or under it to the first page. Out: through the staff, across the gap into the warm black stone, up through the crown of the tree of Laws inside it, and onto the thread. He'd felt Seed go up threads before. He'd never gone with it.

It was like being a drop of rain sliding along a washing line. Fast. Up through the roof and the sky over Lingzhou, through the blue into the black. Then sideways, somewhere in the dark under everything, where eleven pale threads ran together like the strands of a rope, and a voice he didn't know, very old and slow, said there you are, boy, I like the sea, hold on, and passed him along. Then another hand, rough, a hand that had hauled ropes on the Atlantic docks for thirty years before something sang, took his and said, in an accent he'd only ever heard on the radio from Sterling: Right then. Up we go. Don't look down. There's nothing down, but don't look.

And up.

He didn't know how long it took. Seed knew and didn't say.

Past the keeper's field, gold and vast and a third smaller than it had been, so close he could feel it like the warmth of a stove through a wall. Past the place where the branch was, a darkness where the far lights bent, with a small gold bud at its end that turned, he thought, very slightly as he went by, as a flower turns. Along the arm. Past where the Mouth had been.

The cold fire. He felt it go by beneath the thread: a great still coldness, and at its center, tiny, sixty embers standing in a row, facing out.

Huo, he thought as hard as he could. Huo Changsheng. You held.

He didn't know whether anything heard.

Then past the end of the arm into the deep water between arms, where Seed's map had nothing and there was no light at all, and the thread ran on into the dark, taut and pulled and humming like a fence wire in a gale.

There. Ahead. Don't look at all of it. Look at the light.

He looked at all of it. Nobody could have helped it.

Seed had said bigger than anything. The Shiomi bell had said bigger than anything. He'd thought he knew what that meant. He'd seen the keeper's field from inside, and a Hunger's mouth, and a branch the size of a mountain range across the sky over Lingzhou.

It wasn't like any of them. It wasn't a shape. It was a place where the dark was busier, as a forest at night is busier than an empty field: not brighter, not darker, but full. Moving. Something enormous and patient and complicated, coming along eleven threads at once out of the deep water, hand over hand. He couldn't see the hands. He could feel them, as you feel a crowd behind you in the dark. Thousands. Each on a thread, each finding the next hold and closing on it and pulling, and the whole of it coming on, without hurry and without stopping, like a tide up a beach.

It didn't see him. It didn't see anything. He understood that at once, as you understand, standing beside someone in the dark, that their eyes are open and they still can't see you. He wasn't bright. He was a drop of rain on a line. It had no eyes for anything that wasn't burning.

Except one thing.

Out in front of it, on a single thread, held out in one of its thousands of hands like a lantern on a dark road, something burned.

So bright it was the only thing in the deep water. White, gold, every color, burning as Lingyue had burned on the wall and the Sixth in the eyes of the Mouth, everything at once, nothing held back. A man. He could see the shape of him in the fire. Young. Broad in the shoulders. Arms raised, as if he'd been holding something heavy over his head and never put it down.

The hammer.

Callum Ward. Still burning, thirty-four days on.

It's using his light to see the threads: where they go, which way. That's how it walks. It holds him out in front and follows what he shows it.

He's being used up. Slowly, like a candle. There's still a lot of him. There won't always be.

Callum, he thought.

Nothing. Fire. The roar of it, which wasn't a sound.

He can't hear you through that. It's too bright. You'll have to go nearer. And if you go nearer, the hand will see you, in his light.

I've run it. In most runs it won't know what it's seeing. You're not bright; you're a shadow in his light. It'll look at you as you'd look at a moth. In some runs it reaches.

On a platform in Lingzhou, a very long way below, a hand pressed flat on his chest. Warm. He felt it as you feel your own heartbeat lying very still.

Nearer, he thought.

He went into the light.

Into the heat of it, which wasn't heat. It was the feeling he'd had on the platform the night before Awakening Day, when the stone opened him and it was the best thing he'd ever felt. He felt it again: a door in his chest, light, everything at once. He wanted it. He set his teeth against wanting it and went nearer, a raindrop sliding into a fire.

Under the roar, small and far down, as a voice is far down at the bottom of a well, someone was talking. Not to him. To nobody. Over and over, as a man talks to himself after thirty-four days alone in a bright place.

...told me what I was. F. Told me what I was. Seven years. Docks. F. Lowest it gives. Told me. So I told it. I told it what it was. Swung. Hit it. Told it.

...didn't know it'd feel like this.

...so bright. It's so bright. Never been bright. F. Never been anything. Look at me now. Look at me.

...where's Mum.

...where's Mum.

Callum.

The talking stopped. The fire didn't; it roared on in the vast hand. But at the bottom of the well the voice had stopped, as a voice stops when it hears its own name for the first time in thirty-four days.

...who's that.

My name's Zhou Yuanshu. I'm from Lingzhou. I'm the one with the thing in my head.

...the one off the telly. The orchard. The sack.

Yes.

...the one who broke all eleven. Something that in a man not on fire might have been a laugh. ...I only did one. You did all eleven. With your hand.

I didn't mean to.

...I did.

The fire roared.

...is my mum all right?

She's in the queue at your stone in Port Halvard with her hand on it. Holding it down, so the thing that took you can't take anything else.

The voice was quiet a long while.

...that's Mum, said Callum Ward. ...that's exactly Mum.

Callum, I'm going to ask you something. First I'll tell you what I'll do with the answer.

...go on then.

If you want to come out, I'll find out how. I don't know how yet, but I'll find out. If you want to go through the door, the one at the bottom of everything, where things go when they're finished, I'll open it for you; it's always a little open now. If you want to stay where you are, I'll tell your mum that's what you said. Whatever you say, I'll tell her exactly. He held on in the light, to the knot and not the thread. What do you want?

The fire roared. He waited, and didn't look in the bowl.

...I don't know.

The voice was very small.

...I want to stop. It's using me up. I can feel it. Like a candle, like you said.

...and I don't. I want to stop and I don't. Because I've never been bright. Not once. Twenty-five years. And now I'm the brightest thing out here. It holds me out in front. It follows me. I'm the only thing it can see.

...I'm the lantern. I've never been anything and now I'm the lantern. How can I want to stop being that?

He didn't have an answer. He stayed in the fire with the voice at the bottom of the well and didn't have one, and didn't pretend to.

...ask me again, said Callum Ward. ...tomorrow. Can you? Ask me again tomorrow.

Yes.

And the hand turned.

He felt it before Seed spoke. The vast hand that held the lantern, one of thousands, turned, slowly, as a man on a dark road turns his lantern when he thinks he's seen something move at the edge of the light.

Toward the shadow in the fire.

It sees you. In his light. It doesn't know what you are, but it's seen something on the thread in front of it that shouldn't be there.

Come back. Now.

He let go. Not of the knot: of the fire. He let himself slide back down the thread, falling, faster and faster, out of the light into the dark, along the arm, past the cold fire and where the Mouth had been and the branch with its bud and the keeper's warmth. A docker's rough hand caught his on the way down, steady, boy, steady. An old man's voice, I like the sea. A girl's, brisk: got you, got you, you idiot. And then—

A hand on his chest. Warm. Pulling.

He opened his eyes on the platform of the Awakening Hall with Lingyue's face over his, white, and the flame in her other hand blazing the size of a lamp, blood running from his nose into his mouth, and a line of two thousand people with their hands on a black stone staring at him.

"You went too near," said Shen Lingyue. "I felt it. You went too near."

"Yes."

"Did you ask him?"

"Yes."

"What did he say?"

"Ask again tomorrow."

There's something else, said Seed. I'm sorry. I'd rather tell you now than in the morning.

He was lying on the narrow bed in the back room. It was evening. Bai Lu had been and gone, had called him an idiot in three languages, and had stopped the bleeding.

The hand that turned didn't turn back. When we came down the thread, it followed. Not the whole thing. One hand. It let go of the thread it was on and took hold of the one we were on, and it's coming down it.

Faster than the rest. Much faster. A hand's lighter than the whole.

The Port Halvard thread. Nine days.
