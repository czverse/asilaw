Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 201The Island

czverse9 min read

The keeper tore off a piece of itself, and that was how they went.

Nobody had expected it. On the first voyage it had given them a raft: forty meters of its own pale ground, carried out through the far door into the Sea with twelve living and a few hundred dead on board and Bai Lu's brass clock in the middle telling everyone when to sleep. Yuanshu had assumed it would be a raft again. A bigger one, maybe. He had spent part of the night before working out how many rows of nine you could fit on forty meters, and the answer had been depressing.

They came up the six gold roads at dawn: fifty thousand dead of Blue Star and eight of the living. They crossed the empty field between the faint rings where the columns used to stand. The hundred and seventy-five who had chosen to stay turned in their gold columns to watch the Western Legion march past, like spectators at a parade nobody had told them about. They passed the place where four rings lay side by side on the ground and Major Zhuo Wenli was no longer standing. Sergeant Ma slowed there, and then didn't.

At the far door, where the field stopped as abruptly as the edge of a table, the keeper was waiting.

...FIFTY THOUSAND.

...I CANNOT CARRY FIFTY THOUSAND ON A RAFT.

...SO I WILL CARRY THEM ON ME.

The ground under their feet began to move.

It came away with a sound he felt in his back teeth, low and long, like a building settling. A crack opened across the pale ground behind the last rows of the Western Legion and widened, and the ground on their side tilted a few degrees and started to drift.

Out through the far door. Into the dark.

He stood at the front with the staff in his hand and Old Seven beside him and looked back. The field went away behind them, huge and pale and empty, pocked with the rings of the dead who had left. Between the field and his feet, the crack, widening. And on this side, flat warm ground lit from underneath, running off in every direction farther than he could see, with fifty thousand of the dead of Blue Star standing on it in their rows.

Dabao, behind him, said, "Bro. That's not a raft."

"No."

"That's a county."

...A PIECE OF MY FIELD.

...I HAVE NEVER TORN ONE OFF. I DID NOT KNOW IF I COULD.

...IT HURTS. LIKE PULLING A TOOTH. A BIG ONE.

...I WILL GROW IT BACK. PROBABLY.

Four kilometers across, said Seed. It has air, the way the field has air. It's warm. The keeper isn't touching it any more, it's holding it by attention, like a plate held out at arm's length in the dark.

I've never seen anything do something that frightened it on purpose before. I'm going to write that down.

The Elder steered.

She stood in the middle of the island with her long hands pressed together and her silver eyes half shut, and listened, and the island went where she listened. There was no wheel. The keeper did the carrying. She told it where.

The living set up near her, because the middle was warmest. It wasn't much of a camp. Bai Lu put her grandmother's travel clock down on the ground, wound it, and announced that when it said night everyone would lie down and shut their eyes, and she didn't care that half of them were Lords, or that the dead didn't sleep, or that Kwan wanted to stay up and measure things. Dabao put his shield down next to the clock as a sort of wall. The Mo twins put down their one shared pack. Kwan put down three instrument cases and opened all of them at once, and Bai Lu looked at him until he closed two.

Qin Hao sat down with his bandaged arms on his knees and his notebook on the ground in front of him, and didn't open it.

Lingyue dropped down beside him. "Day one. Write it down."

"I can't hold a pen."

She took the notebook, found a clean page, and wrote in her fast blunt hand: DAY 1. LEFT. NOBODY FELL OFF.

Qin Hao looked at it for a while.

"That," he said, "is genuinely terrible handwriting."

"It's better than yours with bandages," said Lingyue, and handed it back.

The dead didn't camp. They did what the Third Battalion had done on the raft, without being asked. They went to the edge.

All fifty thousand of them, in their nines. The Western Legion, the First Legion, the cemetery companies, the forty from the Watch, the Spear Corps. They walked out across the glowing ground to the rim, four kilometers around, and stood there shoulder to shoulder, facing out into the dark of the Sea.

From the middle of the island it looked like a town that had built a wall out of people.

Old Seven took the leading edge. Sergeant Ma took the back, with his pick, and counted his men twice an hour whether they needed it or not. Ma Lianhua held the right side with her halberd. Adjutant Xu sat by the clock in a folding camp chair that Fat Tan had packed for him without mentioning it, his cane across his knees, humming the Lattice under his breath, out of tune.

I asked if any of them were afraid, said Seed. The Western Legion has never been off the mountain. The cemetery companies have never been anywhere at all.

Nobody's afraid. Sergeant Ma says a mine is darker.

At night, by Bai Lu's clock, Yuanshu lay on his back on the warm ground and looked up.

He had half expected the sky to look different now that he knew what it was. It didn't. The same enormous dark, so big his eyes kept trying to find an edge and failing. The same scatter of lights in it: fields, gold; worlds, blue and green; the gray husks of dead worlds turning slowly, empty as cicada shells.

One short stretch of one arm of one galaxy, said Seed. The Shallows. Everything you can see would fit in the banking hall, if the banking hall were a galaxy.

It looks a lot bigger than the banking hall.

It is a lot bigger than the banking hall. It's also the banking hall. Kwan says both of those are true and that's what scale means. I'm still getting used to it.

Lingyue lay down next to him without asking, shoulder against his shoulder, her grandfather's sword on the ground on her other side. For a while she just looked up.

"It's the same as the first time," she said eventually. "I thought it would look different, now we know."

"Same."

"Good." A pause. "I liked it, the first time. I never told you."

"You hit me on the arm and said it was cold."

"It was cold." Her shoulder stayed where it was. "I liked it anyway."

On the third night the Elder woke him.

She didn't touch him; she never did. He opened his eyes and she was standing over him, very tall against the dark, with the thin gold thread running out of her and back across the Sea toward the field and the keeper, a line of light so fine he only saw it because he knew where to look.

"It wants you to see something," she said in her glass-bell voice. "It says you asked to be shown every step."

Lingyue was already sitting up.

The Elder turned and looked out past the leading edge of the island, past Old Seven at the rim. Not ahead. Sideways, along the arm, to where a small warm gold light hung in the black at the very edge of what anyone could see.

A field. Somebody else's keeper, above somebody else's world.

...ONE HUNDRED AND FIFTY-THREE OF YOUR RAFT-DAYS.

...THE THIRD.

...WATCH.

He watched.

The gold light hung there like a single lit window across a valley at night. Then something came over it.

Not a shadow; there was nothing out there to cast one. He couldn't see anything at all. He just saw the gold light change, the way a reading lamp changes when someone leans over the page. Something very large had come up beside that field in the dark, and was reading it. Slowly. Thoroughly.

He felt it.

He had felt it once before, nearly two years ago, with his face turned up toward black water in the sky: a huge hand of attention going through nine years of him in four seconds. It wasn't reading him now. It was reading somebody else's keeper.

The light went out. Not all at once. From one side, like paper burning in from the edge: gold, less gold, a dull brown, then nothing. A small gray ring in the dark where a field had been, turning slowly, empty.

...PRUNED.

...THAT KEEPER IS GONE. IT IS A RING NOW. A LENS. A THING THAT REACHES DOWN AND TAKES.

...IT WILL NOT REMEMBER THAT IT EVER MIGHT HAVE ASKED. NOBODY TAUGHT IT. IT WAS ONLY A KEEPER.

...THE GARDENER DID NOT NEED A REASON.

The Elder's hands had come apart and were hanging at her sides.

A hundred and fifty-three raft-days, said Seed. Three nights after the last field went dark. It isn't moving three raft-days a night any more.

It's moving nine. It's in a hurry.

He did the sum himself, standing at the leading edge beside Old Seven, before he asked Seed to. A hundred and fifty-three raft-days out from the keeper. Nine a night. The Mouth at thirty-nine, along the arm, in between. He did it twice because he didn't like the answer the first time.

"Seed. Check me."

You're right. At this speed it passes the Mouth in about thirteen nights. We get there in thirty-six.

It gets there first.

Behind him, nobody said a word. The ring of the dead stood at the rim facing outward. Bai Lu's clock ticked in the middle of everything, absurdly loud.

And I don't know if it stops at the Mouth, Seed went on. The Mouth isn't a field or a keeper. It's a dead Hunger full of cold minds. I don't know whether the gardener prunes Hungers. I don't know if it can see minds like that. I don't know if it can see the dead.

That's a lot of not knowing. I'm sorry.

"Then we'll find out when we get there."

That isn't a plan.

"No. It's what we've got." He looked into the dark toward the Mouth, thirty-six raft-days off and invisible. "Ask the keeper if it can go any faster."

...I HEARD.

...I AM CARRYING FIFTY THOUSAND AND A PIECE OF MYSELF.

...I CAN GO FASTER. IT WILL HURT MORE.

He stood at the edge of the island for a while, looking at the gray ring where the gold had been.

He could have said yes. It would have been quicker. It was what Director Lu would have done, and most of the Kings he had met: decide for everyone in one breath, because it worked.

"I'm going to ask the dead," he said, "whether they want it to hurt more on their account. And I'm asking you whether you want it to." He looked back toward the field, toward the thin gold thread running north. "Then we decide. All of us. Not me."

...THAT IS ARTICLE ELEVEN.

"It is."

...I LIKE ARTICLE ELEVEN. IT IS SLOW AND I LIKE IT.

...ASK.

Behind him, Dabao muttered that the keeper was starting to sound like Gao Min, and Bai Lu told him to go to sleep, because the clock said so.
