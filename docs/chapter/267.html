Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 267Down the Thread

czverse10 min read

Summer went, and the Candle's children learned to wear shoes.

It took most of them a month. Gao Min said so at the long table, with the ledger open at a column she'd headed Shoes. The pressing had stopped their bodies at twelve; it hadn't stopped what eleven or nineteen or forty years of lying in a wall did to the way a person stood. They walked on the sides of their feet. They flinched at stairs. They wouldn't sit with their backs to a door. And they held hands everywhere, in the street, in the market, on the Number Nine, in a line, each holding on to the one in front.

Nobody in Lingzhou told them to stop. After a while, Fat Tan said, people in Lingzhou started doing it too. Not everyone. Some. In queues. On the stairs of the old quarter. The way you stand when you want to do something and are frightened.

Autumn came, and the children went to school.

Not the Academy. A school in the old customs house on the river, which the Bureau gave them and Gao Min ran, with teachers who'd volunteered from all over the city and a rule written on the door in Xiaoyu's capitals that nobody inside was ever to be asked their grade by anyone. They learned to read Huaxian. They learned that the sky was blue because of something to do with light, which most of them refused to believe. They learned to swim in the river, in the shallows by the old ferry steps, and some of them wouldn't come out.

And one by one, through the autumn, they went back to the stone.

Not all. Some never did. Some went once and said I don't know and went again a month later and said it again; Gao Min said that was allowed and wrote it down. Some said what they wanted and Wen Jia opened it. Healer. Swimmer. Teacher. Something that makes things grow. One boy said he wanted to be a bus driver, and Wen Hao, who was in the hall at the time because he was always in the hall at the time, had to go and sit on the steps outside for half an hour.

Tam went every week. He didn't touch it. He stood at the foot of the platform and listened.

Winter came, and Lingyue was seven months pregnant and furious about it.

Not about the child. About the stairs. About Bai Lu, who'd forbidden her to fly, to fight, or to carry her grandfather's sword, which weighed a hundred kilograms and which she'd carried everywhere since eighteen. About the light, which had come back from underneath and wouldn't stop coming, so that some mornings she woke to find the whole back room glowing faintly pale like the inside of a shell, and had to sit on the edge of the bed and breathe until it went down.

"It's the baby," she said at breakfast, over a bowl of rice porridge; she ate two now. "Bai Lu says it's pulling on it. Like the moon on water."

"Is that bad?"

"Bai Lu says it's never happened to anyone before. A Light Law this deep in somebody pregnant." She ate. "So she doesn't know."

I'm counting it, said Seed. The heart. A hundred and forty-four this morning. It's been going down slowly, the way Bai Lu says it should.

I ask it every morning. It doesn't answer. It kicked yesterday when I asked. Lingyue says that was a coincidence. I'm writing it down anyway.

"It was a coincidence," said Shen Lingyue.

Every night at one Xiaoyu went to the Awakening Hall and asked the Collector what it wanted.

Every night it came: warm, patient, from very far away, down the thread out of the deep water, to rest on the hall and on the girl on the platform under her own three lines of light. Every night it said it didn't know yet. That it still wanted to go home to the empty house and find out whose the first want was. That it was still coming, slowly. That it would like someone to ask the boy, when it arrived, whether he'd come. Every night Xiaoyu said tomorrow, and the Collector said yes, that is the rule.

And then it told her things.

About worlds whose wants it had kept. A world of rivers where everyone had wanted to reach the sea. A world of mountains where everyone had wanted to be the last one standing at the top. The Architects, who'd wanted to be forgiven and still did; it had kept that a very long time and was beginning to wonder whether it should give it back. The Lamplit, who wanted to be first.

On the hundred and forty-third night it told her about the Singers.

I kept a great many of their wants. More than anyone's except the Lamplit. I did not have to ask for theirs either. They sang them to me. They sing them to everything that comes near them, and have for longer than the Lamplit have existed.

Every one the same. To be chosen.

Xiaoyu didn't write anything for a moment.

"Chosen for what?"

For the tithe. Every child on their world is taught before it can speak that the brightest thing a person can be is chosen. Dressed in light. Sung up the thread. Taken. So that the rest are spared.

They want it more than anything. More than living. More than their mothers. I kept a great many of them. They were the brightest wants I ever kept. They burned like stars.

I did not think about what they were for. I am thinking about it now.

The mark came on the hundred and fifty-first day after the Candle folded, at four in the morning, and Wen Jia nearly dropped it.

She said so through the staff at a quarter past four, so loudly that he sat bolt upright and Lingyue woke and the back room went pale for a moment like the inside of a shell.

...captain's boy. CAPTAIN'S BOY. Something came DOWN.

He was in the Awakening Hall by twenty to five in a coat over his sleeping clothes, with the staff, Old Seven behind him, and Kwan, who hadn't been asleep, with the laptop, and Gao Min, who had a key and lived four streets away and said afterward she'd heard him running.

The hall was dark and empty: the three lines of light over the platform, the warm stone with the blue jacket on its shoulder, its runes all pointing down as they had since the night before Awakening Day.

Except they weren't. They were pointing up.

...it came down the thread. My thread. The one that goes up. The one the marks used to go up, and the one that pulled, and the one that went quiet when the thing at the end stopped.

...nothing's ever come DOWN it. Not once in forty-two years. Things go up. That's what it's for.

...and at four o'clock something came down it and landed in my knot like a stone in a bucket. A mark.

"Whose?" said Yuanshu.

...I don't know. I can't read it. It's not mine. It's not ours. It came from very far. Farther than the old man's thread, farther than the docker's. From the other side of the thing that stopped.

...it's tied like... like by someone who's never tied anything. Clumsy. Like a child's knot. But it's a mark. A real one. It's MEANT.

May I look? said Seed.

...yes. Please. Before I drop it.

Seed looked. He felt it go out from above his eyes through the staff into the warm black stone, down through the tree of Laws to the knot at the bottom where a girl of seventeen sat holding, as you'd hold something small and hot that's just been thrown to you, a mark.

It's clumsy. She's right. Whoever tied it has never tied a mark before. They tied it the way you'd tie a knot if you'd read about knots and never seen one. But it's tied right.

It came from a stone. Not one of ours. A stone very far away on the other side of the stopped knot, up its own thread into the web, through the place where the one who picks sits with its hands open reading nothing. And down ours.

Someone's found out that the threads still go both ways. That with nothing at the center reading, a mark sent up one stone's thread can cross and come down another's.

Someone's sent us a mark.

"Can you read it?"

Yes. It's in the Federation's language, with no grades in it. I didn't know you could write the Federation's language without grades. It took me a moment.

THE ARRAYS ARE OPEN.

THIRTY-TWO THOUSAND AND FORTY. HOME. EVERY ONE. THEIR NAMES WERE READ IN THE ASSEMBLY.

THE HOUSE STRUCK MY NAME FROM THE LADDER. I CROSSED OUT MY FACE IN FRONT OF THEM. MY MOTHER LEFT THE CHAMBER.

SEVEN HOUSES HAVE OPENED THEIR ARRAYS SINCE. ELEVEN SHIPS STAND IN THE YARDS AT ANTHOS WITH NO CORES. THEY CANNOT FOLD. THE YARDS ARE FULL OF CHILDREN LEARNING TO WEAR SHOES.

THE OTHER HOUSES ARE AFRAID. THE ASSEMBLY HAS VOTED. NINETY SHIPS. THE GREAT HOUSES. THEY WILL COME TO THE SHALLOWS IN THE SPRING TO SEE THE WORLD THAT STOPPED THE REAPER AND EMPTIED THE ARRAYS.

THEY WILL NOT ASK.

I FOUND THAT THE THREADS GO BOTH WAYS. NOBODY HERE KNEW. NOBODY HAD EVER TRIED TO SEND ANYTHING DOWN.

THIS IS MY FIRST MARK. I AM SORRY IT IS CLUMSY. THE STONE ON ANTHOS HAS NOBODY IN IT. I TIED IT MYSELF WITH MY HAND ON THE STONE. IT READ ME WHILE I DID. IT SAID SSS. I TOLD IT IT WAS WRONG.

MAELIS SAYS: TELL THE BOY IT HAS BEEN FIVE MONTHS. SEVEN MORE. SHE SAYS HE HAD BETTER BE THERE.

— I.

Nobody in the hall spoke for a long time.

Then Kwan said quietly, at the laptop: "The threads go both ways."

"Yes."

"Every stone in every orchard in the deep water is on the web. Thousands of worlds. And the thing in the middle's stopped reading. So it isn't a web any more. It's—" She didn't have the word.

A net, said Seed. Not a tree. A net. Every stone tied to every other through the knot at the center, with nobody reading. A mark can go in anywhere and come out anywhere.

Like the dead.

Gao Min had sat down on the bottom tier with her reading glasses in her hand, not putting them on.

"A mark from Anthos," she said. "To here. In—" She looked at the clock over the doors. "However long it took. Not folds. Not months. A mark."

I don't know how long it took. It was sent five months after she got home and it arrived this morning. It might have taken five months. It might have taken a second, and she only worked out how to send it yesterday.

I think a second. I think the net's fast. I think it was always fast. It was built to carry marks to something that read a billion a second.

He stood in the dark hall at the foot of the platform with the staff in his hand.

Ninety ships, in the spring. The Great Houses of the Federation of the Lamplit Worlds, every ship made of cores, coming to the Shallows to look at the world that had stopped the Reaper and emptied the arrays. And not to ask.

And a net under everything: every stone on every world in every orchard in the deep water, tied together through a knot at the center where the oldest thing anyone had ever made sat with its hands open. A net that a Listener-Captain with her name struck from the ladder had found, alone, with her hand on a stone that told her she was SSS while she told it it was wrong.

Seed. Can we send one back?

Yes. Wen Jia can. She has the knot and she knows how. She's been holding her breath six months.

What do you want to say?

He thought about it. Then he turned and looked up the dark aisle and saw he wasn't alone. In the side door, in a coat over her sleeping clothes, hair sticking up, stood his sister, with the marker in her hand. Already uncapped.

"I heard you running," said Xiaoyu. "Like Gao Min." She came down the aisle. "What are we saying?"

They sent it at six in the morning. Xiaoyu wrote it on the platform floor in front of the stone, as she'd written THE HOUSE IS EMPTY, so that it was true. Wen Jia tied it, not clumsily, as Hush had shown her months ago when she asked how, and sent it. Up her thread into the net, across through the knot at the center where nobody read anything now, and down, in what was probably a second, into a stone on a world called Anthos, where nobody sat in the knot, where a captain with her name struck from the ladder had put her hand and told it it was wrong.

RECEIVED. THE THREADS GO BOTH WAYS. YOU ARE THE FIRST.

WE WILL BE HERE IN THE SPRING. WE WILL LIGHT THE LAMPS.

TELL MAELIS: I WILL BE HERE.

TELL HER: SEVEN MORE MONTHS. THEN ASK ME AGAIN.

— BLUE STAR. UNLISTED ORCHARD 4471. WHICH ASKS.

Then, after a moment, smaller, because she said it had to go on somewhere and nobody else had thought of it, Xiaoyu added one more line:

ALSO: THE SINGERS ARE COMING. TAM CAN HEAR THEM. THEY ARE VERY CLOSE NOW. THEY ARE SINGING TO BE CHOSEN.
