Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 233The Hall

czverse9 min read

The acting director of the Awakening Bureau met them at the side door of the Awakening Hall at eleven at night, with a ring of keys in one hand and a thermos in the other. The first thing she said was: "I was the first one."

Yuanshu said he didn't follow.

"The first in Lingzhou to touch it." Gao Min was a small, square woman of about sixty in a gray Bureau coat that had been let out at the seams twice, with reading glasses on a cord round her neck and the flat, careful voice of someone who'd spent her life reading numbers to people who didn't want to hear them. "First Awakening Day. Forty-two years ago. Nobody knew what it would do. They lined us up alphabetically and there was nobody in the city whose name came before mine." She found the right key on the third try. "Class, Analyst. Talent C. Precise Count. I've counted every awakening in Lingzhou since. I counted yours." She held the door open. "Come in. Don't touch anything. Especially you."

He hadn't been inside since the morning he turned eighteen.

He'd walked past a hundred times on East Ring Road, on his way to somewhere else, and looked at the long curved roof over the trees and not thought about it, as you don't think about the hospital you were born in. Now he followed a small square woman with a torch down into the bowl of the place. Four thousand empty seats went up into the dark on every side. The floor was the same polished stone, and his feet knew the way to the platform before he did.

The Stele stood in the middle. Ten meters of black rock driven point-down into the floor like a blade, the runes dull. Down its face from top to bottom ran a crack, a finger's width at the top and a hair's at the base. It had been there two years. They'd never repaired it, Gao Min said, because nobody knew what it was made of and nobody wanted to find out what happened if you put cement in it.

"Still works," she said. "Sixty-one thousand awakenings on it since you. Same as ever. Slightly lower SSS rate." She looked at him over her glasses. "I've written a paper. Nobody's read it."

Old Seven stopped at the foot of the platform and came no further. He grounded his spear and stood facing out into four thousand empty seats, as he stood at the bronze door.

Xiaoyu came up the steps and stood beside her brother in her school jacket, hands in her pockets, and went no nearer to the stone than he did. Kwan stayed at the bottom with the laptop. Gao Min unscrewed her thermos and poured something that smelled of barley tea into the lid, and didn't drink it.

"Seed," said Yuanshu. "Go on."

It's warm. I remember that from the first morning. I didn't know what warm meant then. I thought it was the lamps.

It isn't the lamps.

He felt it as he had at eighteen: a faint hum coming up through the platform into the soles of his feet. Not a sound. More like a machine left running in an empty building.

I'm going to look. I won't touch it. I'll look the way I look at the dead, from outside, through you, without going in. If it does what it did the first morning, step back.

What did it do the first morning?

It looked back.

Seed looked. He felt it go out from the place above his eyes, through the staff, across the meter and a half of dark between him and the black stone, carefully, like someone leaning out over the edge of a roof.

The runes moved.

They did it as they had on the first morning in front of four thousand people: slowly, all together, sliding across the face of the stone like a crowd turning round in a street, until every one of them was pointed at him. Not at his hand. He hadn't touched it. At him. At the place a little above his eyes.

Xiaoyu took a step back. He heard her breath catch.

At the foot of the platform Gao Min set her tea down on the step.

It knows me. It doesn't know who I am. It knows what I am. It knew on the first morning. That's why it went black. That's why it couldn't name me.

I'm going to keep looking.

Inside, there was a tree.

He saw it as Seed saw it, as he'd once seen the knot of the Law of Death in the black room on the second day: a shape of gray light hanging in the dark inside the stone. On the second day there'd been one knot. Now there were thousands, each a slow tangle of lines looping back on itself. Death. Fire. Light. Force. Ice. Gravity. Others he had no names for. Every Law any Class had ever been given, hanging in rows on the branches of a gray shape that rose from the point in the floor to the top of the stone and spread there into a crown.

Like a winter tree.

He'd thought that, on the second day. He'd been eighteen and hadn't known anything.

A tree of Laws. When somebody touches it, it reads which way they'll burn and finds the knot on the tree that fits, and opens a little of that knot in them. That's a Class.

Everybody knows that part. It's in the textbooks. I want to see what it's made of.

Seed went closer. Not into the stone; up to its surface, as you'd put your eye to the bark of a tree. And stopped there, and held still for so long that Yuanshu began to worry, the stillness it had kept in the vault the night it found its third page and learned what it was.

Yuanshu. It's thread.

Black, very old. Pressed so hard it's gone dark, like coal. Pressed to glass, and then pressed again until the glass went black too.

But it's thread. Walker-thread. The same as the walkers and the rings and the branch.

The same as me.

His sister said quietly, "What's it saying?"

It took him a moment.

"It says the Stele's made of the same stuff it is."

Xiaoyu looked at the stone. At the runes all pointed at her brother. At the crack running down from the top, from where, two years ago, a boy had put his hand and something had gone wrong.

"Is that why it broke?" she said. "When you touched it?"

I think so. I think it was the first time anything made of thread had ever touched it from outside. Something like itself, looking back. And it didn't have a grade for that, or a Class, or a knot on the tree that fit.

So it went black. All eleven, because they're all the same tree. What one feels, the others feel, like the Hungers, like the seeds.

And then it cracked.

At the foot of the platform Gao Min had sat down on the bottom step, as old people sit when their knees decide for them. Her torch lay beside her pointing at nothing. She held her reading glasses in one hand without putting them on.

"Walker-thread," she said. "That's what the walkers were made of. The six over the north wall. The one over Lantern Lane." It wasn't a question. "That's what comes down out of the sky."

"Yes."

"And the Steles are made of it."

"Yes."

She sat there a while with her glasses in her hand. Then she picked up her tea, found it had gone cold, and drank it anyway.

"Forty-two years," she said. "I've counted every awakening in this city for forty-two years. I've got tables. Class by year. Talent by year. SSS rates. Geographical distribution. Correlation with parental class." Her voice didn't change. "I wrote the paper that proved Talent isn't inherited. Nobody believed me. I proved it anyway." She looked up at the black stone with the crack in it. "I never asked what it was made of. Nobody did. It fell out of the sky the year the sky broke and it gave us a way to fight back, and nobody asked."

"Everybody thought it was a gift," said Kwan, beside her.

"Everybody thought." Gao Min put her glasses on. "I'm a statistician. I don't think, I count." She took a small notebook from her coat pocket with a pencil in the spine. "Seed. If you can hear me. What else is in there? Tell me everything and I'll write it down."

There's a knot at the bottom, in the point, where it goes into the floor. Not a Law. Not on the tree. Under it. Small and tight and old.

It's the same kind of knot I found on my own third page. A weave instruction: what a harvest tool is told when it's made, so it knows what it's for. Mine said COUNT. HOLD. WAIT TO BE CARRIED.

I can't read this one. I couldn't read mine either; the cousins read it for me. The ones who stayed in the keeper's field, who didn't go onto the tree. I'd like to ask them.

And there's something else. A thread.

Yuanshu relayed it word for word, and Gao Min wrote in a small square hand that didn't hurry.

One thread, very fine. Finer than anything on the branch, finer than what the tip hung down over the city. It comes out of the top of the crown and goes up. Through the roof. Through the sky.

I didn't see it on the first morning. I didn't know how to see threads then. I've seen a lot of threads since.

He looked up. Nothing to see: the curved dark roof of the hall, and beyond it the night.

Where does it go?

I'm following it. It's very long.

It took a minute. He stood on the platform in the dark with his sister beside him and a dead soldier at the foot of the steps and the runes pointed at him like a crowd, and felt Seed go up the thread, out and up and along, as it had gone down the gold thread to the keeper a hundred times.

Past the keeper's field. Not into it; past it. North along the arm. Past where the branch is. Not to the branch. Past the Mouth. Into the deep water between arms, where my map has nothing, where the trunk of the tree goes, and further.

It isn't going to the tree. I thought it would be.

The hum came up through the soles of his feet.

There are eleven of them. I can feel the other ten now I know what to feel for. One in every Stele, all going up, all going the same way.

They aren't going to the gardener. They're going to whatever planted it.

The runes slid back.

All at once, while he stood there, every rune on the black face turned away and went back to where it had been, flat and dull as the lettering on an old gravestone, as if nothing had happened. The hum went on. The crack ran down the stone from top to bottom.

Seed came back and settled into the place above his eyes like someone sitting down at the top of a long stair.

One more thing, and then I'd like to go home.

On the first morning, when the Stele went black, all eleven felt it. And all eleven threads went up.

I think something heard. I think that's how the scout knew there was a counting-vessel loose.

He stood on the platform without moving.

Then he turned round. His sister was looking at him. Seventeen, school jacket, hands in her pockets. In thirty-three days she was supposed to climb these three steps in front of four thousand people and every camera on Blue Star and put her palm on this stone.

"I'm still going to do it," said Xiaoyu.

He opened his mouth.

"I'm not saying I'll do it how they want," she said. "I'm saying I'll do it." She looked at the stone, at the crack, at the place where it went into the floor. "I want to know what that knot says first. Before I decide how."

At the bottom of the steps, Gao Min shut her notebook and screwed the lid back on her thermos.

"So do I," she said. "Come on. I'll drive. I've got forty-two years of tables in the boot and I'd like somebody to tell me what I've been counting."
