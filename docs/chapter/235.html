Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 235Tell First

czverse8 min read

They told the world from the floor of the Awakening Hall at eight in the evening, with the cracked black stone behind them.

Gao Min chose the place. If you were going to tell everyone on Blue Star that the thing they'd all put their hands on at eighteen was a grading stone for an orchard, she said, you should do it standing in front of the stone, so people could see you weren't afraid to. She said this in the car on the way, with her nine boxes on the back seat, and then added in the same flat voice: "I am afraid to. I'd like that on the record too."

There was one camera, and Kwan ran it. No drones, no audience, no officials in the private boxes, no recruiters. Four thousand seats went up empty into the dark. Old Seven stood at the foot of the platform with his back to the stone, facing out. Xiaoyu sat on the top step with the marker in her lap, because she'd asked to be there, and because, she said, somebody in the family who'd have to walk up those steps ought to be sitting on them.

Lingyue stood beside Yuanshu with her grandfather's sword. On her back this time, not as a cane.

"You don't have to," he'd said in the car.

"I'm the SSS," she'd said. "They should see one."

Gao Min went first, and didn't make a speech.

She stood in front of the camera in her gray coat with the let-out seams, held up the long taped graph, and said: "My name is Gao Min. I'm the acting director of the Awakening Bureau of Lingzhou. I was the first person in this city to touch this Stele, forty-two years ago, alphabetically. I've counted every awakening here since. This is the mean Talent grade by year." She drew her finger along the pencil line. "It goes up. Smoothly. Every year. Through a famine, two wars, the Black Tide, and a decade when half the schools in the city were shut. Nutrition isn't smooth. Training isn't smooth. Something has been turning it up." She lowered the graph. "Last week we found out what."

Then she stepped aside, and he told them.

All of it. He'd decided on the vault steps that he'd leave nothing out, because on the morning of the Stele, two years ago, he'd decided he'd never hide what he was, and this was what he was: a boy with a harvest tool in his head, standing in front of another one. The thread, black, pressed to glass. The tree of Laws inside the stone. The knot at the bottom; he read it himself, every line, from READ WHAT RIPENS to WHEN THE ORCHARD IS BRIGHT, THE PICKER COMES. The fine thread going up out of the crown into the deep water. The first morning, and what had gone up the thread that day.

He didn't tell them what to do. Article Eleven said tell them what's known, and what's known was the knot and the graph, not an answer.

"We don't know yet what we'll do about this," he said. "In thirty days everyone who turns eighteen this year is meant to put a hand on one of these. You should know what it is before they do."

He looked at the small red light on the camera.

"Talent isn't who you are," said Zhou Yuanshu. "It's how bright something wants you to burn. That's all it ever was."

Lingyue spoke last. She hadn't told him she was going to.

She stepped into the light with the sword on her back and took from her coat pocket a rectangle of laminated plastic and held it up. Her identity card: the Shen crest, her photograph at eighteen, unsmiling, and under it in gold, as they printed it on every Huaxian card above an A, SSS. DAWNBREAKER.

She took a pen from her other pocket. In front of the camera, slowly, without a word, she drew a line through the gold.

Then she put the card away and stepped back beside him. That was all she did.

By midnight half of Blue Star had seen it.

Kwan counted. She sat in the back room of the Ossuary with the laptop and three phones, counting, as she did when she didn't know what else to do with her hands, and read things out as they came in. After a while nobody asked her to stop.

In Port Abiri a fisherman's son who'd awakened a C eleven years ago stood up in a harbor bar and read the whole knot aloud off his phone, then sat down and cried, and nobody laughed at him.

In Yamato three thousand people climbed the terrace steps to the mountain shrine in the dark with candles and sat down among the hollow monks without being asked. Shirasagi Ren sent one line through Concordia: The Abbot says: it reads without asking. So did I. Tell them it can be stopped.

In the Northern Union, by morning, the Stele at Novaya Zarya was ringed with soldiers in white facing outward, and Marshal Orlov said on the radio, in one sentence, that they were there to stop anybody touching it until somebody had decided something, and that included him.

In the Atlantic Federation, Roman Sterling said nothing for six hours. Then: "Nobody touches them. Nobody breaks them. We vote. That's all I've got."

And in Lingzhou, on East Ring Road, people began to arrive outside the hall.

He'd been afraid of a mob. Gao Min rang at one in the morning and said only you should see this, and he went down with Old Seven and stood at the corner by the trees, and it wasn't a mob.

It was parents.

Hundreds of them, on the steps, on the pavement, on the grass under the trees. Some had candles; most didn't. Mothers and fathers with coats pulled on over pajamas, standing outside a locked building in the middle of the night, not shouting, not doing anything. Standing as people stand outside a hospital.

And their children, seventeen, eighteen in a month, in school jackets and tracksuits. Some still had the training swords and practice staves they'd been drilling with that evening, as they'd drilled every evening of their lives, for this.

A man saw him and came across the grass. Mid-forties, big, with a laborer's hands. A tall boy behind him with the same jaw, holding a wooden training spear as you hold a thing you've held every day for twelve years.

"You're him," said the man.

"Yes."

"My son's trained since he was six." His voice wasn't loud. It was held very carefully, like a full bowl being carried across a room. "Every morning. Five o'clock, before school. Spear forms. Because he wanted to be an S. Because an S gets into the Defense Academy, and the Academy pays, and I work in the coal yard and his mother cleans at the hospital, and that's the only way out of the yard there is." He didn't look away. "Thirty days. Twelve years of five in the morning, for thirty days from now. What do I tell him?"

The boy stared at the ground.

Yuanshu didn't know, and said so.

"I don't know. I'm sorry. I don't know yet. When I do, you'll hear it first."

The man looked at him for a while. Then he nodded once, as men in coal yards nod when they've been told the truth and it isn't good.

"That's not much," he said.

"No."

"It's more than the rock told us." He turned and went back across the grass to the steps and stood with the others, a hand on his son's shoulder.

The boy looked back once: at Old Seven, the tall dead captain of the Eastern Line at the corner of the trees, spear grounded in a finger and a thumb. He lifted his wooden spear a little. Not high. The way one soldier greets another in a corridor.

Old Seven brought his spear down on the pavement, once.

At twenty past four Seed woke him.

He was asleep on the narrow bed in the back room with Lingyue against his shoulder, and Seed woke him as it hadn't since the night of the three gray rings: not with words but with a feeling, a cold place opening in his chest, as if a door had swung open somewhere far off and let the winter in.

Port Halvard. The Stele at Port Halvard. Someone touched it.

He was sitting up before he knew it. Who?

I don't know. A man. Young, but older than eighteen. He didn't put his hand on it.

He hit it.

Seed showed him what it had, which wasn't much: a shape felt down the fine thread that ran up from the crown of the Port Halvard Stele to join the others in the dark. Night. A square hall. Soldiers asleep by the door. A young man who'd come in through a window with a sledgehammer, who stood in front of the black stone, said something nobody heard, and swung.

He was an F. I can see the mark the Stele sent up for him, seven years ago. F. The lowest it gives.

It read him again when the hammer hit, because the hammer was him touching it. And it did what it was made to do. It found which way he'd burn and opened him that way.

Not a little. All the way.

Seed didn't show him the rest, but he felt it anyway, like a bell rung in the next room: a man in a square hall in Port Halvard with a hammer in his hands, burning. Brighter than an F, brighter than an S, brighter than he'd ever been, for nine seconds. The best thing he'd ever felt.

Then the fine thread out of the crown went taut, and he went up it.

He's gone. Not through the door. I'd have felt that; I know the door. Up.

It read him and opened him and he burned, and it sent him. Not the mark. Him.

I didn't know it could do that. I don't think anyone knew. I think it's never been hit before.

Lingyue was awake, sitting up beside him in the dark with her hand on his arm.

"What," she said.

He told her.

She was quiet a long time. Then she reached across to the chair where her coat hung, took the laminated card out of the pocket, and held it in the dark, the gold struck through, turning it over and over in her fingers.

"So you can't break them," she said.

"No."

"And you can't touch them without being graded."

"No."

"And in thirty days," said Shen Lingyue, "your sister walks up to one in front of the whole world."

Twenty-nine, said Seed. It's past midnight. Twenty-nine days.
