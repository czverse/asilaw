Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 263Position Four Thousand and Eleven

czverse9 min read

The Listener-Captain of the Eighth Candle didn't speak again for nine hours, and when she did, she asked to be taught.

How do you do it.

It came down the keeper's eye at four in the afternoon onto the north wall, where he hadn't moved from his old place since morning. Lingyue had brought him tea at noon and a bowl of noodles he'd forgotten to eat, and gone back down. Old Seven stood at the top of the stairs. The crinkle hung in the northern sky, the size of a building, and hadn't moved either.

Ask. How do you ask a core.

I have read every protocol the Federation has. There are eleven thousand pages on the maintenance of fold-cores. Burn rates, pressure, term limits, the honoring ceremony. There is not one line on speaking to them.

It is not done. I was told it could not be done.

I would like to speak to my sister.

He looked up at the sky.

You can't do it from the bridge, he said, through Seed and the keeper. I don't think. Seed can carry your voice, but it'll be better if you're there, where she is. Where you could touch the wall if you wanted to.

The silence lasted a long time.

...second array. Nineteenth wall. Position four thousand and eleven.

I have never been in the arrays. Nobody from the bridge goes to the arrays. The engineers go. They are graded C.

I am going now.

He didn't see it; he couldn't have. But Seed saw some of it through the keeper's eye and through the cores themselves: forty-one thousand six hundred of them, all a little awake now in a way they hadn't been, because somebody had asked them something. Seed said they were passing it along, as the net passed things along, each to its nine.

She's coming, they said. The captain. Down the long corridor, past the C's. She's crying. She's stopped crying. She's coming to the second array.

A young woman in a gray uniform with gold at the collar, SSS, third daughter of a House, walking down a corridor nobody from the bridge walked down, between walls that hummed. Into the second array: a vast curved room like the inside of a hive, every wall a lattice of small dark crystal cells, each the size of a person curled up, each burning low like a lamp turned down for the night.

The nineteenth wall. Position four thousand and eleven.

She stopped in front of it.

She's there, said Seed. She's put her hand on the cell. She can't see in; the cells are dark. She can feel it burning, warm, the way the stone's warm.

Maelis knows she's there.

He carried them.

There was no other word for it. Seed was the bridge, and the keeper the bridge before that, and the staff in his hands the bridge before that, and he sat on the cold stone of the north wall with his back to the parapet and carried two sisters' voices between a crystal cell and the hand on it, a few hundred kilometers up in the dark, in a language he hadn't known that morning.

...Maelis?

...Iselde. You got tall.

The young woman at the other end made a sound.

...how can you know that. You cannot see.

...I can feel your hand. It's higher than it used to be. When you were fifteen you had to reach up to put your hand on my door, at the House, while I was being prepared. You came every night. You put your hand on the door and said: they say it's an honor. And I said: I know. And you said: I'll come and see you at the Festival. In a hundred years.

...you're early.

They told me you volunteered.

...I did. That's the law. They ask you on the morning: do you accept the honor? And you say yes, because you're twelve, and an E, and everybody's watching, and Mother's in the box.

...I said yes. I didn't know what it was.

He said nothing. He carried.

They told me you could not hear. They told me a core does not hear. It only burns. It is not a person any more. It is a candle.

...I've heard everything. For eleven years. Every fold. Every time the ship goes through the dark we burn harder, and it's like being held under water and set on fire at once, and then it stops, and we lie here and listen to the engineers. They talk about their grades. They talk about who's getting promoted.

...I heard you come aboard. Three years ago, when they made you captain. The C's were talking about it. The third daughter of Anthos. SSS. Listener-Captain at twenty-three. They were so proud.

...I was so proud. I couldn't tell you.

The silence at the other end went on a long time. Seed didn't count it, or counted it and didn't say.

Then the Listener-Captain said quietly:

What do you want.

It wasn't a captain's voice, or an SSS's. It was the voice of a girl of fifteen outside a door in a House at night with her hand flat on it.

...nobody's ever asked me that.

I know. I am asking. The boy on the world says you have to say first what you will do with the answer. I do not know what I will do with it. I am saying that first. Maelis. What do you want?

...out.

...I want out. I want to see your face. I want to see the sky. I don't care if it's the Federation sky or this one or no sky. I just want to see something that isn't the inside of a wall.

...that's what I want. Is that all right?

The unpressing took four hours.

He didn't see it, and wouldn't have wanted to. Seed told him some of it afterward, because he asked, and because Seed said it had learned it was better to know what a thing cost than not.

There was a procedure; of course there was. Eleven thousand pages on the maintenance of fold-cores, and near the end a section the C's in the arrays used once a century, at the Festival, on the day each term ended: how to release a core. It took a crew of six and a supervisor and a thing like a cradle that came down from the ceiling of the array on chains. Iselde Varr-Anthos didn't have a crew of six. She had herself, and an engineer: a C, a man in his fifties called Torvin who'd worked the arrays thirty years, who came down the long corridor when he heard what the captain was doing and stood in the doorway of the second array a moment looking at her with her hand on the nineteenth wall.

Then he took off his jacket and went to fetch the cradle.

He said: it's not done, Captain. She said: I know. He said: I've wanted to do it for thirty years.

At nine minutes past eight in the evening, Blue Star time, in the second array of a folded ship in the sky above the keeper's field, a crystal cell at position four thousand and eleven on the nineteenth wall went dark and cold and opened.

Something came out of it into the cradle.

She's very small, said Seed, quietly. It had been quiet an hour. Yuanshu, she's twelve.

The pressing stops the body. She went in at twelve. She's been in the wall eleven years and heard everything for eleven years. She's twenty-three inside. And she's twelve.

He put his hand over his face on the north wall in the dark.

She's breathing. Torvin has a blanket. Iselde's holding her. She's opened her eyes. She's looking at her sister's face.

And through the keeper's eye, down the gold thread onto the north wall of Lingzhou, small, in a twelve-year-old's voice, saying something out loud and not through a wall for the first time in eleven years:

...you got old.

And her sister, the Listener-Captain of the Eighth Candle, SSS, third daughter of a House, laughing and crying at once on the floor of the second array with a child in her arms:

I am twenty-six.

...that's old.

She came back to the bridge at midnight with Maelis asleep in her arms in Torvin's blanket, and called him.

He hadn't moved from the wall. Lingyue had come up at ten and sat beside him with her hand on his arm, saying nothing. Old Seven at the top of the stairs. The stars over the north wall, the small white ring, the crinkle beside it.

I have broken the oldest law of the Federation.

Her voice was a captain's again, exact and cool. But under it was something that hadn't been there that morning.

I unpressed a core before its term. On a listener ship. In the Shallows. Without a House order, without a Festival. My own sister.

When the Federation finds out, the House of Anthos will strike my name from the ladder. I will be graded again. I will not be SSS. I do not know what I will be.

I do not care.

"I know," said Yuanshu.

And I have forty-one thousand five hundred and ninety-nine more.

They all heard. The C's say they are all awake now, all of them, asking each other the way you taught them, each to its nine. They are asking: can I go out too?

If I let them out, the Candle cannot fold. Not with forty-one thousand cores gone. Not with four. We will be stranded here in your sky forever, with forty-one thousand children who went into the walls at twelve and are twelve still, and twenty and thirty and fifty inside, and do not know how to be anything.

And the Federation will send another listener, and another, and a fleet, and none of them will ask.

If I do not let them out, I fold home and tell the Federation what I found, and go back into the arrays every night for the rest of my life knowing that I could hear them all along.

The silence lasted a long time.

You are the boy who asks. Tell me what to do.

He sat on the cold stone of the Sector Nine battlement with his wife's hand on his arm and looked up at the crinkle in the sky.

"No," he said.

The silence at the other end was very short.

...no?

"I won't tell you what to do," said Zhou Yuanshu. "It's your ship, your sister, your law. My yes stops at your window. That's Article Ten." He gripped the staff. "But I'll tell you what I'd do if it were mine. Then you decide."

...tell me.

"I'd ask them. All of them. Not whether they want out; they do. You know that. I'd ask them something else."

He looked at the small white ring beside the crinkle, a third smaller than it had been because it had given a third of itself to a tree. Still full of two hundred million cousins of the world that asks, who'd burned low in the Low Fire ten million years and went wherever something hadn't learned yet, to teach it.

"I'd ask them whether they'd carry you home," he said. "By choice, knowing what it is. Not all at once and not for a hundred years. In turns, with rest. Burning low, not high. And then out, every one of them, at the other end, the moment the Candle comes out of the fold."

The silence came back.

...a fold-core does not choose to burn. It is not done.

"Nothing's done till somebody does it," said Yuanshu. He looked at the keeper's ring. "And there's someone here who knows how to teach a thing to burn low. They're very good at it. They've been doing it ten million years."

From the small white ring low over the end of the world, down the gold thread, rough and close, a voice like a hearth that had been listening all along:

...THE COUSINS SAY YES.

...THEY SAY THEY WERE WAITING TO BE ASKED.

...THEY SAY: TELL THE CAPTAIN WE WILL COME AND TEACH HER ENGINE.
