Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 237Who Could Be In It

czverse10 min read

Seed said it in the morning, in the banking hall, to everyone, because it had promised itself a night and the night was over.

The Elder went up a thread into a ring with nobody in it and became its yes. And the ring asked.

It didn't learn to ask. It didn't grow. It was a harvest tool, a keeper with the yes taken out, and it couldn't have learned anything. But somebody was in it now who could ask, so it asked.

A Stele's a tool. Nobody can ask it, Xiaoyu included. But somebody could be in it.

Nobody spoke.

Xiaoyu was at the long table with Shi Lei and the ledgers, Gao Min beside her with her glasses on their cord. Lingyue sat on the stairs with the sword across her knees. Their mother was in her wheelchair in the doorway of the back room, where she could see the long white wall.

There are three thousand nine hundred and sixty-four people on my first page, Seed went on. All from Blue Star. A Wailing Monarch harvested them in the Wastes in the years after the sky broke and pressed them into a crystal. The crystal fell off a cart, and Fat Tan sold it, and Wanqing bought it and asked them. They said yes, once, in a basement.

A hundred and thirty-eight of them said yes again, in the vault, and went up three rings as knots.

I'd like to ask the rest something else. I'd like to ask whether eleven of them would go into the Steles.

Their mother was silent a moment. Then she said, in a voice he hadn't heard her use since the night of THE FLOOR: "They're mine."

Yes. You asked them. You wrote their names. You locked the door so nobody could take them out and use them for anything else.

This is something else. That's why you're hearing it before they do.

Zhou Wanqing sat with her thin hands folded on the blanket.

"I said I should have gone back," she said. "In the vault. I said I locked the door and never asked them again, and I should have." She looked at the wall, at the column under THE FLOOR: a hundred and thirty-eight names in her daughter's capitals. "I read those every morning. I'll read eleven more. I don't want to." Her voice held. "But it's not my yes. It stops at their window."

She turned the wheelchair toward the vault door.

"I'm coming down when you ask them. They'll want to know if I'm there. They asked last time."

They went down at three in the morning, because the vault was the only room in Lingzhou nobody came into without asking.

As before: the round steel door open, the shelves of queens of hearts in their boxes, Yuanshu on the cold floor in the middle with the staff across his knees, Lingyue against the shelves, their mother in the doorway. And this time, on purpose, on the bottom step of the vault stairs, Xiaoyu in her school jacket with her marker already uncapped.

Seed took him down. Not into the Death Law; under it. Under the counting and the models and the weights, under the third page with its pressed silver weave, to the first. To the quiet at the bottom of the well.

It was quieter than last time; he felt that at once. Toward one side there was a place that wasn't empty, exactly. Still. Like a well where a stone has been lifted out and the water has closed over it, and you can only tell where it was because the water's a little deeper there.

That's where the hundred and thirty-eight were. I sit in it sometimes.

I'm going to ask the others now.

Hello. It's Seed. I'm sorry it's been so long. I said I'd ask you more often, and I haven't.

She's here, in the doorway. She came down to be here.

I'm going to ask you something, but first I'll tell you everything I'll do with your answer.

There are eleven stones on Blue Star. You know them. Some of you touched them. Some of you never got to. They're made of the same thread I am. They read children at eighteen and open them further than they'd go, and mark how bright, and send the mark up a thread into the dark, to whatever planted the tree. It's written in them: READ WHAT RIPENS. WHEN THE ORCHARD IS BRIGHT, THE PICKER COMES.

Nobody can ask them, because there's nobody in them.

I'd like to ask whether eleven of you would go into them. One in each. Sit where the knot is, and when a child puts a hand on the stone, be the one who answers. And ask them first, before anything else: what do you want to become? Then open what they say. Not what the stone reads. What they say. And send nothing up the thread.

I don't know if it'll work. I don't know what the thread will do when nothing goes up it, or what's at the other end, or whether it'll notice, or what it'll do if it does.

If you go, I'll forget you, as I forgot the hundred and thirty-eight. Xiaoyu will write your names on the wall and your builder will read them every morning, and I won't remember them.

You can leave whenever you want. The door at the bottom of everything is always a little open now, and nobody will hold you.

If you say no, nothing happens. If you say nothing, I'll wait. But I can't wait long. There are twenty-seven days.

Will any of you go?

For a long time, nothing.

He'd expected it. Last time had been the same. Three thousand nine hundred and sixty-four people at the bottom of a well, who'd been a harvest and then a floor, and had been asked anything twice in forty years. He sat on the cold floor and waited as Hush had taught Seed to wait: question set down, bowl on the ground, eyes turned aside.

His mother didn't move.

Then, faint and far down, as the first answer had come last time, something stirred. Not a question. He could feel its shape before Seed found the words: small, young, stubborn. Somebody who'd been waiting a long time to say something and had decided not to wait any more.

...I never touched it.

...the stone. In the hall. I was seventeen. The year after it fell. The first summer they did it.

...we were all going to go. My whole class. My mother made me a new jacket. Blue.

...and then the Wastes. The evacuation. The thing in the dark that sang. And then the sack.

...everyone in my class touched it that summer. I was in the sack. Nobody ever asked me what I wanted to be.

In the doorway Zhou Wanqing had put her hand over her mouth.

"Wen Jia," she said through her fingers. "W. Near the end of the second night. I remember. I asked if she'd let me build something out of her, and she said—"

...I said: only if it's something good.

...I remember you. You had a cold. You kept sneezing on the glass.

Their mother laughed. It came out all at once, wet and sudden, a noise he hadn't heard from her in a year.

"I did. I had a terrible cold. It was November and the basement had no heating."

...I would like to go into the one in Lingzhou. In the hall, where my class went.

...I would like to be the one who asks them. Since nobody asked me.

On the bottom step Xiaoyu wrote at the top of a sheet of ledger paper: WEN JIA. LINGZHOU. Her hand wasn't steady. She wrote it anyway.

The others came slowly through the rest of the night.

One, then nothing for half an hour, then another. Seed translated each with care, waiting for every word to finish before it could tell what the word would be, and Xiaoyu wrote each name, and their mother repeated each one quietly after Seed said it, as if checking it against something written inside her.

A midwife from Kesse who'd held four hundred children at their first breath and would like to hold some at their second. KESSE.

A man from the Northern Union who'd fished through the ice for forty years before the Wastes reached his village, and who said only that he'd been an E, and that it had been wrong, and he'd like to make sure it was wrong for someone else too. NOVAYA ZARYA.

A dock worker from the Atlantic coast, from Port Halvard, who said that somebody had hit his stone with a hammer last week. He'd heard about it, down here at the bottom of the well. He'd like to be there the next time someone was that angry at it, so there'd be somebody to be angry at who could answer back. PORT HALVARD.

A novice from a mountain shrine in Yamato, fifteen when she was taken and too young to have touched anything, who asked through Seed whether the shrine was still there. Seed said yes, and that a girl called Ren sat on its steps with an old man who swept them. The novice said then I would like the one nearest the shrine, please. Seed said there was one in the valley below, and the novice said yes. YAMATO.

By five in the morning there were nine.

Then nothing for an hour. The gray of dawn came through the small high window upstairs and down the vault stairs and lay on the floor in a thin pale square. Lingyue had fallen asleep against the shelves with the sword across her knees. Xiaoyu hadn't. She sat on the bottom step with nine names on her knee and the marker in her hand, watching her brother's face.

At a quarter past six, the tenth.

An old man. Older than any of them when the Monarch came. He didn't say where he was from. He said he'd been asked twice in forty years, both times by the same woman, which was more than anyone had asked him in the eighty years he'd been alive, and he'd go anywhere she liked.

He means you, Wanqing.

Their mother shut her eyes.

"Tell him the Southern Compact. Port Abiri. There's a hall by the harbor. There's no Tide King any more. Somebody old should be there."

...yes, said the old man. ...good. I like the sea.

The eleventh didn't come.

Seed waited till seven, then till eight. Upstairs the Ossuary woke: Fat Tan's slippers on the marble, a kettle, Kwan's voice, the bronze door. Their mother sat in the doorway with her eyes shut and her hands folded, and Yuanshu thought she'd fallen asleep until he saw her lips moving and understood she was saying the names. Not the ten. All of them, from the beginning, three thousand nine hundred and sixty-four. She'd written every one in a basement, with a cold, nine autumns ago.

At half past eight Seed said to him, quietly:

I'll stop. I said I wouldn't push. Ten isn't eleven, but ten isn't nothing. I'll think of something for the last stone.

And from the bottom of the well, slow, the way the coldest of the drowned had answered off Kesse, one word and then nothing and then the next:

...you said.

...if we said nothing. You would wait.

...I said: ask again tomorrow. Last time. In the vault. The night of the knots.

...it is tomorrow. It has been tomorrow for a long time. I have been thinking.

...yes.

Where would you like to go?

...wherever is left.

...I was a C. Precise Count. Like the woman with the boxes.

...I would like to count the ones who say no. Somebody should.

Gao Min found Wen Jia in the ledgers that afternoon.

She came to the vault steps with a gray ledger open in her hands, LINGZHOU AWAKENINGS, YEARS 6–10, a finger on a line, reading glasses pushed up into her gray hair, her face gone flat and careful as it did when she was holding a number that mattered.

"She was taken in year two. The Wastes evacuation. There's no awakening for her; she never got there." She turned a page. "But there's a Wen in year nine. Same district, same street. I checked the address against the census."

She put her finger on the line.

WEN HAO. DRIVER. TALENT D. STEADY HANDS.

"Her little brother," said Gao Min. "He'd be fifty-one. He drives the Number Nine bus, East Ring Road to the river." She looked up. "It goes past the hall twelve times a day. I checked that too."

Yuanshu sat on the steps a long moment.

Seed. Ask her.

I'm asking.

From the bottom of the well, faint, the small stubborn shape of a girl of seventeen in a new blue jacket she'd never got to wear to the hall.

...Hao-Hao?

...he was eleven. He cried when I left. He wanted to come.

...he drives a bus?

...is he still annoying?
