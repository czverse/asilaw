Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 276Two Mothers

czverse9 min read

On the second day someone from the fleet asked.

Not Severin, and not through every radio on Blue Star. Through the keeper's eye, quietly, to the platform of the Awakening Hall, where he'd slept three hours on the steps with the staff across his knees. A woman's voice, older than Iselde's, with the same precision, the same cool exactness of someone taught from the moment she could speak how she was to be addressed. But under it was something Iselde's hadn't had on the first morning, and had had by the last.

This is Corvane Varr-Anthos. SSS. Matriarch of the House of Anthos.

I would like to come down.

I am aware that on your world one asks. I am asking.

He sat up on the steps.

She's never asked anyone for anything, said Seed. I can tell. The grammar doesn't fit her mouth.

She's Iselde's mother. And Maelis's.

Ask her why.

She says: because the Fleet Marshal won't, and someone from the Ladder should look at the world that took her daughters before he burns it.

And because she'd like to see the woman who raised the boy who asks.

The keeper laid the road at noon. It asked first: her, and him, and the city, and Concordia, and the woman in the wheelchair in the back room of the Ossuary who was the one being asked to be seen. Everyone said yes. His mother said it last, without looking up from the ledger she was reading, the one with the column headed What they said. Then she put the ledger down and asked Fat Tan to fetch her good coat.

They met in the courtyard behind the Ossuary by the dry fountain, because his mother said she wasn't having a Matriarch of the Ladder in the banking hall under the wall of names, and Lingyue said she wasn't having her in the back room with the drawer, and the courtyard was the only place left. Ninth was there, three meters tall, translucent, standing by the fountain as she always stood. She didn't move when the road came down. Hush was on the sill with its limbs drawn in.

The Matriarch of the House of Anthos came down the road alone.

Tall: Iselde's height. Straight-backed. Sixty or so, gray hair cut short, a face that had been beautiful and was now something more exact than beautiful, as a blade is more exact than a jewel. A long coat of deep red with gold at the collar, and at her throat on a fine chain a small black disc with three gold marks cut into it.

She stepped off the road onto the courtyard stones and looked at Ninth. At Hush on the sill. At the potted plants and the dry fountain and the tall dead soldier in the doorway with a finger and a thumb on his spear.

Then at the small woman in the wheelchair in the middle of the courtyard, in her good coat, with a blanket over her knees.

Neither of them spoke for a while.

"You built it," said Corvane Varr-Anthos at last, in the Federation's language, and Seed carried it. "The thing in his head. Iselde told the Assembly. A woman built it in a basement out of a harvest crystal and four thousand voices and put it in her son."

"Yes," said Zhou Wanqing.

"Did you know what it would do?"

"No."

"Did you ask him?"

His mother looked at her a long moment.

"He wasn't old enough to be asked," she said. "I decided. I've owed him the question every day since." She folded her thin hands on the blanket. "Did you ask yours?"

The Matriarch didn't answer.

He stood at the side of the courtyard by the potted plants with the staff and said nothing. Lingyue stood beside him with Xiaodeng asleep in the sling and the sword on her back. Old Seven in the doorway, facing in for once.

"The Ladder saved us," said Corvane Varr-Anthos.

She said it to the courtyard, to the dry fountain, to the small woman in the wheelchair.

"Ten thousand years ago the Reaper came to the first human world. Before the Federation, before anything. It took everyone: the bright and the dim and the children, every age. Then someone learned to read the stones and mark how bright, and found that if the bright fought, they could hold the Reaper's hands back a little. And that if the bright had ships, they could fight farther. And that ships could be made to fold, if they burned something." She didn't look away from his mother. "So we burned the dim. Because the dim could not fight. Because without the folds the bright could not reach the worlds the Reaper was eating. Because it was that or lose everything."

"Four hundred and twelve worlds," said Zhou Wanqing.

"Four hundred and twelve," said the Matriarch, "out of nine thousand. We saved eight thousand five hundred and eighty-eight worlds, with the Ladder, with the folds, with the dim." Her voice didn't change. "I am not a monster. I am a mother of the House of Anthos. My mother gave her E's to the fold. Her mother gave hers. I gave Maelis. Because the stone said E and the Ladder said fuel and the Reaper was coming."

She stopped.

"And then it stopped," she said. "And the Ladder has nothing to say about that."

His mother said nothing for a long time.

Then she wheeled herself forward across the courtyard stones, slowly, until she was close enough to reach up and touch the tall woman in red, and didn't.

"I gave mine too," said Zhou Wanqing.

The Matriarch looked down at her.

"Not to a fold. To a world. My son walked out of this house at eighteen with a training spear and no permit and a harvest sack in his head, and I let him. He raised the dead and fought walkers and went up a stalk into the sky and through a Hunger's mouth and into the deep water, and every time I sat in this chair and let him." Her voice was very level. "Because he asked. And when I asked him what he wanted, he told me. And it was that."

She looked up.

"Did Maelis tell you what she wanted? On the morning, when they asked if she accepted the honor?"

The Matriarch of the House of Anthos stood very still in the courtyard with the black disc at her throat.

"She said yes," she said.

"Did she know what it was?"

The answer was a long time coming.

"No," said Corvane Varr-Anthos. "She was twelve."

"Did you?"

"Yes."

"Then you decided," said his mother. "For her, because she couldn't answer. As I did." She didn't look away. "So you owe her the question. Every day, until she can answer. And after." A pause. "Have you asked her?"

The tall woman in red said nothing.

"She will not see me," she said at last, very quietly, in the Federation's language, which puts a grade in every sentence about a person. Seed said she took the grade out of that one. "She is in a room in the yards at Anthos with the other unpressed children, learning to wear shoes. She will not see me. Iselde says she has told her to tell me that she will see me when I have asked her something."

"Then ask her," said Zhou Wanqing.

"I do not know how."

"Nobody does," said his mother. "You open your mouth and find out."

Xiaodeng woke up.

She did it as she did everything: all at once, furious, in the sling against her mother's chest, with a sound. A note. Small. Here I am.

The Matriarch turned her head.

She looked a long time at the child in the sling. At Lingyue holding her, with a sword on her back and a lamp in her hand. At the small red face screwed up in fury at the courtyard and the sky and everything.

"How old?"

"A month," said Lingyue.

"Has she been read?"

Lingyue's hand on the baby's back went still.

"No. We don't do that here. She can't be asked yet. When she's eighteen the stone will ask her what she wants to become. Or she can say no."

The Matriarch looked at the baby a long moment more. Then she said something Seed carried very carefully, word by word, as it carried things it had checked three times.

"Then hide her," said Corvane Varr-Anthos. "Before the ninth day."

Nobody in the courtyard moved.

"I am going to tell you something," said the Matriarch. "First. The way you do. Because my daughter learned it here, and I would like to know what it feels like." She didn't look away from the sling. "The Fleet Marshal is not going to wait for you to surrender anything. He does not need you to. The flagship carries a Lens."

"A lens," said Yuanshu.

She turned and looked at him for the first time.

"A piece of the first stone. The one that fell on the first human world ten thousand years ago, before the Federation. The oldest stone there is. The House of Severin has kept it nine thousand years. It is ground into a lens the size of this courtyard and mounted in the bow of the Severin flagship." Her voice was flat. "It reads worlds. Not one child at a time on the morning of their twelfth year, with a hand on a stone. A whole world, from the sky. Everyone. Everything that breathes. In one night."

"And marks them."

"And marks them, and sends the marks to the registry on Anthos, and the registry places every one of them on the Ladder in their proper order." She paused. "And the F's and the E's are collected."

The courtyard went very quiet.

"Your world has eight billion people. The Lens will find two billion F's and E's among them. Perhaps more. Your stones stopped marking forty-two years into your orchard; the Ladder would say you are badly under-tended." She looked at the sling. "It marks everything that breathes. Infants too. On Anthos we wait until twelve, because the stones wait. The Lens does not wait."

She looked back at his mother.

"On the ninth night the Fleet Marshal will turn the Lens on your world. He has not told you. He does not tell. I am telling you. I do not know why. I think it is because a woman in a chair asked me whether I had asked my daughter anything."

She went back up the road at dusk.

At its foot she stopped and turned and looked once more at the courtyard: the dry fountain, the potted plants, the tall translucent girl from the Hollow Bell, the small pale thing on many legs on the windowsill, the dead captain in the doorway, the woman in the wheelchair. The boy with the staff.

She took the black disc on its fine chain from her throat.

She didn't cross it out. She held it in her palm and looked a long time at the three gold marks cut into it.

"In the Federation's language," she said, "the word for this is the same as the word for face."

"I know," said Yuanshu. "Your daughter told us."

"Iselde crossed hers out in front of the Assembly. In front of me. I left the chamber." She closed her hand on the disc. "I am not going to cross mine out. I am sixty-one. It is the only face I have."

She put it back round her throat.

"But I am going to go back to my ship," said Corvane Varr-Anthos, "and sit down at the transmitter, and send a message to the yards at Anthos, to a room full of children learning to wear shoes." A pause. "I am going to ask my daughter what she wants."

"And if she doesn't answer?"

The Matriarch looked at the woman in the wheelchair.

"Then I will ask her again tomorrow. That is the rule. Is it not?"

She went up the road.

Seven days, said Seed. And a Lens. A piece of the first stone, the oldest there is.

Yuanshu, if it's a stone, it's on the net. Every stone is.
