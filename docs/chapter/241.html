Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 241What Do You Want to Become

czverse12 min read

Morning came to Lingzhou the color of strong tea, and by seven there was nowhere left to stand on East Ring Road.

He'd thought it would be like his own Awakening Day. It wasn't. On his day there'd been four thousand people inside the hall and ordinary traffic outside. Today the four thousand were inside by half past eight, every seat and every aisle, and outside there were more: on the steps, on the grass under the trees, along the pavements, on the shop roofs across the road, halfway up the lampposts. Quiet. Not a crowd waiting for a match. A crowd outside a hospital, like the parents on the first night. Half of Lingzhou standing in the summer heat with nothing to look at but a long curved roof and a screen somebody had rigged over the doors. A man was selling iced plum juice from a cart and doing so well that he'd stopped bothering with change.

And low in the north, faint even in daylight if you knew where to look, a small gold point: the bud on the end of a branch nobody could see.

Wen Jia, said Seed.

He'd told it at six, sitting on the edge of the bed with his feet on the cold boards. Wen Jia. Seventeen. Lingzhou. She went first.

Thank you. She's in the stone. I can feel the stone now. It's warm. It has someone in it.

I don't remember her. I know her name. That'll do.

At 9:52 the Number Nine bus stopped outside the hall.

There was no stop there. It came in to the curb through a crowd that parted for it unasked, and the handbrake went on, and both doors opened with a hiss.

Wen Hao counted to ten.

Then he switched off the engine and climbed down onto the pavement in his blue Transit Authority jacket with the shiny elbows, carrying over one arm a girl's jacket in a different blue. Brighter. Never worn. Forty years in a box on a wardrobe.

Nobody had told the crowd who he was. They let him through anyway.

Inside, the hall was a bowl of silence where his own had been a bowl of noise.

Four thousand people on the curved tiers. Camera drones hanging still overhead like silver fish asleep. The private boxes on the back wall empty: Gao Min had locked them and announced that anyone who wanted to sit above the children could sit on the roof. In the front row at the foot of the platform, where nobody had ever been allowed to sit because it was too close, a woman in a wheelchair with a blanket over her knees. Beside her, a bus driver with a blue jacket on his lap. Beside him, a big man from the coal yard with his hands clasped between his knees.

At the official's table at the side, where on his own day a man with a tablet had read out names and grades, Gao Min sat behind a new gray ledger ruled in pencil. She'd drawn the columns herself the night before: Name. Date. What they said.

There was no column for Talent.

At the foot of the steps, facing out, Old Seven. Beside him Roman Sterling in his raincoat. Sister Ning on her stool with her candle lit. And at the corner of the platform with her arms folded, exactly where she'd stood two years ago when a boy put his hand on the stone and it went black: Su Mingli.

Yuanshu stood beside her.

"She made me promise," said Su Mingli, without looking at him. "Your mother. A long time ago. That if this day ever came I'd stand right here. I thought she meant you." Her arms stayed folded. "She meant both of you."

"Zhou Xiaoyu," said Gao Min.

She said it from memory, in her flat careful voice, and four thousand people heard it, and half of Lingzhou outside on the screen, and ten other halls on the open channel, and every radio on Blue Star that was switched on.

His sister came out of the line.

Plain dark school jacket; she'd refused the training uniform. Hair tied back badly, as always. A black marker capped in her pocket, because she'd said she wasn't going anywhere important without one. She walked to the foot of the steps and stopped and looked at her mother in the front row, and her mother looked back, and neither of them did anything at all.

Then Lingyue came out of the line behind her.

Nobody had put her name down. She walked out among the eighteen-year-olds in her gray sweater with her grandfather's sword on her back, and the hall saw her, and a murmur went through four thousand people like wind through standing grain: the SSS. The Dawnbreaker. The one who crossed out her card. She stopped a step behind Xiaoyu.

"I said I'd stand behind you."

"I know," said Xiaoyu. "Obviously."

They went up the three steps together.

Up close the Stele was warm. You could see it from the foot of the steps, a faint shimmer of heat off the black stone under the ceiling lamps. The crack ran down its face. The runes weren't flat and dull and hadn't been since midnight. Every one of them was turned to point down into the stone, at the place where it went into the floor.

At someone.

Xiaoyu didn't touch it straight away. She stood there in front of four thousand people and every camera on Blue Star with her hands at her sides, looking at it, as you look at a door before you knock.

Then she said, clearly, in her ordinary voice: "Hello. I'm Xiaoyu. I'm going to put my hand on you now. I'm telling you first."

And she did.

Nothing happened.

One second. Two. High in the tiers nobody coughed. The drones didn't whine. Four thousand people held their breath together, and he could hear it, the absence of four thousand breaths, like a held note.

Three.

Then the runes moved. Not toward her hand; not pointing at her as they'd pointed at him. They rose. All over the stone each rune lifted a finger's width off the black, like the hairs on your arm when someone you love walks into the room. And light came out of the Stele.

Not the white-gold that had burst out for Lingyue. Not black. Soft and low, the color of a lamp in a window at night, seen from the street, when somebody inside is still awake.

Letters formed in the air above the stone. Not [Class:]. Not [Talent:]. Slowly, as they'd formed for him two years ago, as if the Stele were hunting for words it had never needed. But they didn't crack or break into question marks. They formed one at a time, carefully, in a hand that wasn't the Stele's.

[What do you want to become?]

The hall made no sound.

He'd remember that longer than anything else about the day. Four thousand people, half a city outside, ten other halls, every radio on Blue Star that was switched on, and nobody made a sound. They read it, all of them: a question hanging over a black stone that had told every eighteen-year-old on Blue Star for forty-two years what they were.

In the front row the bus driver laid his hand flat on the blue jacket in his lap.

Xiaoyu kept her hand on the stone. She looked up at the letters a long time. Then, because she'd never in her life been able to say anything important except to someone's face, she looked down at the stone, at where the runes pointed, at the place in the floor where a girl of seventeen sat in a knot.

"I was scared," said Zhou Xiaoyu. "I was scared you'd say Scribe, Talent D, and everybody would go oh, and that'd be what I was. Because you said so."

The letters hung there.

"I've been writing names on a wall for two years," said his sister. "Everybody who comes home. Everybody who doesn't. Everybody who gives something up so somebody else can stay. My brother's thing forgets them because it has to. Somebody has to not forget." Her voice shook. She let it. "I write them down and I don't hold on to them. They're not mine. I just write them so they're written. So nobody can say afterward they weren't there."

She drew a breath.

"That's what I want to become. That. Whatever it is. I don't know what it's called."

The letters went out all at once, and the hall breathed in.

Then, carefully, in the same hand, new letters. Not a grade. Another question, smaller.

[What should we call it?]

Xiaoyu stared at it. Then she laughed, out loud, in front of four thousand people and every camera on Blue Star, with her hand flat on the black stone and tears running down her face: her real laugh, the one from the long table when Shi Lei said something stupid.

"Scribe," she said. "Call it Scribe." She wiped her face with her other wrist. "That's what I was scared of. So I want it. I want it to be mine. Not because you said. Because I did."

The light in the stone came up. Not bright. Warm. The lamp in the window, turned up because somebody was coming home late.

[Class: Scribe]

[Chosen.]

[Talent: — not marked —]

And under it, so small that only the front row could read it, and the cameras, and Gao Min with her pencil frozen over her ledger, one more line in the hand that wasn't the Stele's:

[Only if it's something good. It is.]

Xiaoyu took her hand off the stone, and the letters stayed.

He saw it and didn't understand at first. On his own day, when he took his hand away, the black had drained out of the Stele like water from a basin and taken the letters with it. Today his sister stepped back from the warm stone and the letters went on hanging over the platform, not fading. Written. As if somebody had written them down so they'd be written, and nobody could say afterward they hadn't been there.

That's her Law, said Seed. I can see it. Very small and very new. It isn't in any of the Bureau's files and it isn't on the tree in the stone. The tree had no knot for it.

Wen Jia didn't open a knot on the tree. She opened what Xiaoyu said.

At the corner of the platform, Su Mingli's arms had come unfolded.

The hall didn't cheer, not at first. Nobody knew what you were supposed to do. Four thousand people sat in silence with three lines of soft light hanging over a black stone, and a girl of seventeen in front of it with her face wet, and the Dawnbreaker a step behind her with a hand on her shoulder now.

Then in the front row the woman in the wheelchair put both thin hands on the arms of her chair and pushed.

Fat Tan started forward and stopped himself. Wen Hao turned his head. And Zhou Wanqing, who hadn't stood out of that chair in a year, got her feet under her on the polished floor of the Awakening Hall and pushed, arms shaking, face set the way her daughter's set, and stood.

Not for long. Three seconds, four. Long enough. On her own feet in the front row in front of four thousand people, looking up at her daughter.

"I said I would," said his mother, to nobody and everybody. "I said if it looked at you wrong I'd get out of this chair. It didn't look at you wrong." Her knees were going. "I'm getting out anyway."

Fat Tan caught her as she sat back down.

Then the living cheered.

"Shi Lei," said Gao Min, when it was quiet enough, and her voice wasn't quite as flat as usual.

He came up the steps with his wooden spear and didn't put it down, and forgot to tell the stone first, and halfway to touching it stopped dead, went red, and said, "Sorry. Hello. I'm Shi Lei. I'm going to touch you now." Four thousand people laughed, gently, all at once.

[What do you want to become?]

"Somebody who stands in front of the coal yard when a rift opens," said Shi Lei. He didn't look at the stone. He looked at the front row, at a big man with his hands between his knees. "So my dad doesn't have to. That's it. That's all I ever wanted. The S was just for the Academy. The Academy was just for that."

His father didn't stand. He put his face in his hands.

They came all morning and all afternoon.

Not everybody touched it. Some walked to the foot of the steps, looked up at the warm stone, and turned round and walked back, and Gao Min wrote in her new column chose not to, this year. On the open channel a faint voice from the eleventh stone, the one who'd been a C, Precise Count, and had asked to count the ones who said no, read out the number every hour. Some went up and put their hands on it and said I don't know, and the stone said, in the hand that wasn't its own, [Then come back when you do. I will be here.] They came down again crying or laughing or both, with nothing opened in them, and nobody in the hall thought less of them.

And some said things nobody had ever said to a Stele. A healer, because of my grandmother. A Guardian, but a small one, for the school. Something that makes things grow. I want to be able to hear the dead, like him. Not raise them. Just hear them. I want to fly. Just fly. Nothing else.

And the stone opened what they said.

On the radio from Novaya Zarya, Kesse, Yamato, Port Halvard, Port Abiri, all eleven halls, it was the same, hour after hour: children putting their hands on warm black stones on every continent and being asked, for the first time in forty-two years, what they wanted, and answering.

At ten past six, when the line was done and the hall emptying and the drones had landed, the bus driver came up the steps.

Gao Min didn't call him. He'd awakened thirty-three years ago and there was no line for him. Nobody stopped him either. He climbed the three steps slowly in his Transit Authority jacket with the other jacket over his arm, and stopped in front of the Stele.

He didn't touch it. He unfolded the jacket and laid it over the black stone, carefully, as you'd lay a coat over someone asleep in a chair, across its shoulder where the crack began. Bright blue on black. Forty years on a wardrobe.

"There," said Wen Hao. "You never got to wear it to the hall."

The light in the stone came up. Warm. The lamp in the window.

He stood there a while. Then he put his hand flat on the stone beside the jacket. Steady hands. Twenty-six years of them.

[What do you want to become?]

The driver of the Number Nine looked up at the letters for a long time.

"Nothing," he said. "I'm fine. I've got a bus. I just wanted to hold your hand."

The letters went out, and came back. Not in the air this time. Small, on the surface of the stone under his palm, where only he could see them, and the boy at the foot of the steps with a harvest tool behind his eyes that could read anything written anywhere.

[Hao-Hao. You're still annoying.]

[Come past twelve times a day.]

[Count to ten.]
