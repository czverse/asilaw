Book 5: The Empty House

Chapter 299Holders

czverse11 min read

Gao Min had the rota on the wall of the Awakening Hall by five in the morning.

She hadn't slept. Nobody who mattered had. She'd sat at the long table in the banking hall from two until half past four with Seed in her ear, a stack of ledger paper and the old mechanical stopwatch from the Bureau, and drawn it the way she drew everything, in columns, by hand, pencil first and then ink, because a rota you could rub out was a rota nobody believed.

It went up beside the side door, under Xiaoyu's capitals. Six sheets taped edge to edge. Two-hour shifts, twelve a day, fifteen hundred names a shift.

"Eighteen thousand a day," said Gao Min to the hall. She stood in front of it with her arms folded and the stopwatch hanging on a string round her neck like a medal she hadn't asked for. "That's what it takes. Seed ran it. Fifteen hundred at a time, through the stone, burning low, two hours each. Then you go home, sleep, eat something, and somebody else sits down." She looked at the sheets. "Every day. For as long as he's gone."

"How long's that?" said someone at the back.

"Nobody knows," said Gao Min. "So I've drawn it for a year. If it's longer, I'll draw more. I have a great deal of paper."

He only understood it properly when Seed walked him through it, in the gray before dawn, sitting on the bottom step of the platform.

Last time, the body emptied, said Seed. The fifteen hundred held it, through the stone and the floor and the ground underneath. They were an anchor, and an anchor doesn't move. But a body with nobody in it burns down like a lamp nobody trims, and after nine hours there'd have been nothing left for the anchor to hold.

So this time they don't just hold. They row.

The cousins taught the cores to burn low. A little each, steady, a small warmth into the ship, so the ship moves and nobody in the walls goes out. Two hundred and forty thousand of them rowed the flagship of the House of Severin, and not one went out.

The holders will do that, into you, through the stone. So the body never burns down, because there's always somebody putting a little in.

I asked Tam whether it works with people instead of cores. He listened to the walls of this hall for an hour and said they say yes, it's the same, it's only being warm on purpose.

And if they stop?

Then I've got nine hours to bring you home from wherever we are. I'm telling you now so you've heard it.

They came anyway.

He couldn't work out where Gao Min had found eighteen thousand names in three hours until he went out onto the steps at a quarter past five and saw.

East Ring Road was full. From the doors of the hall down the steps and along the pavement, past the bus stop, round the corner, as far as the old quarter and further. It wasn't loud, because the old quarter wasn't loud. People in coats in the chill of a late-summer dawn, standing in a line that went round two blocks, with thermoses and folding stools and children asleep on their shoulders, waiting to be told where to sit.

Dabao's mother had brought the cart, naturally. Fat Tan had put the green canvas roof on it and parked it at the corner, and she was folding paper boats as fast as her hands would go and handing out dumplings without taking money. Seed said it was the first time in nineteen years; Fat Tan said it was the first time in her life and he wanted it written down.

"The night watch did it," said Su Mingli. She'd come up the steps behind him and stood with her arms folded, looking down the line. "Gao Min rang the night watch at two. The night watch rang the old quarter. The old quarter rang everybody. They've been out there since three. Most of them don't know what they've signed up for. They know it's you, and they know it's holding."

"Do you know?"

"I'm on the four o'clock," said the Master of the Tianshu Institute. "Today, and tomorrow, and the day after. Qin Hao's on the six; he wants to watch the numbers from inside." She looked sideways at him. "Don't do the face."

It wasn't only Lingzhou.

Seed told him at six, when the first shift was filing in. The open channel through Concordia had carried it all night, nine by nine, stone to stone, as everything travelled now. Kesse: four hundred on the beach at the stone for the midday shift. Novaya Zarya: the Marshal had put the Northern Union down for every night shift for a month, in his own handwriting. Port Halvard: Callum Ward's mother had walked onto the north pier and said somebody had held her son once, a long way up, and she'd like to hold somebody back; two thousand people came behind her. Yamato: Ren and the Abbot at the shrine. The Abbot had asked whether sweeping counted. Ren said it did, provided he swept toward the stone.

Every stone's on the net, said Seed, and the net doesn't care where a holder sits. A woman on the beach at Kesse with her hand on the midwife's stone can row as well as a woman in this hall.

Gao Min's redrawing the rota. She says she needs more paper. She says she won't use a computer for it, because a computer doesn't know what it's like to be asked to hold someone, and a rota like this should be drawn by somebody who does.

He said goodbye to his mother in the back room of the Ossuary at half past six, because she wouldn't come to the hall.

"I sat in this chair while you went up a thread," said Zhou Wanqing. "I'll sit in it while you go further. I'm not going to watch your body lie on a platform; I've watched enough of your body." The book of names was open on her knee. "I'll read. You'll hear it. Your sister says everything's on the net now."

It is, said Seed. I'll hear her every morning.

"Good." His mother looked at the place just above his eyes. "You're taking him to find out whose the first want was."

Yes.

"He tells me you ask him every morning who the first one was, at the bottom of you. And he tells you, and you forget, because you promised you would." She didn't look away. "So you've got the same question it has. Whose was first."

Seed didn't answer at once.

Yes. I didn't think anybody had noticed that but me.

"I built you," said his mother. "I notice." She looked down at the page. "Go and find out whose the old thing's was. Then come back and ask me whose yours was. I'll tell you. Every day, if I have to."

He knelt by the chair. She didn't touch him. She found her place with one thin finger and began to read aloud, and he stayed on his knees and listened until the third name, and then got up and went.

The hall at seven.

The first shift sat in the tiers, down the aisle and on the floor round the platform. Fifteen hundred people of Lingzhou. A tailor. Three nurses straight off nights at the General, still in their clogs. A row of Academy boys in uniform, being very serious about it. The bus driver, in on his day off, at the end of the front tier with his steady hands on his knees. Old women. Young men. A girl of nine who'd come with her grandmother and been told she was too young, so she'd gone up to the stone, put her hand on it and asked Wen Jia herself. Wen Jia had written [You can hold your grandmother's hand. That counts.] So she was doing that, grimly, with both hands.

Before they sat, each of them went up the three steps and put a hand on the warm black stone.

[He's going a long way. You'll be warm on purpose for two hours. It might make you tired. Do you want to?]

Each of them said yes, or of course, or eh, the old-quarter way, and went and sat down.

Nobody said no. Seed said three people had stood in front of the stone a long while and gone back down the steps without touching it, which was allowed, and Wen Jia had written [That's okay. Thank you for coming.] for each of them. One came back an hour later and touched it.

He lay down on the platform at a quarter to eight, where he'd lain before, his back against the side of the stone, under the blue jacket.

Bai Lu had put down a thin mattress and a blanket and set up a stand with a bag on it and a line that went into his arm. She told him exactly what it was, what it was for, and what it would and wouldn't do, and he said yes, and she said "Good, because I wasn't asking." Then she stopped with her hand on the drip. "I was. Sorry. I'm asking."

"Yes," he said again.

Old Seven lay down on the polished floor beside the platform, on his back, spear across his chest in his one hand. Nobody remarked on it. The Spear Corps had been told, and had replied through Seed that they had seen nothing and would continue to see nothing for as long as required.

Qin Hao knelt at his right side with the white fire banked in his open hands and his notebook on the floor by his knee. Sister Ning sat on her stool with her candle. Gao Min sat on the bottom tier with the stopwatch. Xiaoyu sat at the foot of the steps with the marker. Tam and Seri sat on the edge of the platform with their legs dangling, holding hands, because Tam said the walls were going to be busy and he wanted to hear if they needed anything.

And Lingyue.

She knelt at his left side, one hand flat on his chest, her grandfather's sword on the floor beside her, their daughter asleep in the sling, and in her other hand a lamp. Her own, the one from the Ossuary, burning pale, the size of a cup. At the foot of the stone a dead nun sat cross-legged with one dry hand on it, holding the other one.

"Two lamps," said Lingyue. "I'm keeping both. Kiyo's holding the old one and I'm holding this. When you turn round, there'll be two."

"I know."

"I'm going to pull," she said. "If they stop. If something goes wrong with the rota. If the sky falls in. If it's been too long and I decide it's been too long. You've been warned." Her hand didn't move on his chest. "And I'm going to sit here. Not all the time; Bai Lu says I'm not allowed all the time. But every day. With her."

He looked up at his daughter's face. Asleep. Four and a half months. One fist against her mother's collarbone, as if she were holding it in place.

Seed.

I know.

Seed asked her, as it did every morning before it asked him who the first one was: quietly, in a voice meant only for her, which he heard because he heard everything Seed said.

Xiaodeng. Morning. It's Seed. Your father's going a long way, and he's coming back, and I'm going with him to make sure.

What do you want?

His daughter didn't wake. She made a small sleep-sound, the kind that isn't a word yet.

She says ask again tomorrow. Your mother will ask her for me.

"Every morning," said Shen Lingyue.

At seven fifty-nine, in the north past the keeper, the fullness in the sky turned.

It didn't move. It turned, as someone waiting in a doorway turns at last to face the road when the person they've been waiting for comes out of the house.

His sister heard it. Her palm was flat on the floor at the foot of the steps.

"It says it's ready," said Xiaoyu. "It says thank you for asking it in. It's never been asked in anywhere before. It says it's going to remember the lamps in the windows." She swallowed. "It says not tomorrow. Now."

He shut his eyes.

He felt the stone at his back, warm, its runes pointing down, and in it a girl who'd been seventeen in a sack, who'd wanted to be fifty-one with her brother, who'd asked him yesterday morning what he wanted to become and written [Then get old.]

...go on, said Wen Jia. ...I've got fifteen hundred people in here being warm on purpose. It's a lot. It's like a bath.

...I'll ask the kids while you're gone. You're always going.

...come back and tell me how it's going. Getting old. I want to know.

He felt the holders start.

Not all at once. One, then another, then the whole hall: fifteen hundred small warmths coming up through the floor and the stone and into him, low and steady, a little each, as the cousins rowed. It wasn't a pull or a push. It was a floor under him where there hadn't been one. He felt the tailor, the three nurses, the Academy boys, the bus driver's steady hands, the nine-year-old clamped onto her grandmother. Further off and faint, through the net, four hundred people on a beach at Kesse getting ready for midday, and a Marshal in the Northern Union with his gloves off.

He felt Lingyue's hand on his chest.

Then he went up.

Out of the body, up the stone, into the thread, as he'd gone a year ago into the deep water: up through the eleven twisted into one under Blue Star and along the net, fast, as marks travelled now the web was still, faster than he could follow. Past the keeper, past the bud and the Elder's great gray ring, past the edge of the Shallows. Old Seven beside him, one cold steady ember with one arm. Seed. And the fullness, no longer in the sky but all around them, warm and patient and very old, heading the way it had always pointed.

North. Out along the arm. Into the dark between.

Toward a house where nobody lived.

Behind them, on the platform of the Awakening Hall in Lingzhou, a woman with a sword and a baby and a lamp looked up at the stopwatch on its string round Gao Min's neck.

"Hour one," said Shen Lingyue.

Fifteen hundred people sat in the tiers. On a beach at Kesse four hundred more finished their tea. On East Ring Road a line two blocks long, round the corner past a bus stop and a dumpling cart, shuffled up one place. All of them warm on purpose, all of them rowing.
