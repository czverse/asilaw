Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 275Ninety

czverse9 min read

They took a day to cross the Shallows, and when they arrived they didn't crinkle. They lit.

He watched from the north wall with half of Lingzhou. Kwan had told the radio at dawn that the line of crinkles at the far edge of the arm had begun to move; by noon the battlements were full, and by dusk all of Blue Star that could see the northern sky was watching it. Low over the end of the world, beside the keeper's small white ring with its gold haze and the Elder's vast gray one with its soft white center: nothing. A clear spring evening. The first stars.

Then, one after another, like lamps coming on along a street at dusk, ninety lights.

Not stars. Bigger. Brighter. Each a long shape lit from end to end, burning white and gold and the colors of their Houses, so that the northern sky over Blue Star was suddenly full of what looked like cathedrals, enormous, hanging in the dark in a great curved line round the keeper's field like a necklace. Some of them kilometers long. Spired, buttressed, windowed, with light pouring out of them. They burned on purpose. The quiet ones call them the Lamplit, Ichiro had said, because they burn on purpose.

Nobody on the north wall said a word.

Ninety ships, said Seed. The smallest is eleven times the size of the Candle. The largest is forty times. Every one of them made of cores.

I'm counting. I haven't finished. Three million. Four million. More. In the walls, burning.

Yuanshu, every one of those lights is people.

Beside him Lingyue stood with Xiaodeng asleep against her chest in a sling Fat Tan had made from an old Ossuary banner, and her grandfather's sword on her back again, because Bai Lu had said three weeks and it had been three weeks, and she'd picked it up that morning and said there and not put it down. The lamp-sized flame in her free hand, held, not burning. She was looking up at the ninety lights.

"They're pretty," she said flatly.

"Yes."

"I hate them," said Shen Lingyue.

On his other side Tam had his face turned up and his eyes shut. He'd come up the Sector Nine stairs at five with Xiaoyu and three hundred of the Candle's children behind him and stood at the parapet, and hadn't said anything since the first light came on.

"Tam," said Yuanshu.

The boy didn't open his eyes.

"I can hear them," said Tam in Huaxian; he'd been learning nine months. "All of them. Four million. Five. Burning. In the walls." His voice was very steady, as the Candle's children's voices went steady when something was worse than they could say. "They're not asking anything. They don't know they can."

A pause.

"Some of them are from Corraine. I know their voices."

The voice came at nine.

Not through the keeper's eye, nor through a recording that taught itself, slow and patient, as the Candle's had. Through everything: every radio on Blue Star, every speaker, every phone. In Huaxian and the Northern Union's tongue and Yamato's, in the languages of the Southern Compact and the Atlantic Federation, perfectly, as if the speaker had learned them all in a day. Which, Seed said afterward, he probably had, from listening to Blue Star's radio while the fleet crossed.

A man's voice. Calm, courteous, absolute. The voice of someone who'd never once in his life needed to raise it.

"People of Unlisted Orchard Four-Four-Seven-One. I am Dacian Severin. SSS. Paragon of the House of Severin. First of the Ladder. Fleet Marshal of the Lamplit Worlds."

On the north wall three thousand people went still.

"By decree of the Assembly of the Federation, your world is listed. From tonight you are an orchard of the Lamplit. You will be read, and marked, and placed upon the Ladder in your proper order, as every human world has been since the first stone fell."

A pause. Not for effect; the man didn't need effects.

"Three things are required of you.

"First. Six days before the Reaper was stilled, a Lord-bright flare was recorded on your world. Then the Reaper's threads went quiet. The last thread it read before it stopped leads here. You possess the instrument by which this was done. You will surrender it to the Fleet.

"Second. Eight months ago a Federation listener ship was boarded in your sky by an unknown intelligence, and its Listener-Captain was induced to unpress her cores. Since then seven Houses have emptied their arrays. Eleven ships stand unfoldable in the yards at Anthos. The Ladder has been dishonored. You will surrender to the Fleet any person on your world who took part in the speaking to cores.

"Third. Your stones have been altered. They do not mark. They ask. This is heresy against the oldest law. Within ten days your stones will be restored to their purpose, or they will be removed."

Another pause.

"And the ungraded one," said Dacian Severin. "The one the Listener-Captain called the boy who asks. Whose stone could not rate him. He will present himself to the Fleet and be read. We would like to know what he is.

"You have ten days."

The radios went quiet.

Nobody on the north wall moved: three thousand people, the Candle's children, the night watch with their helmets in their hands, a woman with a baby in a sling and a sword on her back, standing on the battlements of a city on a world that had just been told it was an orchard.

Then Xiaodeng woke up and made a sound.

Not a cry. A note: the one she'd made in the Awakening Hall the morning she was born, furious, here I am. Very small, on the north wall, under ninety burning cathedrals.

Lingyue put a hand on the baby's back without looking down.

"He didn't ask," she said.

"No."

"Iselde said they wouldn't." Her jaw was set. "He didn't even say please. He said required." She looked at the lights in the north. "He wants to read you. Like a stone. To see what you are." She almost laughed. "The last time a stone read you, it went black."

I'm going to tell you what I can see, said Seed. Then you decide what to say. Not me. You.

He sat down on the cold battlement in his old place with his back to the parapet and the staff across his knees, shut his eyes, and let Seed show him.

The ninety ships are made of cores, like the Candle. Cells in the walls, small dark crystal, each the size of a person curled up, with pressed people in them, burning. Four million one hundred and eighty thousand. I've finished counting.

They're not burning low. They're burning the old way, held under water and set on fire, every fold.

I tried to ask one. I asked you first; you were asleep. You said yes in your sleep. I'm telling you now in case that doesn't count.

She didn't hear me.

He opened his eyes.

The Candle's cores heard you.

Yes. They were awake. They'd always been awake. They heard everything.

These aren't awake. There's something round every cell. A layer. Very thin. Something they've put round the crystal since the Candle, since the Assembly heard thirty-two thousand names read out and found out cores could hear.

It doesn't stop them burning. It stops them hearing. They've stopped up their ears.

Beside him Tam had opened his eyes.

"I can hear them," said the boy.

"Seed can't."

"I know." Tam looked at the lights. "I asked the stone to make me someone who hears the ones in the walls, so nobody's ever in a wall again with nobody listening." He didn't look away from the north. "It didn't say only the ones whose ears are open."

They answered at midnight, from the platform of the Awakening Hall before the warm black stone, because Wen Jia said that if they were answering a man who wanted the stones put back to marking, they should do it standing in front of one that asked.

Concordia had met by radio for three hours. Article Eleven: where one answer acts for all, all are asked. There were ten days and no time to ask everyone. So they asked the question everyone could answer fast, which Blue Star had already answered seven months before, from Concordia and the net of the dead and the paper ballots and Shiomi in the fog: Will we hide, or will we light the lamps?

They'd said: light the lamps.

So he lit them.

"Fleet Marshal," said Zhou Yuanshu, through Seed and the keeper's eye, which the keeper had offered before it was asked, in the Federation's language. He took the grades out; Seed helped. "My name is Zhou Yuanshu. I'm the one you asked for. The stone couldn't rate me. I'm going to tell you first what we'll do. That's how we do things here."

The fleet didn't answer. It was listening. He could feel it listening, as you'd feel ninety cathedrals full of people listening in the dark.

"We won't hide. We tried that once, with rings, and decided we don't want to be that kind of world.

"We won't attack you. We have a rule: the dead may defend, never conquer. The living keep it too.

"We won't surrender anyone. Nobody here did anything to your ship that its captain didn't choose. We asked her cores what they wanted. They said. She chose. That's all.

"We won't change our stones back. There are people in them now. They chose to be there. You'd have to ask them, and they'd say no."

He looked up at the roof of the hall, and past it, at ninety burning lights in the north.

"And Fleet Marshal: you asked what I am. I'll tell you. I've never hidden it. I'm a necromancer. I raise the dead, if they say yes. I've got a thing in my head that was a harvest sack once and learned to ask. That's all I am. That's the instrument. It isn't a weapon. You can't have it, because it isn't a thing. It's a question."

He paused.

"There are four million one hundred and eighty thousand people in the walls of your ships," said Zhou Yuanshu. "You've stopped up their ears so nobody can ask them anything.

"I'm telling you first: we're going to ask them anyway."

For a long time the fleet didn't answer.

Then, through every radio on Blue Star, calm, courteous, absolute, the voice of a man who'd never once needed to raise it:

"Ten days."

And the radios went quiet.

He came down off the platform. At the foot of the steps: Lingyue with Xiaodeng asleep in the sling, the sword on her back, the lamp in her hand. His mother in her wheelchair. Xiaoyu with the marker. Tam. Gao Min. Kwan. Su Mingli with her arms folded. Old Seven at the side door, facing out.

"You told him we'd ask them," said Su Mingli flatly. "You told the First of the Ladder, with ninety ships in our sky, that we'd ask his engines what they want."

"Yes."

"He's going to make sure we can't."

"Yes."

"Can we?"

He didn't answer. He looked at the warm black stone with the blue jacket on its shoulder and its runes pointing down. At Wen Jia in the knot, holding. At the thread rising out of its crown through the roof into the net: every stone in every orchard, tied through a knot in the deep water where nobody read anything any more.

Cut from the stone, Iselde had said on the north wall, a small black disc on a chain in her palm. Every Federation child is given one on the morning of the reading. Cut from the stone that read them.

And the cells in the Candle's walls. Small. Dark. Crystal. The size of a person curled up.

The same black.

Yes, said Seed. I see it. I didn't see it before. I'd like to stop not seeing things.

I'm going to need to look very carefully. And I'm going to need Tam.

And Yuanshu, I'm telling you first: I don't know yet if it'll work. And if it does, it won't only reach the walls.
