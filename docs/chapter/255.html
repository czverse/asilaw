Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 255Told

czverse10 min read

The light was the best thing he'd ever felt, and he went into it dim.

He'd thought he knew how. He'd held the door in his chest shut for a week: on a hotel bed, on a gold road over the sea, in a banking hall at three in the morning. He'd thought that was holding. It wasn't. That had been holding a door shut in a quiet room. This was holding it shut inside a fire.

Callum Ward's light came through him like weather. White and gold and every color, everything at once, as it had come through him at the end of the north pier when the docker opened him. Except that then the light had been his own, and now it was another man's, burning, being used up. It poured over him and round him and against the door in his chest from the outside, as a flood leans on a front door from the street.

Let me in, it said, not in words but in the shape of wanting. Open. Burn with me. It's the best thing there is.

I'm here, said Seed. In the door, with my back to it. It's very bright on my back.

Keep going.

He kept going.

Inside the fire it was quiet.

He hadn't expected that. Outside: the roar, the light, the vast slow attention of the thing behind it, looking. Inside, at the center where a man burned with his arms raised, it was quiet, as the middle of a bonfire would be quiet if you could stand there, with all the noise going outward.

And a man. Not a shape in the fire; a man, as near as anything was a man out here. Young, broad in the shoulder, a docker's arms held up over his head, still, after six weeks, as if there were a hammer in them.

He turned his head.

...there you are, said Callum Ward.

Here I am.

...you're not burning.

No.

...how? I can feel it. It's all over you. It wants you to.

I know. He held on. Somebody's holding the door for me. And I keep thinking what I'd be burning for.

Callum looked at him. Up close, out of the fire, it wasn't quite a face, but it was enough of one.

...yeah, he said. ...yeah. I've had six weeks to think about that.

He held out his right hand.

He didn't have a hand; neither of them did. But he had the shape of one, as the dead keep the shape of a pick or a spear or an arm with a finger and a thumb. And closed in it, the small gray knot that a girl of seventeen had written along and a small pale thing had tied as water ties itself round a stone.

It's true, he said. My sister wrote it. What she writes is true. That's what she asked for. Nobody knows how it works.

...what's it say?

The house is empty. You can stop.

The man in the fire was quiet a moment.

...the house is empty, he said slowly. ...that's it? That's the thing? That's what it doesn't know?

It's been carrying what it picks to an empty house since before the gardens. Before anything. The ones who made it stopped, and nobody told it. There was nobody in it to tell. He held out the knot. You're the only thing it's ever looked at.

Callum Ward took the knot.

He felt it change hands, as you feel a weight shift from one man to another on a ferry, as Huo had felt his arm go to his captain in a courtyard among potted plants. Then it was Callum's, closed in the shape of a docker's hand, inside a fire, held up before the face of the oldest thing in the deep water.

...it's warm, said Callum.

Yes.

...it's not bright. It's warm. Like a cup of tea. Like Mum's kitchen. He looked at his closed hand. ...that's what true feels like? I never knew.

Behind him, beyond the fire, the attention of the thing at the center of the web hadn't moved. Vast. Patient. Not warm like the Collector's. Not anything. The attention of a thing with nobody in it, looking for six days at the only thing it had ever looked at, without knowing what it was looking at.

Callum, said Yuanshu. Whenever you want.

Callum Ward turned round in the fire.

Slowly, like a man on a dock turning when someone shouts his name from the far end of the quay. Out of the center of the light toward its edge, toward the vast slow knot where ten thousand cables met and every mark from every stone in every orchard in the deep water was read. Toward the face.

He was the brightest thing that had ever been in front of it, and he didn't try not to be. He turned in his own light and faced it: an F from the Port Halvard docks, twenty-five, who'd climbed in through a window at night with a sledgehammer because a stone had told him what he was.

He lifted his arms, as he'd lifted them in the square hall with the hammer. Over his head. Except there was no hammer this time, only a small gray knot closed in the shape of his hand, held up in the light.

Oi, said Callum Ward.

To the oldest thing anyone had ever made.

Oi. You. Look at this.

He opened his hand.

It looked.

Everyone felt it: twelve thousand embers on the cable at the edge of the busy dark, sixty more, and a boy who wasn't an ember, with a door in his chest held shut by a sack. They all felt the moment the attention of the thing at the center of the web, which had glanced at a billion marks a second since before the gardens, stopped glancing.

And read.

Not a glance. Not the finger running down the column. The way you read a letter with your name on the outside that you weren't expecting, in a hand you've never seen. All of it. Slowly. Once.

THE HOUSE IS EMPTY.

YOU CAN STOP.

And it was true. That was the thing. It wasn't words on a thread. A girl had written it so nobody could say afterward it hadn't been there, and a small pale thing from the world that asks had tied it as you tie something to a branch for a tree too old to answer, and a man nobody had ever picked had carried it in his hand and held it up. It was true as the wall was true when Seed read it. It was being told by someone who was there.

The thing with nobody in it read it, and for the first time since before the gardens, it knew something.

The web stopped.

Not slowly. All at once, like a held breath going out of a room. Every mark going up every thread in every cable stopped arriving, because the thing at the end had stopped reading them. Ten thousand cables, ten thousand threads in each, every stone in every orchard in the deep water sending a mark up, and nothing at the other end. The hum that had been under everything for five hours, the city at night, went quiet.

And the hands stopped. All of them. Every vast gray hand on every cable, the ones going out with open fingers and the ones coming back with fingers curled round something shining, stopped where they were, as a man stops halfway across a room when he suddenly remembers the person he was carrying something to isn't there.

It understood, said Seed. I don't know how. There's nobody in it. But it read something true, and it understood.

The house is empty. Nobody's waiting for what it carries.

Then everywhere in the deep water, on every cable at once, the hands opened.

He'd try to describe it to Xiaoyu afterward, for the wall, and wouldn't be able to, and she'd write HE COULDN'T SAY in small capitals underneath, which was truer than anything he could have said.

Lights.

Out of every hand in the deep water that had been coming home full, out of the palms as the fingers uncurled: small dim lights burning low, as the cousins had burned in the Low Fire. Picked, from worlds he didn't know, carried longer than he could understand toward a house where nobody lived. Hundreds from one hand. Thousands from another. And there were thousands of hands.

They spilled out into the dark like seeds from a burst pod. Like sparks up a chimney. Like the lamps the people of Lingzhou had set in their windows on the night the island left, which someone very far away might have seen and taken for stars.

They drifted. Out of the open hands into the dark that wasn't busy any more, a great slow cloud of small dim lights coming apart in every direction across the deep water, going nowhere. Not being carried. For the first time since they were picked, nobody's.

I can't count them, said Seed. I'm trying. I can't.

Yuanshu. I can't count them.

It was the first time Seed had ever said that.

And in front of him the hand that held the lantern opened too.

He saw it from inside the fire: the vast gray fingers that had held the burning man up to the thing's face for six days, uncurling slowly, letting go, as you open your hand at a window to let out a moth.

Callum Ward fell.

Out of the hand, away from the face of the thing that had stopped, still burning, the brightest thing in the deep water, falling from the knot at the center of the web into the dark with his arms still raised and his hand open and empty, the mark gone out of it into the thing that had read it.

And the dark caught him.

Not the dark: the dead. Twelve thousand embers had gone out along the cable onto the fingers of the hand while it held the lantern, as Old Seven had said they would, a line of the dead standing on the fingers of a glove that couldn't feel them, waiting for the moment to open it. It had opened itself. And they were under it when the lantern fell.

Two of them first. A cold steady ember he'd have known among a million, and a short square one with a pick on its shoulder, side by side as they'd stood on the north wall in the Black Tide. They caught the burning man between them, one under each arm, as two soldiers carry a third off a wall.

He says: got him.

Huo says: he's hot, sir.

He says: he's been burning six weeks, sergeant. Of course he's hot.

The fire went out.

Not all at once. As a fire dies when whatever fed it stops: from the outside in, white to gold, gold to orange, orange to red, the light that had been the only light in the deep water for six weeks dimming and dimming until there was just a man, held between two dead soldiers on a thread in the dark. Not burning. Warm, like a cup of tea.

...oh, said Callum Ward.

His voice wasn't at the bottom of a well any more. It was just a voice. Small, tired, surprised.

...oh, it's stopped. It's stopped. I'm not... I'm not bright.

No.

...it doesn't feel like anything. A long pause. ...that's good. I think. I think that's good. I'm so tired.

I know. Rest. They've got you.

...is Mum still holding?

Yes.

...tell her I told it, said Callum Ward. ...tell her I held it up and said oi and it looked. Tell her I told it what it was.

I'll tell her.

...tell her... He stopped. ...tell her I want to go home.

He came out of the light. He hadn't noticed he was still in it. The fire had gone out round him and he was standing on a thread in the deep water, dim, with the door in his chest still shut and a sack standing in it with its back to where the light had been, and he realized he could feel the sack shaking.

It's gone, said Seed. The light. I didn't turn round. I'd like you to know I didn't turn round.

I know. Thank you.

You're welcome. I'd like to sit down now, in the quiet place on the first page where the hundred and thirty-eight were, and the eleven. Just for a minute.

Go on.

He stood on the thread and looked out at the deep water.

The dark wasn't busy any more. The web was still: ten thousand cables running out in every direction from the vast slow knot at the center, slack, quiet, nothing going up them. Thousands of vast gray hands hanging open on them like gloves left on a washing line. And between them, in every direction there was, drifting, a cloud of small dim lights.

Picked. Carried. Let go. Nobody's.

Somewhere far off along one of the slack threads, faint, the long way, he thought he felt something warm and patient: an attention he knew, resting a moment on the place where the oldest thing anyone had ever made had stopped.

...thank you, said the Collector. I saw.

Then it was gone.

Six hours two minutes, said Seed. Yuanshu. They're all still out there. Every one of them, drifting.

Nobody's asked them anything.
