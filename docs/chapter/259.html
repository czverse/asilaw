Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 259The Lamp on the Shore

czverse11 min read

The bell changed on the forty-first morning after the deep water, and Sister Kiyo asked to be raised.

Ren rang at a quarter to five. She didn't say the bell. She said, calmly, over the sound of wind and three thousand people breathing on the terraces below the shrine: "She wants you. Not on the phone. Here. She says she won't ask anybody else." A pause. "She hasn't asked anyone for anything in twelve hundred years. I'd come."

He went.

Not alone. Lingyue came, because she hadn't been to Yamato since the dark of the moon and wanted to see the gates by daylight. Old Seven came because he went. They flew east over the sea in the gray morning, falling as a Lord falls when he's decided where down is, and came down at noon on the first terrace of the mountain shrine among the cedars, where three thousand people in white and the hollow monks and the Quiet had sat listening every night for a year and a half.

Lingyue landed beside him and stood a moment with her hand on the terrace wall.

"All right?" he said.

"Fine," said Shen Lingyue. "The flight. My stomach. I didn't eat." She straightened up. "Don't look at me like that. Go and see your nun."

The Abbot was sweeping the path.

He did it every morning, Ren said: from the foot of the mountain up under all twelve hundred and three gates to the top, and down again, with a straw broom, slowly. He didn't remember why. He remembered that it was what he did. He'd swept the gates every morning for forty years before the Hollow Gods, then stopped, then come back with no memory of what he'd wanted, and the first thing he'd done, Ren said, was pick up the broom.

He stopped when he saw them, at the three hundred and fortieth gate, leaned on the broom, and looked at Yuanshu with the mild puzzled courtesy of a man who knows he ought to know you.

"She's under this one," said Kurose Tōya. "I sweep it more carefully. I don't know why." He looked at the stone under the gate. "Ren says she's been talking to you. For a long time."

"Yes."

"I used to think the dead should be left alone. I remember that. I don't remember why." He swept a cedar needle off the stone. "She doesn't seem to want to be left alone."

Under the three hundred and fortieth gate, in a stone chamber the size of a cupboard, under a flat stone that Ren and two monks of the Quiet eased aside with ropes, Sister Kiyo of Shiomi sat in the dark where she'd sat for twelve hundred years.

He'd seen the dead in ten thousand forms: bones in coffins and in earth, the drowned at the bottom of the sea off Kesse, embers on a thread in the deep water. He'd never seen this.

She was small, smaller than Xiaoyu. A woman sitting cross-legged in the dark with her hands folded in her lap, in the rags of a gray robe gone brown, her head bowed, her skin become something like old paper drawn tight over a frame of tiny bones. Not a skeleton and not a body. Something between, like a leaf pressed for a long time in a book.

She'd stopped eating, she'd told him once, on the night of the cutting. She'd already been sitting. She'd sat until she stopped, and gone on sitting.

She says you came, said Seed. She says you think very loudly, even in daylight.

"Sister," he said. "You asked for me."

She says the bell's changed. It isn't the fog warning any more, or the boat call, or the name.

She says it's the sound a bell makes when a boat comes round the point and sees the shore.

He didn't understand. Ren did. She'd gone rigid at the edge of the chamber with the rope still in her hands.

"It's here," said Shirasagi Ren. "Isn't it. Shiomi. They're here."

She says: in the bay below the mountain, where the village was, where the boats came home. There's fog on the bay this morning. There hasn't been fog on that bay in twelve hundred years. The fishermen in the town there now say they don't know where it came from.

She says it came from the deep water. It's them. They came quietly. They've been quiet a thousand years. They're very good at it.

They're waiting in the fog off the point for the lamp.

Under the gate the cedar needles stirred and lay still.

She says in Shiomi the boats never came in until the lamp was lit. Not in the worst fog, not in a storm. They waited off the point ringing their calls until a woman on the shore lit the lamp, so they knew where the shore was.

She says there's nobody to light it. There's her.

She's asking, said Seed. She's never asked anyone for anything; Ren said so, and I checked. Twelve hundred years of being talked to through a stone by monks and Kings and a girl who sits with hollows, and she's never asked for anything.

She's asking you to raise her.

He knelt on the stone at the edge of the chamber in the cedar shade with the staff across his knees.

He'd raised the dead of three nations, but never anyone in Yamato. You don't ask the dead, Ren had said once on this path in the dark. That's the rule in Yamato. Then she'd asked the monks under the gates, all four hundred and six of them, for the first time, and they'd woken and listened up instead of down.

Sister Kiyo, he thought. I'm going to tell you first what I'll do with your answer.

She says you always do. She's heard you do it from under this stone for a year and a half. Go on.

If you say yes, I'll raise you, with the Death Law, through the staff. You'll be able to stand and walk and see, and your voice will come through Seed as it does now, but out of you. You'll be dead and you'll stay dead. You won't be what you were. And when you want to stop, you can. I'll put you back or open the door, whichever you want. Nobody will hold you.

If you say no, I'll put the stone back over you, and nobody will ask again unless you want them to.

The chamber was quiet. On the path above, the Abbot's broom stopped.

She says: one question first. Will she be able to carry a lamp?

Yes.

She says: then yes. And hurry. They've been off the point twelve hundred years. Her brother was always in a hurry. He'll be complaining.

He did it gently. He did everything gently now; Seed said his Death Law had grown too wide to do anything else. Down through the staff to the cool dark door at the bottom, a little open. Not to it: past it, to where the dead are, where the ember in the small dry woman under the gate was waiting as she'd waited twelve hundred years, patient, sitting.

Get up, he said. If you want. You asked.

And she got up.

Not like Old Seven on the first night, two meters ten, rising out of the earth in rusted armor. Not like the First Legion in their rows. Slowly, as an old woman gets up from a cushion she's sat on a long time: one hand on the stone, then the other, the folded hands parting in her lap, the bowed head lifting. Light as paper. He felt her weight come up out of the chamber and it was almost nothing. A leaf. A lamp.

She stood under the three hundred and fortieth gate in the cedar shade in her rags of gray robe and lifted her face, paper over bone, toward the light coming down through the trees, and he saw her eyes. Not eyes: the places where eyes had been, dark, with a faint warmth in them, like a coal banked in a hearth for a very long night.

She says— Seed stopped. She says the light's very bright. She'd forgotten it's green, under cedars.

On the path above, the Abbot laid down his broom.

They carried her down the mountain.

She could walk, but not far, and it was three hours to the sea. Old Seven knelt on the path in front of her without being asked and held out his hand, the sergeant's hand with a finger and a thumb, flat. She looked at it a moment and then sat in it, as Hush had sat in it on the cut, and he stood up with her held against his chest like something light and precious, and walked down the mountain.

Under all twelve hundred and three gates. Past the terraces, where three thousand people in white stood as they went by, one terrace after another, silent, as the line at Port Halvard had stood. Past the hollow monks, who turned their heads. Past the Quiet with their candles. Down through the cedars to the road, and along the road through a small town of gray roofs and fishing boats that hadn't existed twelve hundred years ago, to the edge of a bay.

The fog lay on it.

He'd never seen fog like it. Not a mist: a wall, white and still, standing on the water from the end of the old stone breakwater to the point, motionless in the afternoon sun, as fog doesn't stand in afternoon sun. The town's fishermen were on the breakwater in boots and caps, staring at it. Nobody had gone out.

From inside it, very faint, a bell.

Not a bell anybody could hear. Seed heard it: a long low tone, a wait, and then the small bells, five quick notes, a little too fast, rung by someone who'd always been in a hurry.

She says: put me down.

There was no lamp.

He hadn't thought of it. Nobody had. They stood on the old stone breakwater in the late afternoon with a dead nun of twelve hundred years and a fog from the deep water and a bell nobody could hear, and there was no lamp.

Ren began to say something.

And Lingyue, who'd walked down the mountain beside him for three hours without a word, holding her stomach once when she thought nobody was looking, stepped past him to the end of the breakwater and held out her hand, palm up, to the small paper woman on the stones.

In her palm, a flame. Pale, steady, the size of a lamp. Not burning: held. Her light, as it had come back to her from underneath when she stopped hauling it up.

"Will this do?" said Shen Lingyue.

Sister Kiyo looked at the flame a long moment with the warm dark places where her eyes had been.

She says yes. It's the right color. The lamps in Shiomi burned whale oil, and they burned that color in the fog.

She says: give it to me.

Lingyue didn't know how. Neither did he. But she tipped her hand slowly, as you tip a cup to pour, and the pale flame ran off her palm into the small dry cupped hands of the nun and stayed there. Burning. Held. In the hands of the dead.

I didn't know light could do that, said Seed. I didn't know she could. I don't think she did either.

Sister Kiyo of Shiomi walked to the end of the old breakwater, slowly, light as paper, with a lamp in her hands, and held it up.

The fog didn't move.

The bell went on inside it, faint: the long tone, the wait, the five quick notes. Then it stopped.

Then, in the fog-code of a village gone twelve hundred years, which Sister Kiyo had taught her brother on this shore when he was small and which Seed had learned from her over a year and a half through a stone, slowly and carefully, as if whoever rang it wanted to be sure it was understood:

WE SEE THE LAMP.

WE SEE THE SHORE.

WE ARE COMING IN.

The fog came in.

Not drifting: coming. The whole white wall moved in off the bay toward the breakwater, slowly, as boats come in to shore in the dark when someone's lit the lamp, all together, in a line, toward the light. And in it, as it came, he saw them, with Seed's eyes, not his own. Lights. Small and dim, burning low, as the cousins burned and the lights in the hands of the one who picks. Hundreds. A village. Twelve hundred years quiet, coming in out of the fog toward a woman on the end of a breakwater with a lamp in her hands.

At the front, a little ahead of the rest, a little too fast, as someone moves who's always been in a hurry: one.

The fog reached the end of the breakwater and stopped and stood there, white and still, round the small paper woman with the lamp, and the light at the front of it came out and stopped in front of her.

Seed could hardly get it out. He felt it try twice.

He says: Kiyo. You got old.

"Tell him," said Yuanshu. His voice wasn't working; he made it work. He'd promised, on the vault steps, a long time ago. "Tell him Kiyo says: you talk too much."

The light in front of the nun went very still.

"Tell him she says: I waited. Tell him she says: light the lamp on the shore." He looked at the small dead woman at the end of the breakwater with the pale flame in her hands. "Tell him she lit it."

He says: I know. I saw. It's the right color.

He says: Kiyo, we were so quiet. For so long. We were so good at it. You wouldn't believe how quiet I was. Me. I didn't say anything for a thousand years.

He says: I've got so much to tell you.

Sister Kiyo of Shiomi stood at the end of the breakwater with the lamp in her hands and the fog all round her and her brother in front of her, and her face, paper over bone, didn't move at all. But her voice when it came through Seed wasn't dry any more. It wasn't twelve hundred years old.

She says: I know you have. You always did.

She says: come in, Ichiro. Come in out of the fog and tell me.
