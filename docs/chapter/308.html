Book 5: The Empty House

Chapter 308The Weir

czverse10 min read

Old Seven was still holding on when he went back up the slope.

He'd left the Collector at the wall. It hadn't wanted to come. It didn't say so, but he felt its warmth pressed close to the small uncrossed mark at the bottom of the long count and understood that it wasn't going to move for a while, and that this was allowed. He said, I'll be back. It said, Yes. He went.

Up the slope, out of the empty house, back along the end of the carry road into the dark, where a tall dead captain with one arm stood off the road on someone else's Field, facing uphill, a lost spark held to his chest.

There were three now.

He says two more slid into him while you were gone, said Seed. He didn't do anything. He just stood there, and they bumped into him and held on. He says he's a stone in a stream, that's all, it isn't hard.

He says there are a lot more coming down.

He could feel them all round in the dark on both sides of the road: the thin scatter of dim gray sparks, a few hundred, all sliding one way, slowly. A year of falling, and they were most of the way down. In a while, Seed said, at this rate a few days or a few weeks, they'd reach the middle of the hall, where he didn't look.

And go through. Unasked.

He sat down on the carry road beside Old Seven, as he sat on the vault steps on East Market Road when he needed to think, and thought.

I can't stop the slope.

No. It isn't yours.

But I've got one.

Seed took a moment, and when it answered it was one word.

Yes.

He'd been thinking about it since the first spark went by. Long ago, in a square in Lingzhou, on one of the worst nights of his life, a seed in the ground had been pulling the dying toward it, and he'd stood between them and it and opened his Field, and the slope of his Field had beaten the seed's pull. Beyond six hundred meters, Seed had said. Inside that the seed won; outside it, everything dying rolled downhill toward him instead.

Tannery Square. He hadn't thought about it in years.

If I open the Field here, he thought. Not big. Small. Up the slope from the door, between them and it. They'd roll toward me instead.

He thought of the old weir on the river below the ferry steps, where the water came down and piled up against the stones and stopped.

Like a weir. They'd come down the slope and fetch up against my Field and stop. And I could ask them.

I thought of it too, said Seed. Two days ago. I didn't say.

Why not?

Because of where the two Fields would touch.

It explained, slowly and carefully, the way it explained things it was afraid of.

Your Field's a slope. So is this one. Open yours here and there'll be a line where they meet: on one side things run toward you, on the other they run toward the door. Along that line the two slopes push against each other.

You'll feel the other one push. You won't be able to help it. And when something pushes on your Law, you learn it. That's how a Law grows. That's how yours grew at Tannery Square and the Western Pass and in the deep water: every time something pushed, you learned its shape.

Learn the shape of this one and your Law goes up. And this one's very large, and the wall's just past 59.9.

How fast?

I don't know. I'm not looking at it; that's the whole point of not looking. Keep your Field small and the line where they meet is short. Less pushing, less learning. Keep it very small and I think you can hold it a while before the number moves too far.

I don't know what a while is. I'm saying that now, before you ask.

He sat on the carry road and watched the sparks slide past in the dark. Old Seven beside him, one-armed, three lost lights against his chest, said nothing.

What's the smallest it can be, he thought, and still catch them?

They're coming down both sides of the road, spread out. The scatter's about three kilometers wide.

Three kilometers is small. I used to do five. Then ten.

Not here, said Seed. Here, three kilometers is the biggest thing you've ever held.

He did it anyway, but not fast; he'd learned that too. He sat on the carry road and let Seed walk him through it step by step, like a child learning to carry a full bowl across a room. Not the whole Field. A strip, three kilometers wide across the slope and only a few hundred meters deep, laid across the dark uphill of where the carry road ran down into the house. A weir. Low. Just enough tilt toward him that anything coming down would stop against it.

He opened it a crack and felt the other one push.

It wasn't like anything he'd felt before. The seed at Tannery Square had pulled like a drain. The gardener's reader had pressed like a hand. This was neither. It was like standing in a river that had run the same way since before there were rivers and putting your palm flat against the current. Not hostile. Not anything. Just ancient and enormous and going one way, with his hand in it.

59.4 to 59.5 percent, said Seed. Small. Keep it there.

He kept it there. The first spark came down the slope into the weir eleven minutes later and stopped.

He felt it stop: a dim gray light burning low after a year of falling, sliding out of the dark, and then the slope under it changing just enough that instead of going on toward the middle it came to rest against the edge of his Field, as a leaf comes to rest against a stone.

It turned toward him. They all turned.

It says: where am I? said Seed. They all say that.

Not in the house, he thought, down to it. Not yet. You're with me. I'm going to ask you something, and nobody's going to make you go anywhere. What do you want?

Most of them wanted to go home.

He hadn't expected that, and he should have. The cloud at the edge of the deep water had mostly wanted the door. They'd been picked long ago and fallen and lain cold in the dark, and when somebody finally asked, most of them said let me rest. But these had been on the carry road. They'd been in the hands, on the way to the house, at the very end, when the picker stopped. They'd been asleep in the hands and heard, all the way, the only thing the hands ever said. Home. And they'd thought it meant their own.

This one says: I want to go home, said Seed. It says a name. A world. I don't know it.

This one too. A different name.

This one wants the door. It says it's tired. It says thank you for asking.

This one wants to go home.

This one doesn't know. It says ask again.

He couldn't send them home. He knew it before Seed said it. He didn't have their threads, and Seed couldn't find their threads without looking at the slope, and they weren't looking at the slope. He was one boy in somebody else's Field at the bottom of the oldest road ever made, holding a weir open while Seed counted his Law up a tenth at a time, and a hundred lost lights from a hundred worlds he'd never see were piling up against it, asking whether they could go home.

Not yet, he told them. Later. I'm sorry. Wait here. You're safe here.

He didn't know if that was true.

59.5, said Seed. Holding.

The mark came on the twenty-ninth day, two days and an hour after it was sent.

He nearly missed it. The thread behind him was so long and thin now that a mark from home arrived like a sound from the far end of a valley, faint and late and whole. Square. Black. True from anywhere.

FOUR HOUSES. TWENTY SHIPS. SIX DAYS. FOR ME.

THEY WANT ME TO WRITE: THIS IS YOUR FACE. I WON'T.

THEY'LL BURN WALLS IN FRONT OF ME. TAM SAYS. I BELIEVE HIM.

I'M GOING TO MEET THEM AT THE KEEPER. IN THE FIELD. WITH THE COUSINS. TAM, SERI, SU MINGLI, THE NIGHT WATCH, STERLING.

LINGYUE ASKED ME. I SAID.

SHE'S HOLDING. SHE HATES IT. SHE SAYS TELL YOU SHE HATES IT.

DON'T TURN ROUND. YOU CAN'T GET HERE IN TIME ANYWAY. QIN HAO DID THE SUM.

— XIAOYU

He sat on the carry road in the dark with the weir open and a hundred lost lights against it and Old Seven beside him, and read it, and read it again.

Six days from when she sent it. She'd sent it two days ago.

Four days, then. Less, by now.

Seed.

I did the sum too, said Seed. It's twenty-nine days back along the carry road at the pace we came. Twenty-five, maybe, if the Collector hurries. The ships reach the keeper in four.

You can't get there. Nothing you do from here reaches there in time. A mark takes two days. By the time one from you arrives, it'll be two days before they come. By the time one from her comes back, it'll be over.

Over, he thought.

Whatever it turns out to be.

He didn't move. He thought of his sister, eighteen, in a school jacket she'd outgrown, standing in the ring of the keeper's field with a marker in her hand while twenty ships the size of cities came out of the dark burning a million people in their walls. On the bridge of each, a Paragon who'd stood in an Assembly and said the dim should burn so the bright could live, and who'd hold up a wall in front of her and say write it, and burn one.

He thought of her at the parapet the day he left for Anthos: You already decided. You don't get to sit on a step about it twice.

He thought of her on the north wall: No. You tell it. When you get home.

She'd decided. She always decided before him. She'd decided at sixteen in the back room of the Ossuary, marker in hand, that she'd keep the board. She'd decided on Awakening Day that she'd ask the oldest thing in the dark what it wanted. She'd decided at a long table two days ago, while he sat in front of a wall of tallies at the end of everything, that she'd stand in front of four Great Houses with a marker and tell them no.

And Lingyue had asked her. Lingyue, who hated asking, who wanted to decide for everyone she loved, had asked, and listened, and let her go.

I want to go home, he thought. Right now. More than anything.

I know.

I'm not going to.

I know that too.

He looked at the weir: the hundred lost lights piled against it from a hundred worlds, waiting, asking. At Old Seven, holding three. At the dark below, where the carry road ran down into an empty house and a very old warm thing sat by a wall at the end of a count, and at the middle, where he didn't look, something that had never once asked anybody anything stood open.

He couldn't get home. He couldn't help her. He could do this.

Send her a mark, he thought. Please. Now. It'll take two days. Send it anyway.

Tell me what to write.

He told it, and Seed set it down square, the way she would have.

XIAOYU. I CAN'T GET THERE. I'M SORRY. I KNOW YOU'VE DECIDED. YOU ALWAYS DECIDE BEFORE ME.

DON'T WRITE WHAT THEY WANT. WRITE WHAT'S TRUE.

AND ASK THEM WHAT THEY WANT. NOBODY EVER HAS. THAT'S HOW IT STARTS.

I'M AT THE END OF THE ROAD. THE HOUSE IS EMPTY. YOU WERE RIGHT. YOU'RE ALWAYS RIGHT. IT'S VERY ANNOYING.

— GE

The mark went up the thread behind him, small and square and true, into the dark, toward a small blue world twenty-nine days away, where it would land in two days: two days before a girl with a marker stood in a field of light and waited for twenty ships.

He let it go. Then he turned back to the weir and the lights against it, and to the next one sliding down out of the dark toward him, and asked it what it wanted.
