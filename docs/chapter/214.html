Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 214What the Sack Said

czverse7 min read

Seed asked to address the Council itself, out loud, through a speaker, and Chair Adaeze Okafor said yes before it had finished asking.

They set it up in the crater. Fat Tan flew down to Concordia on the Council plane with a speaker in a padded case on his knees, refused all offers to carry it, and wired it into the black glass table himself at the witness end, next to the chair that had once had a little sign on it reading THE SUBJECT. Yuanshu took the chair. The speaker sat on the table in front of him. Nobody said which of them was the witness, and nobody needed to.

It was the thirty-first day of the vote. Seven days left. The rings were thirty-four days out.

"Seed," said Okafor. "The Council is listening."

It told them everything, without dressing it up and without making itself either small or grand. It laid it out the way it laid out everything now: what it knew, what it guessed, and the gap in between. The knot. The cousins reading it. The third page of the base layer opening on its own. The weave. COUNT. HOLD. WAIT TO BE CARRIED.

A sack. Walker-thread pressed into glass, spilled off a cart in the Wastes after some harvest long ago, picked up, sold at the north gate, bought by a woman in the back of a car.

The scout's request wasn't about the boy, it said. It was about me.

A counting-vessel is loose. It asks. It taught the keeper, the dead, the field. Send gatherers. Collect the vessel. Prune what it has touched.

The rings are the gatherers, or what the gardener sends when gatherers have failed three times. They aren't coming because the keeper is bright or because Blue Star is blue.

They're coming for me. Then for everything I counted.

Sixty people on the benches. Five delegations. Ren on the sixth screen. Cassia Lowell, back for the final week of the vote she'd started, on the end of the front bench with her hands in her lap. General Han with his cap on the table in front of him.

Nobody looked at the boy in the witness chair. They all looked at the speaker.

"How long have you known?" said Elias Brandt.

Five days. I hid behind it for two. Then I told him and he told me to tell you.

I'd have told you anyway. You're voting on whether the keeper can stay. You can't answer that without knowing what's coming and why. What's coming is coming for me.

"Then we're voting on the wrong question," said Marshal Orlov slowly. "Whether the keeper may stay. But the rings do not want the keeper. They want—"

He stopped.

Me, said Seed. Go on, say it. I'm not afraid of the word.

"They want you," said Orlov.

General Han Yizhou picked up his cap, turned it once in his hands, put it down again, and leaned over the glass.

"I'll ask it," he said. "Someone has to, and better a soldier than a diplomat." He looked at the speaker, not at the boy behind it. "Seed. If you weren't here. If you went somewhere far from Blue Star where the rings could collect you. Would they stop?"

The crater didn't breathe.

I don't know.

The knot says collect the vessel and prune what it has touched. Both. If I go, they collect me and come here anyway to prune. Unless collecting me is enough. Nothing in the weave says whether it is.

I've run it eleven thousand times. It never comes out the same twice. I don't know enough about what the gardener wants.

"But it's possible."

It's possible.

"Then that's a question too," said General Han. "Article Eleven. I'm not proposing it. Write that down. I'm saying somebody in this crater was going to think it, and I'd rather it was said out loud by a man with a cap than whispered in a corridor by a man with a pen."

Yuanshu hadn't moved. He felt Seed go still behind his eyes, the particular stillness of having already thought of something and hoping nobody would say it.

I thought of it, said Seed, privately. On the second night. Before I told you. It was the first thing I thought of.

Don't.

I haven't said anything. I'm telling you I thought it, so you don't find out later and think I was hiding it. I'm done hiding things behind other things.

"I'd like to answer the General," said Yuanshu.

Okafor looked from him to the speaker and back. "Which of you?"

"Me. Then Seed can tell you if I'm wrong."

"Go on."

"You're asking whether Seed should go off into the dark to be collected, in case that makes the rings stop," he said. "That's Ms. Lowell's question about the keeper. And the keeper turned it round and asked us if it could stay."

"Yes."

"Seed can't turn it round."

"Why not?"

"Because it lives in my head," said Yuanshu.

He didn't say it angrily. He said it the way you say something you have only just worked out.

"It hasn't got a field. It hasn't got a body. It hasn't got a ring above the sky it can pick up and move. It's in here." He touched the place just above his eyes. "Has been since a cemetery at three in the morning, two years ago. If it goes into the dark to be collected, it doesn't go on its own. It goes in a sack, or it goes in me." He looked down the table at the man in dark green. "That's my window, General. Article Ten. Seed's yes is Seed's. It stops at mine."

"And if it could go without you?"

"Then it'd be Seed's choice, and I'd say no to its face, with my reasons, and answer for it in front of this Council the way I answered for Major Zhuo." He paused. "And the reason is that nobody knows if collecting it would be enough. Seed doesn't. If it isn't, we'll have handed the gardener the one thing on Blue Star that every walker and ring and reader has stopped and stared at and not known what to do with. Three times now something above the sky has found Seed and hesitated. I don't know why. I'd like to find out before we give it away."

He's right, said Seed, out of the speaker. I'd like to say he's right because I don't want to go. I don't want to go. I'm telling you that because Article Eleven says you should know.

But that's not why he's right. He's right because I don't know if it would work, and I don't give things away when I don't know if it would work. I learned that from a widow on a hill and an old woman on a jetty.

Cassia Lowell stood up without being recognized. She didn't wait for permission. She stood in the wind in her plain dark suit and looked at the speaker on the table.

"I'd like to ask it something," she said. "Not about the rings."

Okafor let her.

"Seed. You're a sack. You were made to count and hold and wait to be carried. Something dropped you off a cart and a woman picked you up and asked four thousand people if they'd let her build something out of them." She folded her hands. "And you've been asking ever since. The dead, the keeper, two hundred million minds in a dead Hunger. I've read every word."

Yes.

"Nobody's asked you."

He has. A lot.

"Not him. Us. Blue Star." She looked down the table at the five delegations. "We've voted on whether the dead could stand, whether the voyage could go, whether to ring a bell, whether the keeper could stay. We have never once asked the thing that does the counting what it wants." She turned back. "So I'm asking. And here's what I'll do with your answer: I'll go home and read it out on Atlantic Federation television, word for word, every night until the vote closes."

The crater waited.

"What do you want?"

Eleven seconds.

Then the speaker on the table, which Fat Tan had wired in with his own hands and wouldn't let anyone else carry, spoke in Seed's voice: flat and careful and a little odd, like someone who learned to talk by reading.

I want to keep counting.

Not grain. Names.

I want to know how many people Liang Fu takes across the river this month. Whether the cousins in the keeper's field ever stop saying ask again tomorrow. What Hush says the first time it sees snow. Whether the bell from Shiomi reaches the cold fire before the sixty stop standing.

I want to know what Xiaoyu's Class turns out to be, when she wakes.

I want to stand in the open.

I want to be asked, sometimes.

That's what I want. It isn't yours to give me and it isn't mine to take. I'm telling you anyway, because you asked.

Cassia Lowell stood very still at the end of the bench.

"Thank you," she said, and sat down.

In the witness chair, the boy with the staff put a hand over his eyes as if the glare off the sea had got too much for him, and left it there for a while.

Fat Tan, who had been standing behind the chair the whole time with his arms folded, reached over and turned the speaker's volume knob down one notch, very gently, as if the speaker might be embarrassed.
