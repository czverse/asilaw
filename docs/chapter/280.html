Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 280Precedence

czverse10 min read

They began the crossing at eleven, and at eleven minutes past the sky over East Ring Road opened and the First of the Ladder came down through it on foot.

They'd planned for it, all of them, for two days at the long table in the banking hall, with Iselde's drawings of the flagship and Kwan's laptop and Gao Min's ledgers and Su Mingli with her arms folded. Seed had run it four thousand times. He'd stopped asking for the number after the first hundred.

Twenty-three minutes, said Seed. That's how long it takes, from the knot in our stone along Wen Jia's thread into the net, across, through the knot in the deep water, and into the Lens. A mark takes a second. A mind's heavier than a mark, and a mind and a lamp are heavier than that.

Twenty-three minutes, and the stone has to stay whole the whole time, and Wen Jia has to hold the knot.

If the stone breaks, they're in the net with nowhere to go.

So: twenty-three minutes. A hall. A stone. A city.

And a man nobody marked could lift a hand against.

Sister Kiyo's body sat cross-legged on the platform before the warm black stone, as she'd sat under a gate for twelve hundred years, her dry hands cupped in her lap round a lamp that was Lingyue's light. The keeper had carried her from Shiomi at dusk down a road of gold. Ichiro was with her: not a body but a light, small, a little too quick, hovering at her shoulder as, Seed said, he'd always walked half a step ahead of her on the shore.

At eleven she'd laid one dry hand flat on the stone.

...hello, said Wen Jia. ...I've been expecting you. Both of you. Come in. It's warm. Don't let go of the lamp.

She says she hasn't let go of it for six months and she's not starting now.

And they'd gone in. He'd felt it: down through the tree of Laws to the knot at the bottom, past Wen Jia holding it, and up the thread into the dark, slowly, heavier than a mark. A nun and a fog-ringer and a lamp, toward a knot in the deep water, across it, and out along another thread toward a disc of black stone ground thin as glass in the bow of a ship forty times the size of the Candle, two hundred kilometers overhead.

The body on the platform stayed sitting, its hand on the stone, the lamp in its lap still burning. Seed said that was right: the body was the shore, and the lamp had to stay lit on the shore until the boat was in.

At eleven minutes past, the sky opened.

Not a crinkle, not a fold. The sky over East Ring Road, over the long curved roof of the Awakening Hall and the trees and the crowd on the pavements, simply parted like a curtain, and out of the dark beyond it, standing on nothing, came a man.

He didn't fall. He didn't fly as a Lord flies, deciding where down is. He walked: down through the air over the city as if there were a staircase only he could see, at an even pace, unhurried, hands at his sides. Tall, though not as tall as Old Seven; a man's height. In white, with gold at collar and cuffs, and at his throat on a fine chain a disc of black stone with three gold marks and a fourth above them, small, like a star.

He came down onto the roof of the Awakening Hall, stood there, and looked down.

The crowd on East Ring Road fell silent all at once. Not by choice. Yuanshu felt it go through them: thousands of people, men and women and old folk marked forty years ago by a stone in this hall and told what they were, going still. Hands at their sides. Breath held. As water goes still in a cup when you stop moving the cup.

Precedence, said Seed. He hasn't done anything. He's only arrived. Everyone on East Ring Road with a mark lower than his has yielded. Everyone.

At the corner of the platform Su Mingli had stopped moving.

He saw it: the King of Gravity, who'd held a forty-thousand-tonne bridge across this city with one hand and pulled the tip of the gardener's branch down out of the sky, standing at the corner of the platform with her arms half unfolded, as if she'd been about to do something and it had been taken out of her hands. Her face didn't change. Her eyes moved. Nothing else did.

"Can't," she said through her teeth. "Can't lift… anything. At him. Not a fold. Not a weight." Her eyes went to Yuanshu. "It's like trying to hit your own mother."

Beside her Qin Hao stood with white fire banked in his healed pink hands, hands open, nothing coming out of them. Dabao, A-rank, Unbroken Wall, frozen in the side aisle with his great shield on his arm and his mouth open. The night watch at the doors.

And on the bottom step of the platform, Lingyue.

She'd stood when the sky opened. She had her grandfather's sword in both hands, and she wasn't moving.

He saw her try. He saw her jaw set and the cords stand out in her neck and the light come up off her, pale, as it did in the mornings, and her arms not move a centimeter. The sword in her hands as if set in stone.

"It's in me," said Shen Lingyue through her teeth. "SSS. The mark. I crossed it out. It's still in me."

Up on the roof, the First of the Ladder looked down through the high windows at the platform. At the stone. At a dead nun sitting before it with her hand on it and a lamp in her lap. At a boy beside her with a white staff.

"You," said Dacian Severin.

Not loud. He didn't need loud. The word came down through the roof as a stone sinks through water.

"The ungraded one."

And he stepped off the roof.

He came down through the long curved roof of the hall as if it weren't there. He didn't break it; the roof was simply behind him, and then he was in the air over the four thousand seats, walking down his invisible staircase toward the platform, unhurried, hands at his sides. The holders in the side aisle yielded as he passed over them. The night watch at the doors. Every one of them who'd been marked.

Twenty minutes.

And from the four thousand seats, from the aisles, from the dark under the tiers where they'd stood since dusk, the dead rose.

Not raised. Asked. Two days before, down the net, nine by nine, the slow way: a man is coming whom nobody marked can lift a hand against. The dead are not marked. Will you stand in front of the stone? It had come back as the net answered everything now, a pile of index cards on the long table, and on top the one that had gone round six hundred times:

The stones never read us. We were never on any ladder. Yes.

The First Legion in their rows of nine, rising out of the dark under the tiers where they'd lain since dusk. The cemetery companies. Grid Nine. Through the side doors the Spear Corps, two hundred and seventy-two, round the platform, facing out. Rusted spears and swords and shovels and lengths of anchor chain, and one pick.

And at the foot of the steps, between the stone and the man walking down out of the air: Old Seven.

The First of the Ladder stopped, in the air above the aisle, ten meters up on his invisible staircase. He looked down at the dead.

"Ah," he said.

The Spear Corps struck first.

Two hundred and seventy-two spears thrust up at the man in the air at once, pale fire on every blade. Yuanshu gave it to them himself, down through the staff: Lingyue's light and his Law wound together. Lingyue couldn't lift her sword against Severin, but the light in the staff wasn't hers any more; it had been his for two years, every time she'd laid her hand on it and lit it. It went down the bone of the staff and the bones of the dead and up the spears.

It reached him.

Not Precedence: the dead weren't marked. He felt it reach, two hundred and seventy-two points of pale fire touching a man in white with gold at his collar.

And stop.

Not yield. Stop, as a stone stops against a wall. The man in the air didn't move. The pale fire on the spears guttered against him and went out, and two hundred and seventy-two spears stood frozen in the air with their points a hand's breadth from his white coat.

It isn't Precedence, said Seed. It's him. His body. His realm.

He isn't a King. He's above a King. Star Monarch. I didn't know there was such a thing. I'm reading it now. I'm learning. It's very fast.

He doesn't need Precedence for the dead. He's simply stronger than anything in this hall.

The First of the Ladder lifted one hand.

Not fast, not dramatic. As a man lifts a hand to brush a moth from his face.

And the Spear Corps went back. All two hundred and seventy-two dead soldiers of the Eastern Line, thrown off the platform and across the floor into the first tier of seats, spears and bones and rusted armor, like leaves blown back from a door opened in a gale.

Seventeen minutes. The First Legion. Now.

They came over the tiers, eighteen thousand of them, down the steps between the seats in their rows of nine as they'd come down the slope of the War Memorial Cemetery for a wedding, toward the man in the air, pale fire on every rusted blade. Not one each this time. Yuanshu poured it, everything he had, down through the staff into the dead, until the whole Awakening Hall was full of pale burning edges coming at one man from every side.

He didn't throw them back. He walked through them.

Down the invisible staircase at the same even pace, unhurried, hand still raised, and where his hand went the dead went aside. Not destroyed: moved, folded out of his way as water folds round the bow of a ship. Eighteen thousand of the dead of Blue Star, and the First of the Ladder came down through them like a man walking down a corridor that happens to be full of people, without looking at any of them.

Toward the platform. Toward the stone.

He isn't fighting them. He doesn't need to. He's going to the stone to break it.

If the stone breaks, Ichiro and Kiyo are in the net with nowhere to go. Fifteen minutes.

At the foot of the steps, Old Seven didn't move aside.

The First of the Ladder reached the bottom of his invisible staircase and stepped down onto the floor of the Awakening Hall before the platform, for the first time onto the polished stone where every eighteen-year-old in Lingzhou had walked for forty-two years to be told what they were. Between him and the steps stood a tall dead captain of the Eastern Line, two meters ten, in rusted armor, spear grounded in a finger and a thumb.

Severin lifted his hand.

Old Seven didn't go aside. He went back a meter, as a man goes back who's been hit by something he decided to be hit by. His feet scraped on the stone. His spear didn't leave the floor. He stopped a meter back at the foot of the steps and stood there.

The First of the Ladder looked at him.

For the first time since the sky opened, something moved in his face. Not much: a ripple, as on a still pool when something very small has dropped into it from very high.

"You're dead," he said in Huaxian, perfectly. "You were never on the Ladder."

He says: no. I was on a wall.

Severin lifted his hand again.

This time it wasn't a brush. It was a man who'd never once in his life needed to raise his voice, raising it. The air in the hall went heavy all at once, as it had gone heavy when Sterling decided for a stone at Port Halvard, except that Sterling had been a King and this was something above a King, and the heaviness didn't settle. It came down: on the foot of the platform steps, on a tall dead captain standing in front of them with his spear.

And Old Seven's left arm broke.

Everyone in the hall heard it: a crack like a branch going in a hard frost. The sergeant's arm, put on in a courtyard among potted plants by a boy who didn't know anatomy. Huo Changsheng's arm, with a finger and a thumb.

It came off at the seam.

It fell on the polished floor at the foot of the platform steps, and the finger and thumb slowly opened, as a hand opens when it's let go of something, and lay still.

Old Seven didn't go down.

He stood at the foot of the steps with one arm, as he'd stood on the north wall in the Black Tide, as he'd stood two years at the bronze door of the Ossuary. His spear was in his right hand. He didn't look at the arm on the floor. He looked at the man in front of him.

And he lifted the spear.

He says—

Seed's voice did something Yuanshu had never heard it do. It broke.

He says that was the sergeant's. He had those fingers forty years.

He says: I was using them.

Twelve minutes.
