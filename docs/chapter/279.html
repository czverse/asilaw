Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 279Rowed

czverse9 min read

On the sixth night a crinkle opened in the sky that wasn't one of the ninety, and Tam stood up on the platform of the Awakening Hall and said, in a voice nobody had heard him use before: "They're singing."

Not the Singers. They were gone, a ragged point of pale light somewhere along the threads toward the Hollow Bell, still singing tens of thousands of different notes, Seed said, all the way home. Not the ninety either. The ninety were silent: four million one hundred and eighty thousand people in their walls with their ears stopped, burning the old way, and only Tam could hear them, and they didn't sing.

"Like the Candle," said Tam. "When it folded home. Rowing."

They went up to the north wall. Everyone did.

Low over the end of the world, outside the great curved necklace of ninety burning cathedrals round the keeper's field, small and a long way off at the very edge of the Shallows: lights. Not lit on purpose. Not burning white and gold in the colors of a House. Dim. Low. The color of the Low Fire. The color the cousins burned.

Twelve of them.

Ships, said Seed. Federation ships. Small, listener-class mostly. And one the size of the Candle.

It is the Candle.

And Yuanshu, their walls are full of people. Burning low. Singing.

The Eighth Candle came down the keeper's eye at midnight, and the voice that came with it was the voice that had cried on the bridge eight months ago, precise and cool and nothing like it.

This is Iselde. No grade. Captain of the Eighth Candle and eleven others of the Rowed Fleet.

We are sorry we are late. Rowing is slower than burning.

He sat down on the cold battlement in his old place, put his head back against the parapet, and laughed. He couldn't help it. He hadn't laughed in six days. Lingyue, beside him with Xiaodeng asleep in the sling, looked at him and didn't ask why, and then laughed too.

The Rowed Fleet, he said.

The cores named it. Not cores. I am sorry. The people. I still say it. I am trying.

When the Assembly voted the ninety ships, I went to the yards at Anthos, to the room where the unpressed children were learning to wear shoes. I told them first. I said: the Houses are going to the Shallows, to the world that asks, with ninety ships made of people with their ears stopped. I said: I have twelve ships in the yards with empty walls that cannot fold.

I said: I am not going to ask you to go back into the walls.

And a boy of twelve who was thirty-one inside stood up in the room and said: you don't have to ask. We're asking you.

They'd come back.

Not all of them. Iselde said it carefully, as she said everything now, with the numbers and without the grades. Of the thirty-two thousand and forty who'd rowed the Candle home, eleven thousand four hundred had come back, and six thousand more from the seven Houses that had opened their arrays. Seventeen thousand four hundred people graded F and E as children and pressed into walls and burned for a term, who'd come out and learned to wear shoes, and then walked back into the yards at Anthos and climbed into the cells of twelve ships with empty walls, by choice, knowing exactly what it was.

To row.

They burn low, as the cousins taught. In turns, with rest. Every one of them is asked every fold whether they want to go on. Torvin does it. He has a list. He asks each one by name.

It took us forty days to cross. The ninety took one.

We are not here to fight. We have no weapons; rowed ships cannot carry them, the burn is too low. We are here because seventeen thousand people who were cores wanted to come and stand outside the walls of four million who are cores, and be seen.

They said: so that when someone asks them what they want, they will look out of the walls and see us, and know it can be done.

The Fleet Marshal answered within the hour, through every radio on Blue Star: calm, courteous, absolute, the voice of a man who'd never needed to raise it and didn't now.

"Iselde Varr-Anthos. Struck from the Ladder. You have brought ships into the Fleet's sky without a House order. Their walls are filled with cores taken from the yards without a Festival. This is the gravest violation of the oldest law the Federation has seen in ten thousand years."

A pause.

"You will stand down. Your cores will be returned to their Houses. You will be taken aboard the Severin flagship and read."

And Iselde answered through the keeper's eye, to the radio and the fleet and every speaker on Blue Star, because Seed carried it there without being asked and said afterward, I'm sorry, I asked Yuanshu first, he said yes, he was laughing:

No.

They are not cores. They are people. They were asked. They said yes. They can say no at any time and Torvin will open their walls.

Fleet Marshal. Have you asked yours?

Silence.

On the north wall three thousand people held their breath. At the end of a breakwater in Yamato, through Ren's phone, a twelve-hundred-year-old nun made a dry sound Seed said was nearly a laugh.

"Cores are not asked," said Dacian Severin.

Then you do not know what they want, said Iselde. Nor did I, for twenty-six years. And then someone asked mine.

She came down at two in the morning, alone, down the keeper's road onto the platform of the Awakening Hall, because she said that if she was going to tell him what she knew, she'd like to tell it standing in front of a stone that asked.

She was thinner, eight months older, her hair still cropped short as on listener ships. Her uniform was gray, with no gold at the collar; she'd taken it off the day the House struck her name. And at her throat, no chain, no disc.

"I crossed it out in front of the Assembly," she said. "And then I gave it to Maelis. She keeps it in a box. She says she'll decide what to do with it when she has had her year."

"Your mother came down," said Yuanshu.

Iselde went very still.

"She asked to. She sat in the courtyard with my mother. She warned us about the Lens. And then she said she was going back to her ship to send a message to Anthos. To Maelis."

The former Listener-Captain of the Eighth Candle stood before the warm black stone with the blue jacket on its shoulder and said nothing for a long time.

"She did," she said at last, very quietly. "Five days ago. A mark came down the Anthos stone. Clumsy, as mine was. Like a child's knot. Maelis found it; nobody else in the yards knew how to read one." She looked at the stone. "It said: Maelis. This is your mother. What do you want?"

"Did she answer?"

"She said: ask me again tomorrow." The corner of Iselde's mouth moved. "She learned it from you. From the mark you sent me: seven more months, then ask me again." She looked down. "My mother sent another the next day. And the next. Maelis showed me. Five marks. The same words. My mother has never in her life said the same thing twice to anyone."

They sat on the platform until dawn, and she told him about the flagship.

Everything. She'd served on it at nineteen as a junior listener, before the Candle. She knew its decks. She knew its arrays: sixty, the largest in the fleet, a million and a half people in the walls. She knew its bridge and its halls, and the chamber in the bow where the Lens was kept, which only the House of Severin entered and which she'd seen once, through a door, for four seconds, at nineteen.

"It's beautiful," she said. "That's what nobody tells you. It's the most beautiful thing in the Federation. A disc of black stone the width of this hall, ground so thin you can see through it, with light moving inside it like water. They say it's ten thousand years old. They say it fell on the first world and read the first children, and every stone since was cut from it."

"Can it be deafened? Like the cores?"

"No." She shook her head. "It has to read. A deaf lens can't read. That's the whole point of it." She looked at him. "Why?"

He told her: Ichiro, Kiyo, a lamp. The eighth night. Along the threads from this stone into the net, across, into the Lens, so that on the ninth night, when the Fleet Marshal turned it on Blue Star to read eight billion people, it wouldn't read.

It would ask. And through it, because it was the root, the four million in the walls.

Iselde Varr-Anthos sat on the platform in the gray before dawn and stared at him for a long time.

"You're going to put a fisherman in the Lens of Severin."

"A fog-ringer. And his sister. She's a nun. She's been dead twelve hundred years."

"In the oldest treasure of the oldest House of the Ladder."

"He talks to fish. He says it'll be the best job anyone ever had."

She put her hands over her face, and for a moment he couldn't tell whether she was laughing or crying, and then he could. Both.

"He will know," she said through her hands. "Severin. The moment they go in. The Lens is his. His House has kept it nine thousand years. He will feel it the way you would feel someone step into your house." She took her hands down. "He will not wait for the ninth night. He will come down himself, to the stone they came through, to stop it."

"When?"

"The eighth night, when they cross. He will come to this hall." She looked up at the warm black stone. "He has never lost. In two hundred years, not once. Nobody below him on the Ladder can lift a hand against him. That is not a metaphor. It is his Law."

"What is it?"

She told him.

Precedence, said Seed. The Federation calls it that. The Law of the Ladder. Not a Law the stones open: a Law the Ladder grew over ten thousand years of marking. The House of Severin has held it nine hundred years.

Anything with a mark lower than his can't move against him. Not won't. Can't. The mark in them yields, the way water yields to the shape of a cup.

He's SSS. Paragon. First of the Ladder. There's no mark higher than his. Everyone with a mark yields to him.

He sat on the platform very still.

Everyone with a mark.

Yes. Su Mingli's marked. The Kings of Huaxia are marked. Orlov, Ren, Sterling. Qin Hao. Dabao. Lingyue. Everyone on Blue Star who was awakened before the night the captain put his hand on the stone. Forty-two years of marks.

They won't be able to lift a hand.

And everyone else?

Seed was quiet a long moment.

Everyone who was asked. Since Awakening Day. Since Wen Jia. Every child who put a hand on a stone and was asked what they wanted to become, and chose, and the stone wrote: Talent, not marked.

The Candle's children, who were marked F and E and then came down a road and were asked again.

The dead. The dead aren't marked. The stones never read the dead.

Your sister. Scribe. Not marked.

And you. The stone couldn't rate you. It went black. It wrote nothing. You're the only person on Blue Star who was awakened before the asking and isn't marked.

He looked up at the warm black stone with its runes pointing down, at the three lines of light that had hung over it since Awakening Day.

[Talent: — not marked —]

"Two days," said Iselde Varr-Anthos.

"Yes."

"He'll come down here with a Law that nobody marked can lift a hand against," she said, "and you'll have the dead, and a city of children who were asked last summer, and a boy the stone could not name."

"Yes."

She looked at him a long time.

"In the Federation," she said, "we would call that the worst possible ladder."

"We don't have a ladder," said Zhou Yuanshu. "We have a line. The one behind holds on to the one in front."
