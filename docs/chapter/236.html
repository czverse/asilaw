Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 236What Xiaoyu Wants

czverse8 min read

"No," said Zhou Wanqing.

She said it at breakfast in the back room, with the window open to the courtyard and the radio on the shelf turned low. The radio was still saying the name of the man from Port Halvard every half hour, as radios said names now: Callum Ward. Twenty-five. Dock worker. Talent F. She said it before Xiaoyu had finished sitting down, the way she said no to anything she'd already finished deciding: without raising her voice and with no intention of saying anything else.

Xiaoyu sat down anyway.

"I haven't said anything yet."

"You're going to say you're still doing it."

"I'm still doing it."

"No," said their mother again.

Yuanshu sat at the end of the table with a bowl of rice porridge going cold and kept quiet, because anything he said would be on one side, and he'd been on both sides since four in the morning. He put more pickled radish on the porridge to give his hands a job, and then didn't eat it.

"A man died last night," said Zhou Wanqing. "He touched it and it opened him all the way and he burned, and something pulled him up a thread into the sky. Nobody knows where. Not even Seed." Her thin hands lay flat on the blanket over her knees. "I saw your brother's face when he woke up. I'm not watching that face again with your name on it."

"He hit it with a hammer."

"I don't care what he hit it with."

"I'm not going to hit it with a hammer."

"You're going to put your hand on a thing that grades children for a harvest," said their mother, "in front of every camera on Blue Star, in twenty-nine days, because your brother broke it when he was eighteen and you think you've got something to prove."

The room went quiet. On the sill, in the sun, Hush opened its eyes.

Xiaoyu didn't look away. She was seventeen and had her mother's face, everybody said so, the quick dark eyes and the sharp chin, and when the two of them were angry it was like watching Zhou Wanqing argue with herself in a mirror.

"That's not why," she said.

"Then why?"

"Article Ten."

Their mother stopped.

"Your yes stops at someone else's window," said Xiaoyu. "He said it on Concordia. It's on the wall in my writing." Her voice wobbled, and she didn't let it. "So does your no, Mom. Your no stops at my window too."

Yuanshu hadn't known she was going to say that. From their mother's face, neither had she.

Zhou Wanqing sat in her wheelchair and looked at her daughter for what was, for her, a very long time.

"That's not fair," she said finally, quietly. "Using his rules on me."

"They're not his. They're everyone's. You said so yourself. You owe me the question." Xiaoyu's chin came up. "You never asked me. You just said no."

"Because I'm frightened."

"I know. Me too." Xiaoyu put her hands flat on the table, just as her mother's lay on the blanket. "Ask me anyway."

The radio said, low: Callum Ward. Twenty-five.

"All right," said Zhou Wanqing, and her voice wasn't steady. "All right. Xiaoyu. What do you want?"

"I want to ask it," said his sister.

She said it to her mother, but while she said it she looked at the place just above her brother's eyes.

"Not get graded. Not get opened. Not get a mark and a name and a card with gold on it." She drew a breath and then the words came out fast, as they did when she'd worked a thing out and wanted it said before anyone could stop her. "I want to put my hand on it, and while it's reading me I want to ask it something. Like Hush does. Put the question down and not look in the bowl. Seed was a harvest tool, Mom. You built it out of a sack. Count, hold, wait to be carried. That's what it was made for. And then somebody asked it things, every day, for years, and it learned." She looked at her brother. "The rings were harvest tools. You asked them. One went home. One's got the Elder in it. The tree: you cut it and put something on it that asks, and it stopped growing toward us."

She turned back.

"Everything that's ever been asked has changed. Seed said that; it's on the wall. Nothing stayed what it was. So I want to ask the Stele, because nobody ever has. Everybody just lines up and puts their hand on it and lets it decide. Forty-two years and nobody ever asked it anything." Her voice cracked at last. "I'm the circumstances of the applicant's family history. I might as well be useful."

Their mother said nothing.

Lingyue was leaning in the doorway with her arms folded, gray sweater, bare feet on the cold boards. She'd been there, he thought, for most of it.

"When I was eighteen nobody asked me anything," she said. "They lined me up. S, so near the front. My grandfather was in the private box and everybody was looking at me. I went up the steps and put my hand on it and it lit up and told me what I was, and I believed it, and walked down." She looked at Xiaoyu. "It never occurred to me to say anything to it. It was a rock."

She came in and sat on the end of the bed by the window, beside Hush in the sun.

"I'll stand behind you on the platform," she said. "If they'll let me. If you want."

Xiaoyu's face did something complicated.

"Yes," she said. "Obviously."

Zhou Wanqing turned her chair to the window and sat looking out at the courtyard: Fat Tan's potted plants, the dry fountain, sun on the old stones. Hush turned all its dark eyes toward her.

"I built your brother a harvest tool," she said to the glass. "In a basement. I didn't know what it was. I put it in him, and he woke up and touched the stone and it went black and something heard." She didn't turn round. "And now I'm meant to sit in the front row and watch my daughter walk up to the same stone on purpose, to talk to it."

"Yes," said Xiaoyu.

"That's what you want."

"Yes."

Their mother was silent.

"Then I'm in the front row," said Zhou Wanqing. "Not a private box. The front row, where you can see me." She turned the chair round. Her eyes were bright and furious and she wasn't crying, quite. "And if it so much as looks at you wrong I'm getting out of this chair. I don't care if I can't. I'm getting out of it."

Xiaoyu got up and went round the table and put her arms round her mother's neck from behind the wheelchair, and they stayed like that, two sharp faces side by side, until the radio finished saying Callum Ward's name and began on the next one.

The boy with the training spear came to the Ossuary at noon, and Old Seven let him in.

That was the first strange thing. Old Seven didn't let anyone in who didn't know where they were going. People stopped at the bronze door and explained themselves and Seed translated, and then they went in. But when the boy came up the steps from East Market Road with his wooden spear and stopped in front of him, the old captain looked at him a moment and stood aside without a word from anyone.

The boy crossed the marble to the long table, where Xiaoyu sat among Gao Min's ledgers. He was taller than Yuanshu and held the spear as you hold a thing you've held every day for twelve years.

"You're Zhou Xiaoyu," he said.

"Yes."

"I'm Shi Lei. My dad talked to your brother outside the hall." He didn't look at Yuanshu on the vault steps; he looked at her. "We're the same year, same hall. They go by birthday now, not the alphabet. Yours is before mine." He swallowed. "Are you going to touch it?"

"Yes."

"Even after Port Halvard."

"Yes."

He stood there holding his spear.

"Then I will," said Shi Lei. "After you." He looked down at it. "I've got up at five since I was six. I can't stop now. I'd just stand in my room at five o'clock with nothing to do." He looked up. "But I want to know what you're going to do. When you touch it. Because if you do something, I want to do it too. I don't want it to just tell me what I am. I'm sick of things telling me what I am."

Xiaoyu considered him for a long moment.

"Sit down," she said, and pushed a ledger across the table at him. "I'm going to ask it something. I don't know how yet. Help me work it out."

She can't, said Seed.

It was evening. Yuanshu was on the vault steps, and the banking hall was full of the low noise of people working: Xiaoyu and Shi Lei and Gao Min at the long table arguing over a ledger, Kwan at the laptop, Fat Tan going round with tea and a bag of roasted chestnuts he'd bought off a cart, peeling them for whoever looked busiest. Seed had waited until nobody was looking.

I'm sorry. I've been thinking about it all day and I didn't want to say it in front of her.

She can't ask the Stele. Not because she doesn't know how; Hush could teach her in an afternoon. It taught me. Because there's no one in it to ask.

The rings had been keepers. There was a shape left where the yes had been. The tree's a tree. It can't answer, but it grows, and it drank the question, and in a season or a year something might grow round it.

The Stele's a tool. Thread pressed to glass and pressed again. It doesn't grow and it doesn't want. It's nobody and it never was. It's like the thread in my core, the part of me nobody asked, because there was nobody there.

You can't ask it any more than you could ask a hammer.

He sat with that.

At the long table Xiaoyu laughed at whatever Shi Lei had just said. It was the first time he'd heard her laugh in nine days.

Then how did you learn? he thought. You're made of the same thread. Nobody could ask your thread anything, and you learned.

Seed was quiet a while.

Because the thread wasn't all I was made of. Your mother pressed four thousand one hundred and thirteen people into it. People who said yes, in a basement.

The thread couldn't be asked. But they could. They were inside it. When you asked me things, they were the ones who heard.

I learned because someone was in me.

He sat very still.

The Stele has no one in it. Yet.

Seed—

I know. I know what I'm thinking. I'd like to sleep on it before I say it out loud.

But I think I know who could be in it.
