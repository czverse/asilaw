Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 250Withdrawal

czverse10 min read

He didn't sleep that night, and not because of the hand.

They'd put him in a fourth-floor room of the harbor hotel, the one Sterling's people had taken over for the officers of the dead and the three living, with a window looking out over the quay. He lay on the bed in the dark with the staff on the floor beside him and the window open to the cold wind off the Atlantic, and listened to the harbor. Fifteen thousand people don't go home quietly. They went in groups, slowly, talking and laughing and crying, and now and then under the window a name was shouted and another name shouted back, and then nothing: two people holding on to each other on a quay at three in the morning.

The sixty-one, going home.

He lay in the dark and listened, and wanted the light.

It wasn't like pain. He'd been hurt plenty of times and knew pain, and this wasn't it. It was simpler and worse. For five minutes at the end of the north pier a door in his chest had stood open, exactly as far as he'd asked, and everything he'd ever touched had burned up in him at once. Then Seed had shut it, gently, all the way, because he'd told it to.

Now there was a shut door in his chest. He knew exactly where it was and exactly what was behind it, and every part of him wanted to open it again.

He didn't. He lay there and didn't.

Lingyue was in the chair by the window. She hadn't gone to her own room. She sat with the sword across her knees and the flame in her palm turned down to a candle's worth, so the room had a little light and no more.

"It doesn't go," she said after a long while, without turning round. "I'm not going to lie to you."

"I know."

"It gets less. It's been nine weeks for me, and it's less." She looked at the flame. "But I still know where it is. The door. I still think about it every time I light this. How it'd be to just let it go, all of it, everything at once." She closed her hand and the room went dark. "Then I don't."

"How?"

She was quiet a while.

"I think about what I'd be burning it for," said Shen Lingyue. "On the wall it was for you, for the city. It was worth it, so it felt like that." She opened her hand and the small flame came back. "If I did it now, in a hotel room, it'd just be for how it felt. And then it'd be the thing that ate the Sixth. That's all that is. Burning for how it feels."

He lay in the dark and turned that over.

"That's not much to hold on to," he said.

"No," said his wife. "It's what I've got."

I felt it too, said Seed.

He didn't know how long it had been. The harbor had gone quiet below. Lingyue's flame was out and she was asleep in the chair, upright, a hand on the sword, as soldiers sleep.

I didn't say. I'm saying now. When I stood in the door, in front, with my back to the light, I could feel it on me. All of it. Everything the docker opened in you.

I'm a counting tool. I was made to count bright and hold it and wait to be carried. And there was more bright behind me than I've ever counted, and it was yours, and it would have been very easy to turn round.

He lay very still.

Did you want to?

Yes. For a moment. Not to take it, not like the hand. To hold it, the way I was made to.

And then I thought: he asked for this far, not further. And a thing that holds what someone gave it past where they asked is the Collector.

So I didn't turn round. I wanted you to know, so you know you're not the only one in here who knows where the door is.

For a while he didn't say anything.

Thank you, he thought.

You're welcome. Your heart rate's ninety-one. It's been ninety-one for an hour. It was a hundred and forty on the pier.

I think that means it's getting less.

Sterling was in the Federation hospital on the hill above the harbor, in a bed by a window with his scarred hands on the blanket and a strip of plaster across his nose, and he laughed when Yuanshu came in.

"Look at you. You look like I feel."

"How do you feel?"

"Like I held a stone down against a hand the size of a harbor." He moved one hand on the blanket, slowly, as if it weighed a lot. "Doctors say about a month before I can decide for anything bigger than a door. That's fine. I've decided for enough this week." He looked at the window. "Your sister wrote it in red. She sent me a photograph. It's under my other one. R. Sterling: decided for a stone. It stayed."

"Do you mind?"

"I asked for it." He was quiet. "I'd like you to know I said no first. To the stone, before I did it. Out loud. Same as your twelfth." He turned his head on the pillow. "First time in fifty years I've said no to myself before I used it. I'd like to keep doing that, if I can remember."

"It's on the wall," said Yuanshu. "You'll remember."

"A nurse brought me jelly," said the King of Force. "Green. I haven't been given green jelly since I was nine. I'm not going to complain about anything ever again."

He found Callum Ward's mother on the quay at nine in the morning.

Most of the holders had gone home, but she hadn't. She sat on a bollard at the head of the harbor outside the roofless hall, in her docker's jacket, with a paper cup of coffee she wasn't drinking, looking through the open doors at the black stone. Somebody had put a blanket round her shoulders. She hadn't taken it off and hadn't pulled it round her.

She looked up when he stopped in front of her.

"Sixty-one," she said. "They came out of the hand onto the quay. I saw them." Her voice was flat. "Margaret Okonjo, from the fish market. She was in the line behind me. It took her, and I had my hand on her shoulder, and then I didn't." She looked at the cup. "She came down on the stones in front of me. Woke up. Said, what time is it."

"Yes."

"So you can get them out," said Callum Ward's mother. "Out of the hand. When it's close."

"When it's close."

She looked at him a long time.

"He's not close."

"No."

"He's out there in the other one. The big one. Held up in front of its face, burning." She set the cup down on the quay. "You going to ask him today?"

"Now," said Yuanshu. "If you want to be here."

"Of course I want to be here," she said. "What do you think."

He sat on the next bollard with the staff across his knees, shut his eyes, and went up.

The short way: through the docker's knot in the black stone, along the thread, past the keeper's warmth and the branch with its bud and the cold fire where sixty embers stood facing out, into the deep water. Not near. Close enough to be heard. To the edge of the light.

It was different. He felt it before he got there. The thread had been taut for two weeks, humming, pulled from the far end by something coming hand over hand. It wasn't taut now, and it wasn't slack either. It was still. All eleven threads were still, the way a fishing line goes still when whoever holds it has stopped reeling in and is just sitting on the bank in the dark, holding it, thinking.

The busy dark had stopped moving.

Out in front of it, in one of its thousands of hands, the lantern still burned. But the hand wasn't holding it out ahead any more like a lantern on a dark road. It had drawn it in close, as a man holds a lantern up beside his face to look at something in his palm.

Callum.

...yeah.

It's me. It's tomorrow.

...yeah. I know. I could feel you coming. It's quieter now. It's stopped walking.

The voice was a little clearer, as if the roar had dropped a fraction now that the thing holding him had stopped moving.

...it's holding me up close. Right up close. I think it's looking at me. I don't think it's ever looked at me before. I was just the light. It just looked where I showed it. A long pause. ...it's looking at me now. I don't like it.

Callum, your mum's here, next to me on the quay. She says to tell you she's still holding, and she says: what do you think.

The fire roared. Under it, at the bottom of the well, something that might have been a laugh.

...that's Mum.

Callum, I'm going to tell you something. Yesterday a hand came down the thread to the stone at Port Halvard. The holders held. It took sixty-one of them. We cut the palm open and they came out. They're home. A woman from the fish market called Margaret Okonjo woke up on the quay and asked what time it was.

The voice was quiet a long time.

...you cut it open.

Yes.

...and they came out. Home. Not the door. Home.

Yes.

The fire roared, white and gold and every color, held up close to the face of something enormous in the deep water that had stopped walking in order to look at it.

...then I know, said Callum Ward.

He waited, and didn't look in the bowl.

...I want out. Not the door. Home. To Mum. I want to stop being the lantern. The voice shook. ...I don't care how it feels. I don't care it's the brightest I've ever been. I don't want to be the brightest thing out here. I want to be an F on the docks with a mum who says what do you think.

...that's what I want. You asked.

He opened his eyes on the bollard at the head of the harbor.

Callum Ward's mother was watching him. She hadn't moved or touched her coffee. Her hands lay flat on her knees and her face was very still, still as a face gets when it's been holding something for six weeks.

"Well?"

"He wants to come home," said Yuanshu. "He says: not the door. Home. To you. He says he wants to be an F on the docks with a mum who says what do you think."

For a moment she didn't move. Then she put her hand over her mouth and bent forward on the bollard until her forehead nearly touched her knees, and her shoulders shook, and she made no sound at all.

He sat beside her on the next bollard with the staff across his knees and didn't touch her or say anything. After a while Lingyue came down the quay from the hotel and sat on the stones at their feet with her back to the bollard and her sword across her knees, and didn't say anything either.

The threads are still, said Seed. Kwan's rung. All eleven, since the hand went back up. No pulling. Nothing.

Gao Min says the stones have stopped rising everywhere. Port Halvard went down two millimeters by itself overnight. The holders are going home to sleep.

It's stopped walking. It's out there in the deep water, forty-eight days away, sitting still on eleven threads, holding a man up to its face and looking at him. Like the gardener sitting still after the Sixth.

Thinking.

I don't know if it thinks. I didn't know if the gardener thought. I didn't know if the Collector wanted. I didn't know if a sack could ask.

I've stopped saying what things can't do.

He looked out past the end of the north pier, where two days ago the gold road had come down over the water and the Watch of the Shelf had risen streaming out of the harbor, at the Atlantic going away gray and enormous toward a horizon he couldn't see past. And past that, past the sky and the keeper and the branch and the cold fire and the end of the arm, into the dark between arms where there was no map at all.

"He's forty-eight days away," he said.

"Yes," said Lingyue from the stones, without looking up. "And it's stopped walking. So he's not getting any closer."

"No."

"So if somebody wants him home," said his wife, "somebody's going to have to go and get him."

He didn't answer.

Callum Ward's mother sat up. She wiped her face with the back of her hand, roughly, as dockers wipe their faces, and looked at him.

"You're going," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I'm going to ask first. Everyone. I'll tell them what it is and what it'll cost, and ask them."

"But you're going."

He looked out at the sea.

"Yes," he said.

Lingyue thumped him on the arm. Not hard. As she had since they were eighteen.

"Obviously," she said.
