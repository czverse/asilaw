Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 232Nobody at the Top

czverse11 min read

The question from the Western Pass took eleven hours to come down.

It wasn't an important question. Sergeant Ma Dashan of the Fourth Company wanted to know whether his men could have the spare lamp oil from the depot at the foot of the pass, because the nights up there were long and the men liked to see each other's faces when they said the names. A month ago it would have come down the Lattice in a breath, nine to nine to nine and up to the top and across and down to the Ossuary, and Yuanshu would have said yes, obviously before the sergeant had finished asking.

It reached him at four in the afternoon, three days after the bud, while he was sitting on the vault steps eating a cold bun. It had left the pass at five in the morning.

It went round, said Seed. Up through the Western Legion's knots, the way it always goes, to where the top used to be. Nobody there to carry it across. So it went back down, and sideways, and up a different way, and found nobody, and came down again.

Four hundred times round the Lattice before it found us. Like a letter with the address torn off.

There are nine thousand others still going round.

He put the bun down and looked across the banking hall.

The chair was back in its old place by the long white wall, under the lamp. Somebody had carried it down from the Sector Nine battlement the morning after. A plain wooden chair with a cushion on it that Fat Tan had brought a year ago because an old man kept complaining about his back, although the old man was dead and had no back to complain about. Nobody had moved it since. Nobody had sat in it. Twice in three days Yuanshu had watched Xiaoyu walk toward it with an armful of ledger paper, stop a step short, and put the paper on the floor instead.

"Who's going to carry it?" he said.

I don't know. I think we should ask them.

"It'll take a week to ask them."

Yes. I think that's the first thing they'd want us to notice.

Lingyue could light a candle. That was all.

Bai Lu came every morning and every evening, laid her hands on her and frowned, and on the third day she stopped frowning and started saying weeks. Weeks was better than what she'd said on the first day, which was nothing. A Lord at Level 66 had burned everything she had in one breath on the north wall of Lingzhou, all of it at once, which you were not supposed to do, and what was left was a woman in a narrow bed in the back room of the Ossuary with the window open onto the courtyard and her grandfather's sword leaning where she could see it. Gray under the eyes. She could hold up one finger and make a small pale flame stand on the end of it, the size of a grain of rice.

She did it every hour. She held it until her hand shook, then put it out and looked at the place where it had been.

"Stop counting," she said, without turning her head. "I can hear you counting."

"I'm not."

"You are. Your face does it." She lay back. Her hair was loose on the pillow. There'd been a red ribbon in it on the wall, from the dress from Mount Qingyun, and nobody had found it afterward and nobody was going to. "Sit down. You're blocking the sun and Hush will complain."

On the sill, in the sun, Hush didn't complain. It had all its eyes shut and all its legs out, as it had every morning since the cut, with gold dust still on its legs that it wouldn't let anyone brush off. Fat Tan had tried with a pastry brush. He'd been ignored so thoroughly that he went away and brushed the pastry instead.

Yuanshu sat on the edge of the bed. She held up her finger and lit the grain of rice again and watched it.

"I understand them now," she said.

"Who?"

"The Sixth." The little flame shook. "We would like, for one day, to be very bright. On the island I thought that was the saddest thing anyone had ever said. I thought, they never got to have anything, so they wanted that." She watched it. "It wasn't sad. It was the best thing I've ever done. Everything at once. Nothing held back, nothing careful." Her voice stayed level. "I've been careful my whole life. Grandfather's granddaughter. Dawnbreaker. Don't waste it. Bend it, don't burn it." The flame went out. "For about three minutes I wasn't careful at all, and I've never felt that good."

He didn't trust his voice, so he kept it out of the way.

"That's why it eats them," said Shen Lingyue. "Isn't it. The tree. That's why the brightest go first. Not because they're stupid. Because it feels like that."

Yes, said Seed. I think so. I think that's what the Collector taught. Not that minds are bright. That being bright is the best thing a mind can feel.

She turned her head on the pillow and looked at him properly for the first time that morning.

"Don't make that face. I'm not going to do it again. Not like that." She found his hand on the blanket, the one with the ring, and held it. "I just wanted to tell somebody. That I get it now, and I'm not going to pretend I don't."

"Okay," he said.

"Okay. Now go and fix your net. I can hear it buzzing from here."

They asked the dead on the fourth morning.

It was the first question since the cut that was about the Lattice itself, and it went slowly, round and round, as everything did now. Seed composed it with Xiaoyu at the long table under the lamp, in plain words, and said first what would be done with the answer, as Article Eleven required:

Adjutant Xu carried every question to the top and across. He is gone. Somebody has to be the knot at the top, or the questions go round forever. Who do you want? We will ask whoever you choose whether they want it. If they say no, we will ask you again.

I'd like to add one more line.

"Go on," said Xiaoyu, marker ready.

The top knot is where the tip found us. It drank down the road of light to the top, and the top was one old man, and he tied it off. That's why the rest of you are still here. Whoever's at the top next will be the place that gets found first, next time.

They should know that before they choose.

Xiaoyu looked at the sheet for a while with the cap of the marker in her teeth. Then she wrote it down.

Old Seven said no before the question reached him.

He didn't need it to reach him. He stood at the bronze door with his spear, and when Yuanshu came down the stairs on the fourth morning he turned his skull a little toward the empty chair by the wall, and then back to the street.

He says no. He goes where you go. A top knot sits in a chair. Xu Dehai sat in the chair because he was an adjutant, and adjutants sit at the top and carry, and captains go out ahead of the line. That's what each of them is for, and he won't be the other thing.

Also, he doesn't like chairs.

Yuanshu stood on the bottom step and looked at the tall dead soldier in the doorway with the sun behind him.

"You went to the stone this morning," he said. "Before it was light."

Old Seven didn't answer.

Somebody has to make sure the dumplings aren't eaten by dogs.

"They were eaten by dogs the first night."

Then somebody has to make sure the dogs know who they were for.

The answers began to come back on the sixth day, and they weren't names.

Kwan sorted them. She sat at the long table with a ledger and a shoebox of index cards while Seed read the answers to her one at a time as they came in off the Lattice, and she sorted them into piles, eating sunflower seeds out of a paper twist and dropping the shells into a teacup. By the evening of the seventh day one pile was taller than all the others put together.

It had started at the bottom of the sea.

The Watch of the Shelf, off Kesse. Nana Ampofo asked her eleven hundred and forty-six by name, as she asks them everything, and the first one to answer was Yaw. The one who went over the side of a boat forty-one years ago and answered first the night you asked them.

He said: why does there have to be a top?

Kwan didn't look up from the cards.

Nana sent it up, because she sends everything up, and it went round. And everywhere it went, somebody read it, because it had to pass through them to get anywhere. There was nobody at the top to send it straight.

The Spear Corps read it and passed it on with a word added. Yes. Then Grid Nine. The Western Legion. The First Legion. The cemetery companies. By the time it reached us it had been round six hundred times, and every knot had read it, and every knot had added something.

"Added what?" said Yuanshu.

Kwan picked up the top card and read it out slowly in her careful accented Huaxian, as she read anything she thought somebody would want to keep.

"Why does there have to be a top. Yes. Because then nobody else has to tie it off. Yes. Each asks its nine and the nine that asked it. Yes. It'll be slow. Yes, slow is fine, we're dead. Ask Old Seven. He said no. Good, he's right. No top. No top. No top." She put the card down and spat a shell into the teacup. "It goes on like that for two thousand cards. They didn't choose anybody. They chose nobody."

A net, said Seed. Not a tree.

Every knot tied to the nine below it and the nine beside it and the nine that asked it. No top. A question can go in anywhere and an answer can come out anywhere. Nobody carries everything. Nobody can be found first.

It'll be much slower. Hours where it was a breath, days where it was hours. It'll never be as fast as it was with him.

And nothing can drink it from the top again, because there isn't one.

Nobody said anything. Behind the round steel door of the vault, a box of queens of hearts shifted on a shelf as something settled.

Then Kwan said, without looking up, in an odd voice: "It's not a tree any more."

"No."

"We've been a tree this whole time. Since Concordia. Since the Lattice. Each asks its nine, all the way up to one person." She turned a card over and over in her fingers. "That's the gardener's shape. We were building exactly the shape it was. And the first thing the dead did when the top was gone was decide not to be that shape."

They kept the chair.

Fat Tan proposed it, if you could call it proposing. He came in on the eighth morning with a tin of furniture polish and a cloth, knelt on the marble and polished the chair, every rung, slowly, without a word. When he'd finished he plumped the cushion, put it back, and went out to see about lunch.

Xiaoyu wrote on the wall above it, small: THE TOP. NOBODY. BY VOTE OF THE DEAD.

Nobody sat in it and nobody moved it. When a question came down nine hours later from the Spear Corps, wanting to know whether the chair was being looked after, Yuanshu sent back Fat Tan polished it. The reply took a day and a half to come round, and it was one word.

Good.

The letter came on the ninth day.

He didn't see it arrive. He came up out of the vault at noon, from sitting on the cold floor with the staff asking the Lattice slow small questions to feel how the new shape held, and found his sister at the long table under the lamp with an envelope in front of her. Heavy cream paper, a red seal, broken. The Awakening Bureau's mark in the corner: a black stele in a circle, the shape every child on Blue Star could draw before it could draw the sun.

She'd already read it. She was sitting with her hands flat on the table on either side of it, looking at it as if it might move.

He sat down across from her.

"Thirty-four days," said Xiaoyu.

He knew. He'd known for a year. He'd been not thinking about it the way you avoid a loose stair in the dark, by knowing exactly where it is.

"The Awakening Hall. Lingzhou. Ten in the morning. Same as you." She turned the envelope a little with one finger. "They put a note in. Handwritten, from the acting director. Given the circumstances of the applicant's family history, the Bureau would understand if the applicant preferred a private awakening." She looked up. "Circumstances of the applicant's family history. That's you. You're the circumstances."

"You don't have to do it in public."

"I know I don't." She looked back down. "Everybody'll watch anyway. They watched you. You broke all eleven, it's in the textbooks. My whole class is going to be there. Dabao says the drones are already booked." A pause. "I keep thinking about it at night. Walking up to it. Putting my hand on it."

"It's not going to go black."

"I know." Her voice went small. "That's what I keep thinking about."

He looked at her. Seventeen. Square black capitals on every wall of this building. A queen of hearts on a pin. Fifty-one minutes on a microphone, until her voice gave out, calling the city down into the cellars. Somebody has to write their names when they come home.

"What if it doesn't do anything?" said his sister. "What if I put my hand on it in front of everyone, after you, and it just goes Scribe, Talent D. And they all go oh. And that's it. That's what I am, forever, because a rock said so."

He opened his mouth to say it won't, and shut it, because he didn't know that, and he'd decided a long time ago never to tell her anything he didn't know.

Seed spoke. Not to her; she couldn't hear it. To him, quietly, in the place a little above his eyes.

Yuanshu. I'd like to look at the Stele again.

I haven't looked at it since the first morning. Two point six seconds. I saw the Law of Death inside it and understood nothing else, because I didn't know anything else.

I know more now. There was something about it I couldn't see then. I didn't have the words. I didn't know what thread was. I didn't know what I was made of.

I think I might now. I'd like to look before she touches it.
