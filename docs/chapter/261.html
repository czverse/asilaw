Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 261Loud

czverse9 min read

His mother knew before they were through the bronze door.

She was at the long table at nine in the morning with a cup of tea and Gao Min's newest ledger, the one with the column headed What they said, reading it as she read the names on the wall each morning, one at a time, with her finger. She looked up when they came in: at him, and at Lingyue a step behind him with the sword on her back and her hand, without her noticing, flat on her stomach under the gray sweater.

Zhou Wanqing put down her cup.

"How long?"

Lingyue stopped.

"How did you—"

"I had two," said his mother. "I know what a woman's hand does." She looked at her son. "And you've got the face. The one you had at nine when you'd broken the rice cooker and didn't know how to tell me." She folded her thin hands on the ledger. "How long?"

"Ten weeks," said Lingyue. "About."

Zhou Wanqing sat a moment without saying anything. Then she held out both hands. Not to her son. To his wife.

Lingyue went round the table. She didn't kneel; she wasn't that kind of girl. She stood by the wheelchair and let the small woman in it take both her hands, hard, and hold them.

"Nobody asked me either," said his mother. "When I was born. My mother decided. On a Thursday, she said. I asked her once." She didn't let go. "I'm going to ask this one what it wants every day it's alive. So are you. So is he." She looked up into Lingyue's face. "And when it's old enough to say leave me alone, Grandmother, we'll ask it whether it wants us to stop."

"Yes," said Lingyue.

"Good. Now sit down before you fall down. You flew across an ocean pregnant. I heard."

Xiaoyu came down the stairs at ten past nine, heard, and screamed.

Not a word. A sound: the noise a girl of seventeen makes on learning she's going to be an aunt, which turned out to be much the same noise as Hu Dabao shouting SCRIBE at the ceiling. The pigeons in the upper windows went up all at once. In the back room Fat Tan dropped a teapot.

Then she stopped on the bottom step with her hand over her mouth and went very serious.

"I have to write it. I have to write it on the wall."

"Not yet," said Lingyue.

"Why not?"

"Because it's not anyone yet," said Shen Lingyue. "It can't say whether it wants to be on a wall. Your brother's article." She sat down on the stairs. "You can write it when it can say yes. Or no. Or ask me tomorrow."

Xiaoyu looked at her a long moment, marker in hand. Then she capped it and put it in her pocket and sat on the step beside her sister-in-law and leaned her head on her shoulder.

"That's going to be years," she said.

"I know."

"I'm going to write it in my head every day till then."

"That's allowed," said Lingyue.

From the back room Fat Tan called that the teapot had survived, and so had he, barely, and would someone please tell him things one at a time in future.

They told the world on the third day.

Not from the Awakening Hall this time but from the floor of Concordia on its volcanic island, in front of the five chairs, because Adaeze Okafor said this was Concordia's or it was nobody's. He told them what Ichiro had told him on a breakwater in Yamato: the quiet ones in the deep water; the Lamplit, with their stones and their ladder of marks and their ships that folded, who'd fought the one who picks ten thousand years and had seen a Lord-bright flare in the Shallows and could count; the Singers, who'd sung their brightest up a thread every age to be spared, and had sung this age, and been answered by nothing, and were afraid.

Both coming to look.

He didn't say what they should do. He said what Article Eleven told him to say: that he didn't know yet.

Then he sat down, and the five Kings argued for six hours.

"We hide," said Roman Sterling by radio, from a desk by a window in Port Halvard where he was allowed to sit four hours a day and decide for nothing bigger than a door. "We've done it before. Lanterns out. The whole world. We're good at it."

"We hid from rings," said Su Mingli from her chair, arms folded. "For a night. This is a civilization with ships."

"Then we hide longer."

"They saw him burn," said Marshal Orlov, one sentence through a great deal of static. "You can't hide what's already been seen."

Shirasagi Ren said nothing for the first five hours. In the sixth she said calmly: "The quiet ones in the deep water have hidden a thousand years. A thousand thousand, Ichiro says. They're very good at it. They're alive." A pause. "They're not anything else. They don't talk. They don't burn. They don't ask. They drift together in the dark, being quiet." She looked at the boy in the chair at the end of the row. "That's what hiding is, when you do it long enough. I sat with three thousand hollows on a mountain for a year. I know what it is to be quiet so long you forget what you were being quiet for."

Nobody answered her.

"Ngozi," said Adaeze Okafor.

The woman in the Southern Compact's chair, who sat there because there was no Tide King left to sit in it, hadn't said a word. She sat with her hands flat on the table, a tall, serious, careful administrator from the capital who'd been Ifeoma's deputy minister of the coast for nine years.

"Ifeoma would have said we don't hide," said Ngozi Eze. "She'd have said it before the question was finished." She looked down the row. "I'm not Ifeoma. I'd like it written down that I thought about it for six hours." A pause. "We don't hide."

It went to the world under Article Eleven. The old way it would have been forty days; there weren't forty days, Ichiro had said months, maybe less, so it was ten. Down the net to the dead, slow, nine by nine. Over the radio and on paper ballots to the living, in schools and temples and harbor offices. To the keeper, the cousins, the eleven in the stones, and Shiomi in the fog of a bay in Yamato, because a world that asked everyone asked everyone.

At the top, in plain words, as Seed had learned to write them: Two kinds of people from very far away are coming to look at Blue Star. We do not know what they want. We will either hide, the whole world, every night, for as long as it takes, the way we hid from the rings. Or we will light the lamps and let them see us. We are telling you first.

Kwan read the answers aloud in the banking hall in the evenings, as always. Fifty-nine percent said light the lamps. Thirty-four said hide. The rest said other things.

We hid in the Black Tide. In cellars. For a week. I was six. I don't want my children to do that.

If they're like us, they'll want to be asked. If they're not, hiding won't help.

Ask Shiomi. They know about hiding.

Shiomi had answered. Ichiro rang it in the fog-code through Ren, and Sister Kiyo translated, drily, from the end of the breakwater with the lamp in her lap:

"Shiomi says: we hid for nine hundred years. It worked. We are alive.

Shiomi says: we came home because someone lit a lamp.

Shiomi says: light the lamps."

I've run it, said Seed.

He was on the vault steps on the eighth night. Seed had waited until nobody was looking at him.

Hiding, not hiding, every way I can think of. I don't know what the Lamplit will do. I've never met them. I've got a description from a man who was quiet in the deep water nine hundred years and talked to the fish. That isn't much data.

But I know one thing. The Collector kept the wants of a great many of them. It told Xiaoyu last night. She asked what it wanted, and it said it didn't know yet, and then, because it tells first now, it told her it's kept more wants from the Lamplit than from anyone else in the Shallows. More than the Architects. More than the cousins.

What did they want?

It said: to be first. Almost all of them. The same want, every one. To be first. To be at the top of the ladder. To be brighter than the one beside them.

It said it was the easiest want it ever kept. It didn't even have to ask. They told it before it asked.

He sat with that a long time.

That's what the stones did to them. Ten thousand years of being marked.

Yes, I think so. Gao Min says the same. She says if you mark people for ten thousand years, they stop wanting anything except the mark.

She says it's the saddest thing she's ever heard. She'll put it in a paper. She says nobody will read it.

On the tenth night the answer came back from the net of the dead.

Not a percentage; it never was. A pile of index cards on the long table, and on top, the one that had gone round six hundred times. Kwan read it.

"Stone No. 7. The Spear Corps. They said: we were lanterns in the Black Tide. On the wall. The whole eastern line. We lit the braziers so the city could see where we were, so the beasts would come to us first." She turned the card. "Light the lamps."

"And the rest?"

"Yes. Yes. Light the lamps. Grid Nine would like to see the ships. The First Legion says a wall's a wall, lit or not. The Third Battalion—" She stopped. "Sergeant Huo says: we're at ease. But if someone's coming up the slope, wake us."

Concordia sat on the eleventh morning and voted four to one. The one was Sterling, who said by radio he'd like it noted that he'd said no first, and that he'd light the lamp in his own window anyway.

That night at two in the morning Kwan came down the stairs with the laptop held open in front of her like a tray of something hot, and he knew.

"It's early," she said.

He'd been on the vault steps, not sleeping. Lingyue was asleep upstairs, and his mother, and Xiaoyu, who'd gone to bed at one after asking the Collector what it wanted and writing on the back of Gao Min's hand: NIGHT 75. NOT YET. TOLD ME ABOUT THE LAMPLIT. THEY WANT TO BE FIRST.

Kwan turned the laptop round.

The keeper's eye. Seed's map of the Shallows: the arm, the twelve gold dots of the fields, the gray husks of the pruned ones, the faint dark place of the branch with its small gold point. And near the keeper's own small white ring, low over the end of the world, close to Blue Star, closer than anything since the three gray rings: a mark.

Not gold. Not gray. Not red. A crinkle, like the surface of a pond when something's been dropped into it from very high, and the rings go out, and in the middle, for an instant, the water folds.

That isn't a ring, said Seed. It isn't a hand. It isn't anything I've got a name for. Something folded. Something came through the dark between there and here without going through it.

"Months," said Yuanshu. "Ichiro said months. Maybe less."

"It's been eleven days," said Kwan.

It's small. The size of a building. It isn't moving. It's very bright inside, bright as the stones were bright. Bright as Port Halvard was bright for five minutes.

And Yuanshu—

He waited.

It's full of people. Not the way a ship's full of people, walking about, talking. Held. In the walls. In the engines. Packed. Pressed. Burning. Like the miners in the cores at Heishui. Like Mrs. Pan's husband in Number Three shaft for two years, screaming, before we let him go.

It's a ship made of kept people.

And it's looking at us.
