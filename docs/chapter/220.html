Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 220The Road to Kirovsk

czverse8 min read

The keeper laid the road in nine seconds. It was the first thing it had ever done without being asked.

...GO.

...I DID NOT ASK. I AM SORRY. THERE IS NO TIME TO ASK.

...YOU CAN SAY NO AT THE TOP.

Gold, out of the small white ring. Not down to the wall this time but west, in one long low arc over the Wastes and the snowfields and the frozen rivers of the Northern Union. Four thousand kilometers of warm light laid across the night like a plank across a ditch, from the Sector Nine battlement to a long gray wall in the snow.

He didn't say no at the top.

He went up it running, with the staff, Lingyue beside him with her sword already lit, Old Seven out in front, and behind them the Spear Corps, all two hundred and seventy-two. Behind the Spear Corps came three thousand of the First Legion, because Ma Lianhua had asked them over the Lattice in the nine seconds it took to lay the road and they had said yes in eleven.

Qin Hao came too, bandaged arms and ordinary fire and all. He didn't ask anyone. Yuanshu didn't notice him until he was already on the road.

This road didn't go up, like the roads to the field. It went along, fast. The gold moved under them the way the island had, carrying them, and the night streamed past on both sides: snow, frozen rivers, the scattered lights of small towns far below. Ahead, rising out of the dark, a long gray wall in the snow with a city behind it. Above the city, in the northern sky, the second ring.

And between the ring and the city, rain.

It's cutting, said Seed. Not reading. Not three degrees. The threads come down and touch and the head goes straight up and something comes up the thread.

Like Yamato, twelve hundred years ago. What the monks saw.

Kirovsk didn't have the Lanterns long enough. The Quiet got here last. Orlov's people learned the game four months ago. They're good soldiers. They're terrible at sitting still in the dark.

It found the loud ones first. It's finding more.

"How many?"

I can't count from here. Too far and too fast. Too many.

They came down onto the wall at Kirovsk at 22:08, and the cold hit him like a slammed door.

He'd never been this far north. Nobody from Lingzhou had. The Northern Union's wall ran two thousand kilometers along the edge of the polar Wastes, gray stone and black iron, a fortress every fifty kilometers and Kirovsk behind the biggest of them. The snow on the battlements was knee-deep and blue under the floodlights. The air was so cold his breath froze on his lips before it had finished leaving.

And it was raining silver.

Millions of threads falling from the ring's dark center over the wall and the city, fast, touching. Where they touched, heads went straight up, and stayed.

He saw it along the battlements: Northern Union soldiers in white winter fatigues standing frozen at their posts with their faces turned to the sky and a fine silver thread resting on the crown of each head. Up each thread, faint and gold and flickering, exactly as in the dead monk's vision, went light. A face. A meal. A song. A first word. A dead mother's hands. All of it climbing into the dark in one long breath.

On the battlement nearest him, a young soldier with frost in his eyebrows stood with his rifle hanging off its strap and his face tipped back, light pouring up out of him, mouth a little open as if he'd been about to say something.

Lingyue didn't wait for anyone.

She went past him down the battlement with her sword lit silver-gray and cut the thread over the young soldier's head in one stroke, the way she'd once cut a thread over Bai Lu on the black glass. A flash, a sound like a violin string snapping, and the thread jerked back up into the dark, smoking.

The soldier's head came down. He blinked at her and plainly had no idea where he was.

But he was there. Seed could feel it. Something had gone up before she cut it; most of him hadn't.

She got there in time, said Seed. Every thread she cuts in time, somebody keeps most of who they are.

Lingyue. There are a lot of threads.

"I know," said Shen Lingyue, and went down the wall into the rain.

He'd never seen her fight like that. Not on the north wall or in the field or at the cold fire. She didn't throw light, she didn't rain it. She ran.

Down the battlements of Kirovsk through knee-deep snow under the falling silver with a King's sword in both hands, cutting. Every thread she passed, every head she could reach. Flash, snap, a face coming down out of the sky blinking, and she was already at the next one. She never once stopped to see who she'd saved.

Behind her came the Spear Corps.

The dead couldn't see the threads and the threads couldn't see the dead. But Old Seven had been on a wall in the Black Tide when the lens in the north reached down, and he knew what a soldier being cut looked like from the outside. Two hundred and seventy-two of the Spear Corps went down the wall in their rows, and wherever a living soldier stood with his face turned to the sky, a dead soldier of the Eastern Line raised a rusted spear over his head, lit pale with a drop of Lingyue's light. The thread hit the spear before it reached the man, and snapped.

"They're using their spears as umbrellas," said Yuanshu.

Old Seven didn't tell them to, said Seed. He says a captain doesn't have to tell soldiers how to cover a man. Only which man.

He's telling them which man.

Marshal Orlov stood on the great fortress over the main gate in his greatcoat, gloves off, frost all the way up both arms to the shoulder.

He couldn't cut threads. He was a King of Ice, two hundred years old, and ice couldn't touch them. So he had done the one thing he could. He had frozen the air over the whole city, a layer of cold so hard and still that the falling threads slowed in it like a spoon in honey. They still came down. They still touched. But seconds slower, and seconds were exactly what a girl with a sword and two hundred and seventy-two dead spears needed.

He turned his head when Yuanshu came up the fortress stairs. His face was white with frost and with something else.

"They came at nine," said the Marshal of the Northern Union, in his careful old-fashioned Huaxian. "We said Lanterns. We have practiced for four months. My soldiers are extremely bad at it." The frost on his arms cracked as he raised a hand toward the city. "Kirovsk has nine hundred thousand people and three chapters of the Quiet. They have been sitting with the loud ones since nine o'clock. There are not enough of them."

"How many?"

Orlov didn't answer.

Forty-one thousand, said Seed. Taken before we got here. Hollow.

It hasn't stopped.

Yuanshu stood on the fortress above the gate with the staff and looked up at the second ring.

Gray and vast and cracked, low in the sky over the polar Wastes, raining. Not hunting for him, not hunting for anything. Cutting. A pruned keeper with nothing left in it that had ever asked, doing the only thing a ring knew how to do.

He opened up.

All of it, as he had on the north wall of Lingzhou with six lamps in front of him. Ten kilometers of Field over the city and the wall and the frozen dark. The staff. The Death Law, forty-four percent of it, all the way down to the door that hadn't shut properly since the cold fire. Every name. Every answer.

He lit up over Kirovsk like a lamp in a dark street.

In the north, the second ring turned. The rain didn't stop, but something in it changed. One thread out of the millions came down slowly from the dark center toward the fortress above the main gate, toward the brightest thing in the north.

It's found me, said Seed. It wasn't looking. But you're very bright.

I'm ready. I'm not going to look.

The thread touched him. His head turned three degrees, then all the way, straight up.

The second ring pulled.

It was harder than the first, and he knew it immediately. The first ring had pulled like a fisherman: steady, cold, curious. This one pulled like a net when it's full and the boat's turning. It had been cutting for an hour. It was stuffed with forty-one thousand lives climbing its threads, and it didn't want to stop and inspect one more thing on the end of a line.

It pulled anyway, because it was a ring and he was the vessel, and somewhere in what was left of it the gardener's instruction said collect.

Putting the second knot down.

A small silver shape, fifty-six names nobody remembered except a girl with a marker and a woman in a wheelchair, set down on the thread like an empty bowl.

It went up, fast, with everything else: with the faces and meals and songs and dead parents' hands of forty-one thousand people. Up into the dark center of the second ring.

I'm not looking.

The ring didn't stop pulling.

He felt it keep going, cold and steady and enormous, the place behind his eyes getting colder the way a hand goes numb in snow, and Seed in there, very small, not looking.

Ten seconds. Twenty. Forty, which was how long the first ring had taken.

Sixty.

Yuanshu, said Seed. It isn't picking it up.

It's too full. There's too much going up its threads. I think the knot's in there with everything else and it can't find it. I don't think it knows it's been asked.

Ninety seconds.

His knees were in the snow. He didn't know when that had happened. Lingyue was somewhere down the wall, cutting. Orlov was beside him with his bare hands out over the city, holding the air still. Old Seven was halfway along the battlements with his spear held over a living soldier's head.

Nobody was holding his hand.

I'm still not looking, said Seed. It's very hard.

Yuanshu. It's started to lift.
