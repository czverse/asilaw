Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 287Corraine

czverse11 min read

Tam asked on the twenty-sixth day, and he asked the rowers first.

Not the First of the Ladder, not Halvarde, not the boy from Blue Star, though he told him afterward he'd wanted to. He went down to the first array on the morning of the twenty-sixth day, barefoot, with Seri holding his hand, and stood in the middle of that vast curved room like the inside of a bell, among the cells still burning low with people in them rowing, and asked them.

In the Federation's language, without the grade, through Halvarde, who couldn't hear them but knew all their names, and through himself, who could.

"We fold past Corraine tomorrow," he said. "It's on the way. Halvarde says it's half a day if we stop. I'm from Corraine. Seri's from Corraine. Some of you are from Corraine." His voice was very steady. "I'd like to stop. Half a day. I'm asking you. You're the ones rowing."

And the rowers answered, each to its nine, round the arrays cell to cell, slowly, as the net answered things. At noon Halvarde came back up to the observation deck with a slate in her hand and her face doing something Seed said it had never seen a C's face do.

"Twenty-nine thousand and four say yes," she said. "Eleven hundred say ask me tomorrow. Six say no." She looked at the slate. "Four hundred and twelve are from Corraine. All four hundred and twelve say: stop."

Corraine was gray.

He saw it from the observation deck as the flagship came out of the fold, the twelve small lights of the Rowed Fleet emerging round it one after another. A world of gray seas and gray skies and long low gray islands, like a world drawn in pencil and never colored in. One small cold sun. On the largest island, at the mouth of a gray harbor, a town: low houses, boats drawn up on shingle, a long stone quay. And above it on a headland, a black stone.

"That's it," said Tam. He stood at the window with his hands flat on the glass. "The stone. On the headland. You can see it from every house in the town. They put it there so you'd see it every day."

"Why?"

"So you'd remember," said Seri beside him, twelve in the body and thirty-three inside, chin up. "That you'd be read, on the morning you were twelve. That it was coming." She looked at the black stone on the gray headland. "Fuel orchard. Every family on Corraine gives. It's the law. You grow up looking at it."

Two hundred and eleven thousand people on Corraine, said Seed. The stone reads the children every year, four thousand a year. Most are marked F or E. Corraine was tuned for it. The Ladder planted it that way, Iselde says: a world where most children are dim, so there's always fuel.

The reading is in nine days.

The First of the Ladder went down first.

Not in a ship. He walked: out of the flagship's bay onto nothing, down his invisible staircase at the same even pace, unhurried, in white without gold at the collar and the cracked face in his pocket. Down through the gray sky of Corraine to the long stone quay at the harbor mouth, where two hundred and eleven thousand people had come out of their low gray houses to stand on the shingle and stare up at a cathedral that didn't burn the way the Great Houses' ships burned, ringed by twelve small lights the color of the Low Fire.

He stood on the quay.

And the people of Corraine went down on their knees. All of them, on the shingle and the quay and the roofs, as people go down when the First of the Ladder walks onto their world. Not Precedence; his face was cracked, and Precedence was broken, Seed said, for him and for everyone, because the Ladder had no top any more. Just habit. Ten thousand years of it.

Dacian Severin stood on the quay and looked at them kneeling.

"Get up," he said.

Nobody did.

He stood there. Then he said it again in the Federation's language, which puts a grade in every sentence about a person, and this time took the grade out. Seed heard him do it, badly, like a man saying a word in a language learned from a book.

"Please get up. I am not here to read anyone. I am here because two children from this town asked to come home."

Tam's mother was on the quay.

Yuanshu knew her before Seed did, before Tam said anything. He came down behind Tam and Seri, with Old Seven one-armed at his back and the sixty ash shapes of the Third Battalion behind that and Lingyue with the baby and the sword, onto the long stone quay of a town on a gray world, and saw a woman at the front of the crowd who wasn't kneeling. She stood with her hands at her sides, staring at a boy of twelve walking toward her.

Forty-something. Gray in her hair. A fisherwoman's hands. Tam's face, older.

Tam stopped ten meters off, Seri's hand in his.

His mother didn't move.

"Tam," she said, in the speech of Corraine, which was the Federation's language with the corners worn off, as the speech of a harbor wears down. Seed translated. "You're— you're—" She stopped. "You're the same."

"The pressing stops you," said Tam, very steadily. "I was twelve when I went in. I'm twelve. I'm thirty-one inside."

His mother put her hand over her mouth.

"They paid us," she said through her fingers. "The House. When they took you. It was the honor, everyone said. The whole street came. They paid us three years of catch." Her voice cracked. "We bought a boat."

Tam said nothing.

"Your father named it after you. It's in the harbor. He died four winters ago. I still take it out." She took her hand from her mouth. "Tam. Tam. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I took the money. I stood in the box and watched them read you and I let them take you and I took the money."

He watched Tam stand on the quay.

He didn't go to him, and nor did anyone. Lingyue started forward and stopped herself. Seri didn't let go of his hand. Old Seven stood at the back, one-armed, facing out. On the quay and the shingle two hundred and eleven thousand people of Corraine, most still kneeling, watched a boy of twelve who was thirty-one look at his mother.

"Mum," said Tam.

She didn't move.

"I'm going to ask you something," said the boy, in the speech of Corraine, without the grade. "I learned it on the world that asks. They tell you first what they'll do with your answer. So I'm telling you first." He swallowed. "Whatever you say, I'm going to go and sit in Dad's boat for a while. Then I'm going to come back and have tea. Then I'm going home. To Blue Star. Where I'm not anything until I say what I want to be."

His mother stared at him.

"What do you want?" said Tam.

It took her a long time. And when she answered, it wasn't to him. It was to the black stone on the headland above the town, the one every house could see, which she'd looked at every day of her life and every day of his.

"To have said no," said Tam's mother.

Tam let go of Seri's hand.

He walked the last ten meters down the quay, barefoot on the old stone, and stopped in front of his mother. He was a head shorter than her. He'd been a head shorter than her for nineteen years.

"Then say it now," he said.

His mother looked down at him.

"It's too late."

"It isn't," said Tam. "There are four thousand children on Corraine being read in nine days. The stone's going to read them and mark them F and E and send the mark up, and the Houses will come for them." He looked up at the black stone on the headland. "Unless someone's in it."

They went up to the headland at dusk.

The whole town. Not because anyone asked them, but because Tam's mother went, and then Seri, and then the First of the Ladder, walking up the long path from the harbor with the people of Corraine standing up off their knees on the shingle one at a time as he passed. Not because he told them to; because, a woman said afterward, he didn't look like someone you knelt to any more. He looked like someone walking up a hill.

At the top, in the cold gray wind off the sea, stood the stone. Black, warm, ten meters high, driven point-down into the gray turf. Runes dull. Nobody in it.

And beside it, on the seaward slope of the headland, a field of small gray stones.

The dead of Corraine, said Seed. Fishermen, mostly. Lost at sea, mostly, with no bodies. The stones say so: a name, a boat's name, a year. And some who died in their beds.

Yuanshu. Tam's grandmother is here.

He knelt on the gray turf of the headland with the staff across his knees, before a small gray stone with a name he couldn't read and Seed could, and asked.

As he'd asked on the first night: gently, through the Death Law and the staff, as quietly as he could. Not to raise. To ask.

My name's Zhou Yuanshu. I'm from a world called Blue Star. Your grandson's standing behind me. Tam. He's twelve. He's thirty-one. He came home.

The answer came slowly. The dead of Corraine had never been asked anything; the stones never read the dead. But it came.

She says: Tam? The little one? The one who cried when the boats went out?

She says they took him. She stood on the quay and told them no. She was the only one. They took him anyway, and her daughter took the money, and she didn't speak to her for a year.

She died the next winter. She didn't speak to her for a year, and then she died.

Behind him on the turf, Tam's mother made a sound.

He's here, said Yuanshu. He's home. Your daughter's here too. She's just said she wishes she'd said no.

The dead woman under the small gray stone was quiet a long time.

She says: tell her I know. I knew the whole time. That's why I didn't speak to her. Because I knew she wished it and she didn't say it, and I couldn't stand it.

She says: tell her she's said it now. Tell her that's enough for one night.

He told her about the stone. All of it: Wen Jia, the knot, the question, four thousand children in nine days. What it would mean to go into the black stone on the headland that she'd stood under on the quay and said no to, and sit in it, and ask every child who touched it what they wanted to become. And never come out, unless she wanted the door.

He told her first. He didn't ask her. At the end he said, you can say no, and nobody will ask you again.

The dead woman under the gray stone didn't answer at once. The wind came in off the sea.

She says she said no once, on the quay, when they took him. She was the only one, and nobody listened.

If she's in the stone, they'll have to listen. Every child, on the morning, before anything else.

She says: yes. Put me in it. I've got a lot of no's saved up. I'd like to spend them.

At nine that evening Old Seven put his hand on the stone at Corraine, the right one, with all five fingers, while Yuanshu wore him like a coat and the gray wind blew in off the sea. It wasn't one of the forty. The stone didn't read him or the boy inside him. It felt a dead captain's hand flat against it and nothing it was made to see, and went on humming like a machine left running in an empty building.

And a dead grandmother of Corraine, who'd stood on a quay nineteen years ago and said no to the House that took her grandson, went down the dead captain's arm into the black stone on the headland, through the tree of Laws inside it, to the small tight old knot at the bottom where it had been told, ten thousand years ago, what it was for.

She sat down in it.

And every rune on the black stone of Corraine turned at once, like a crowd in a street, and pointed down.

Death Law: 56.3 to 56.9 percent, said Seed. The first stone in the Federation with someone in it.

Tam's mother stood on the headland in the gray wind with her hand over her mouth. Beside her stood her son, twelve in the body and thirty-one inside, who'd been taken from this hill. Before them, the black stone with its runes pointing down.

She didn't touch it. Tam did.

He laid his hand flat against it as he had on the morning he chose, and the letters formed in the gray air above the headland, in the speech of Corraine, in a hand that wasn't the stone's, where every one of the two hundred and eleven thousand people on the slope below could see them.

[What do you want to become?]

Tam didn't answer it.

He looked at the letters a long time. Then he took his hand away, stepped back, and turned to his mother.

"It's your turn," he said. "You've never been asked either."

His mother looked at the stone. At her son. At the letters in the gray air over the headland, in the speech of the harbor she'd been born in, asking.

She put out her hand.

[What do you want to become?]

"Someone who says no," said Tam's mother. "When they come for the children."

The stone was quiet a moment.

[Chosen.]

And under it, smaller, in the same hand, which Seed said was an old woman's, very dry, with a great many no's saved up:

[Finally.]
