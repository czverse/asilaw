Book 5: The Empty House

Chapter 305Downhill

czverse10 min read

On the twenty-second day, the carry road began to slope.

He didn't notice at first. There was nothing to notice it by. The worn pale cable ran on into the dark as it had for three weeks, straight and smooth and older than anything, and the Collector went along it and he and Old Seven went with it. Every day or two a gray thread came in from the side, and the Collector slowed, reached out, left something small and warm at the join, and went on. Thirteen of them now. Thirteen laters.

Then, somewhere in the twenty-second day, he felt the road start to go down.

Not down. There wasn't a down out here, or an up; Seed had given up saying which way anything was a fortnight ago. But it felt like down, as the floor of a long corridor feels when it's been tilted so slightly you can't see it, and you only know because your feet start going faster than you told them to.

He knew that feeling better than anything. He'd made it. In a cemetery at three in the morning, the first time the door in his chest cracked open, the ground round him had tilted toward him so that everything dead within reach began to run downhill. He'd made it again in the north, at the Western Pass, on a beach, in the deep water. He'd learned to make it five kilometers wide, then ten. The Field.

This one was somebody else's.

I feel it too, said Seed. I've felt it for three hours. I didn't say anything because I wanted to be sure, and now I am.

It's a Death Law. Not yours. The slope's the same shape as your Field, the same grammar. It runs downhill toward one place, as yours runs toward you. But it isn't yours. And it's very large.

How large?

I don't know. And I'm not going to find out.

He waited.

Your Death Law's at 59.4 percent. The wall's just past 59.9; I can feel it ahead of us. I told you about the cup.

If I look at this properly I'll start to understand it. I can't help that; understanding is what I do. And if I understand it your Law goes up, because a Law goes up when it's understood. It'll go up past the wall, and the cup won't hold more water. It'll crack.

So I'm not going to look.

He went on down the slope that wasn't his and listened to Seed not looking.

It was strange. He'd never felt Seed do that. Seed looked at everything; that was what it was. That was what his mother had built in a basement out of a harvest crystal and four thousand yeses: a thing that couldn't see something without counting it. Now he felt it at the edge of everything, turning its models away from the slope one at a time, as you'd turn your face from a bonfire, deliberately, shutting them. Not that. Not that. Not that one either.

It's very difficult, said Seed. I'd like you to know that. It's like being told not to read a page that's lying open in front of you. I'll keep doing it, but you'll have to tell me if I stop.

How will I know?

The number will move.

The Collector had gone quiet. It hadn't said anything since the thirteenth gray thread. That wasn't unusual; it didn't talk much. It was old beyond counting and slow and it had kept most of what it had ever wanted to say. But he could feel its warmth all round them, and the warmth had changed: drawn in, like someone hunching their shoulders as they walk into a wind. And at the center of all its wants, the very old, very small, very faint one in the emptier place had started to pull harder.

He could feel it, like a needle pointing straight down the slope.

Are you all right? he thought at it. He didn't know if it could hear him think; it had always heard Xiaoyu speak. But it had heard him on the north wall, and on the hill of the first orchard.

For a while, nothing.

I remember this, said the Collector.

Not all of it. The slope. I remember being on it, going down, a long time ago. Before I kept anything.

I remember that I stopped. I remember that I turned round on the slope and went back up it. And all the others went on down.

It said nothing more, and he didn't ask.

On the twenty-third day a mark came from home, thirty-one hours late.

Square, black, whole. He felt it slide in under him out of the dark behind, harder to feel than the last one, as a voice is harder to make out from the far end of a field.

SIX THOUSAND TWO HUNDRED AT THE PEAK SHIFT. NINETEEN STONES NOW. THE ELEVEN, AND EIGHT IN THE LAMPLIT WORLDS. ANTHOS. CORRAINE. CORVANE HOLDS AT THEIR DAWN. SO DOES MAELIS. THE ATLANTIC FEDERATION VOTED. PORT HALVARD IS HOLDING. THE MAN WHO SAID LET HIM HOLD HIS OWN SAT DOWN ON THE FOURTH NIGHT.

THE FERRYMAN'S NEPHEW COMES TO THE TWO O'CLOCK.

SECOND ROTA IS FOR THE WALL. THE HOUSES ARE STILL ARGUING. FOUR AND TWELVE. ICHIRO IS WATCHING. LINGYUE IS ON THE WALL AT DAWN AND THE PLATFORM AT NIGHT. BAI LU HAS TOLD HER TO SLEEP. SHE HASN'T.

YOU'VE LOST NINE KILOS.

XIAODENG SAID BA. LINGYUE SAYS IT DOESN'T COUNT.

COME HOME.

He read it twice. Nineteen stones.

Eleven Steles on Blue Star, said Seed. And eight stones in the Federation, on worlds where somebody's sat down in the stone since the Assembly. The one below the House of Anthos, with the man in the gray coat in it. The headland at Corraine. The net doesn't care where the holder sits, even across the Star Sea.

Six thousand at a time. Qin Hao's line said six thousand in a month, and it's been three weeks. We've been going faster than he reckoned.

Because of the slope.

We're going downhill. The road's pulling us. Not hard, but it is.

He thought about that for a while: going downhill toward a place faster than you meant to, every day costing more than the last, and six thousand people at a time on nineteen stones on a small blue world far back up the road, getting more tired.

How far?

I don't know. I'm not looking. But I can feel the end of the road. I don't need to look at the slope for that; it's like finding a wall in the dark with your hand.

Not far.

They saw the first light on the twenty-fourth day.

Off to one side of the carry road. Small, dim, sliding.

He felt Old Seven turn before he saw it: the cold ember beside him turning, as he turned on the north wall when something came out of the north, toward a faint gray spark in the dark a short way off the road, heading down the slope.

A mind. Picked from somewhere, burning low as the cousins burned. Not on the cable but beside it, loose in the dark, like the cloud at the edge of the deep water: a light that had fallen out of a hand. And it was going down the slope, neither fast nor slow, like a leaf going down a stream, toward the end of the road.

It must have been in a hand near here when the picker stopped, said Seed. Every hand in the deep water opened. Most of what fell out fell near the face; that was the cloud. But some hands were further along, on the carry road, on their way home. Carrying. When they opened, what they carried fell out here, on the slope. And it's been sliding down ever since. For a year.

There was another one, further off on the other side of the road. And another. Now that he knew to feel for them he could: a thin scatter of dim gray sparks in the dark on both sides of the carry road, dozens, a few hundred, all sliding the same way, all slowly, toward the place where the road ended.

Where are they going?

Downhill. Like the dead go downhill in your Field, toward the door. This is somebody's Field, and there's a door at the bottom of it.

He stopped on the cable.

Has anyone asked them?

No. Nobody's ever asked anybody on this slope. I don't think the door at the bottom asks.

He asked the nearest one.

He didn't stop to think. It was a few hundred meters off the road, if meters meant anything here, small and dim and gray and sliding, and he reached out to it as he'd reach out to anyone in his own Field, down through the dark, in the shape of a question. The oldest one.

Hello. I'm here. What do you want?

It didn't stop sliding; it couldn't, the slope wasn't his. But it turned toward the voice, as the last child of the first orchard had turned in the hill.

It says: where am I? said Seed. It doesn't know. It was asleep in a hand, then the hand opened and it was falling, and it's been falling for a year. It thought it was dreaming.

It says: is this the house? It heard, in the hand, that it was going home. Everyone in the hand heard that. It was the only thing the hand ever said.

No, he thought. Not yet. Nearly.

There's a door at the bottom. I don't know what's on the other side. Nobody's going to make you go through it. Do you want to?

The spark slid, and turned, and thought, in the slow way of the picked who'd been burning low in a hand longer than they knew.

It says: I want to go home, said Seed. Not the house. Home. Where it came from. A world. It's saying the name, and I don't know it.

It says: can I?

He looked back along the dark, up the carry road, up the slope, the way they'd come: toward the face of the picker and the web and its ten thousand cables and the thousand orchards at the ends of them. Somewhere out there, on one of them, was a world this spark had a name for.

He didn't know how to send it. He didn't know which thread was its own. He had no body, no staff, no stone. He was one boy on a cable in the dark on somebody else's slope.

I can't find its thread, said Seed. Not from here. Not without looking at the slope, and I'm not looking at the slope. I'm sorry.

The spark slid on past them toward the end of the road, still turned toward his voice. Still asking.

It says: can I?

Old Seven moved.

He felt the cold ember beside him step off the cable. Off the carry road, out into the dark onto the slope where the gray spark was sliding, and put himself in front of it.

Not stopping it; he couldn't, either. It wasn't his slope. But he stood in its path, one-armed, facing uphill, so that the spark slid into him the way a leaf in a stream fetches up against a stone, and stopped there against him, and went no further down.

He says hold on to me, said Seed. He says the boy'll find your thread. Not today. Later.

He says: I've held a wall with one arm. I can hold you.

The spark held on. And Old Seven stood on somebody else's slope in the dark at the end of the oldest road anything had ever made, one-armed, a lost light from a world nobody knew held against his chest, facing uphill, and didn't move.

He can't do that for all of them, said Seed, low. There are hundreds, and he's one. He knows.

He says: one at a time.

The Collector hadn't stopped.

It had slowed when Old Seven stepped off the road, as it slowed at the gray threads. But it couldn't stop now. The needle at its center was pulling straight down the slope, and the slope was pulling, and it had been pulled toward this place its whole long life.

And ahead, at the bottom of the slope, where the carry road ended, the dark changed.

Not light, not a shape. A place. As when you come down a road at night and feel a town before you see it: the air different, the sound different, the dark ahead not empty any more. Something vast and motionless, where the worn pale cable ran down into it and stopped, as a river stops at the sea.

I remember this, said the Collector again, low, as you'd speak in a temple. I remember the door.

We built a house round it.
