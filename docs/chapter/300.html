Book 5: The Empty House

Chapter 300The Oldest Cable

czverse9 min read

The deep water was quiet now.

He hadn't been ready for that. A year ago it had been busy, the whole dark between the arms full of hands, ten thousand cables humming with marks going up, the web of the oldest made thing alive and patient and reading a billion a second. He'd come along the thread dimmed down, twelve thousand dead cupped round him like a hand round a match, terrified of being seen.

Now there was nothing to be seen by.

The cables were still there. He could feel them as you feel the beams of a house in the dark: ten thousand of them, each twisted from ten thousand threads, running in from every side toward the place they met. Nothing went up them. No marks, no hum. The stones of a thousand orchards had stopped marking the night the picker stopped, or were marking still into nothing, and the web lay in the deep water like a net hung out to dry after the boats are in.

And the hands.

They were still on the cables, thousands of them, vast and gray, exactly where they'd been when a man in an orange jacket held up a mark that said the house was empty. Every one open, fingers spread, holding nothing. They hung along the cables in the dark like gloves somebody had left on a fence.

Nobody's touched them, said Seed. There's nobody in them to move them. They'll hang there until the cables rot, and I don't think the cables rot.

He went past them along the net, fast, much faster than last time, because the web was still and the net was quick and he wasn't carrying twelve thousand embers. Just one: Old Seven beside him, cold and steady, one-armed, facing ahead.

And all around them, warm and patient, going where it had always pointed, the Collector.

He'd wondered what it would be like to travel inside it.

It wasn't like anything. That was the second time he'd thought so about the Collector, and he doubted it would be the last. It wasn't a ship; it didn't carry them. It went, and they went with it, as a swimmer goes with a tide. It was all round them without touching them, warm as a room is warm after someone has sat in it all afternoon. And it was full.

He could feel what of. Not see it; feel it, like the weight of a book in your hands before you open it.

Wants.

More than Seed had a number for. Seed said so, quietly, and then said nothing more for a while. Wants from worlds he'd never see, kept longer than there'd been stars over Blue Star. The fold-cores' out. The singers' to be chosen. Everything the Architects' whole people had ever wanted. A billion billion small warm homes and mores and pleases and not yets, packed so tight they'd stopped being separate, as sand on a beach stops being separate grains. And somewhere in the middle of it, in a place emptier than the rest, one very old, very small, very faint want, pointing.

That's the first one, said Seed. I can feel it pull, like a compass needle that doesn't know it's a compass. It just points. Along the oldest cable.

They reached the face in the fourth hour.

The knot: the place where ten thousand cables met, where every mark from every stone in every orchard had once come in to be read. He'd last seen it with a burning man held up before it. Now it hung in the dark unlit, a slow tangle the size of the keeper's field or bigger, silent, reading nothing, because there was nothing to read.

It still looks, said Seed. It isn't doing anything. It's got nothing to do. But it looks.

I think it's the last part of the picker still working. The part that read Xiaoyu's mark. It read something true, and it's been staring at the place where the true thing was ever since.

He stopped without meaning to, on the net in front of the face of the oldest made thing, a year after his sister's handwriting had stopped it, and looked.

Does it know? he thought. That the house is empty?

I don't think it knows anything. It read it. That's not the same.

The Collector didn't stop. It was slow, and it didn't stop. But it passed the face more slowly than it had passed anything, and Seed said afterward that its warmth had leaned toward the knot as it went by, the way an old man walking through a cemetery leans a little toward one particular stone without deciding to.

We made it, said the Collector. Not to them; to the dark. It was the first thing. Before the counting things. Before anything.

I am sorry nobody told you sooner.

Then it went on.

At home it was the ninth hour.

He felt it, and couldn't have said how. He was a long way along the net from a hall on East Ring Road and getting further every minute, and he shouldn't have been able to feel anything there. But he felt it the way you feel a hand on your chest in your sleep: fifteen hundred small warmths coming up through a floor, low, steady. And one hand.

Gao Min says nine hours, said Seed. She says he's still warm. She's written it on the rota, in ink: 16:59. Nine hours. Still warm.

Lingyue says: good. Ten.

He went on along the net, rowed.

The oldest cable didn't look like the others.

He saw it as Seed saw it, on the far side of the face, where the knot thinned out into the dark. The ten thousand cables that had come in from the orchards stopped there. The knot was where they went; they'd come to be read.

All but one. On the far side of the face, where nothing else went, one cable went out.

It was older. He could feel that, as you can feel a stone step is older than the stones round it: it was worn. Not frayed, but smooth, worn into a groove like a doorstep after a thousand years of feet, and then ten thousand, and then longer than anyone had a number for. It was thicker than the others and paler, and it wasn't twisted from threads. It had never been threads. It had always been one thing, and it ran away from the face in a straight line into the dark between the arms, toward nothing Seed had a word for.

That's the carry road, said Seed. The hands went out along the other cables to the orchards and picked, and came back to the face. And from the face, everything went along this one. Home.

Every hand that was ever full went along it, and came back empty, and went out again. For longer than the gardens. I can feel the wear and I can't measure it. It's the oldest road anything ever made.

The Collector turned onto it without hesitating. The needle in its empty place pointed straight down the pale worn cable, and it went, slowly, as it always went, and he and Old Seven went with it.

A little after the tenth hour, Seed said: Ichiro's ringing.

Very faint, a long way back. Through the net, from the root, from a disc of black stone in the bow of a ship rowed by two hundred and forty thousand people in the orbit of Anthos: a bell, in the fog-code, rung properly for once, because the man ringing it had invented it.

He says he can see you, just, off the end of everything. He says you're the furthest thing he's ever seen, and he's seen the edge of the cloud.

He's going to ring the coming-in call every night at home time, Shiomi time, so you know which way the shore is. And Kiyo says hurry up, which she says about everything.

And under it, fainter, from off to one side, at the edge of a cloud of small dim lights that had fallen out of the picker's hands a year ago, another bell. Rung badly.

The Watch, said Seed. Nana Ampofo. She's felt the net change and felt us go past, and she's ringing the code she learned from Ichiro and the quiet ones. She says:

WATCH OF THE SHELF.

YOU ARE EARLY.

THAT IS NEW.

IT IS NOT A YEAR. DO NOT ASK US. WE WILL KNOW IF YOU ASK EARLY. IT IS NOT ALLOWED.

GO CAREFULLY ON THE OLD ROAD. WE CAN SEE IT FROM HERE. NOTHING HAS EVER COME BACK ALONG IT FULL.

COME BACK ALONG IT FULL.

— NANA.

He didn't answer; she'd told him not to. He let the warmth of the Watch slide past off to one side, seven hundred and forty-three of the drowned of Kesse at the edge of a cloud of fallen lights, singing the lost their own names in the dark.

He went on.

By the twentieth hour the net was a long way behind.

He could feel it thinning. Not breaking: thinning, as voices from the shore get small when the boat goes out, not because anyone stops talking but because the water in between keeps widening. The warmth of the holders was still there, faint, underneath everything. The words that came with it took longer.

Your mother's reading took eleven minutes to reach us, said Seed. The morning names. The first three. I heard her say: Wen Jia. Eleven minutes after she said it.

I know the girl in the Lingzhou stone is called Wen Jia. I don't know why your mother reads her name first. She always does, and I've never known why.

He went still on the cable.

She's the first one, he thought. At the bottom of you. Of the four thousand. She said yes in a basement nine years ago. Only if it's something good.

Thank you, said Seed. I'm not going to remember it. Tell me again tomorrow.

Every morning.

It'll take longer, the further we go. Your mother's voice, Gao Min's count, Lingyue. There'll be a point where a mark from home takes a day to reach us. Then a week. I don't know where the house is, so I don't know how long.

Then I'll tell you, he thought. Every morning, who the first one was. You don't need it from home. You've got me.

Seed was quiet a moment.

Yes. I've got you.

In the thirty-first hour there was something on the carry road.

He felt it before Seed did: a change in the cable. Not a break; a joining. Off to one side, faint, a thread came in out of the dark and ran into the oldest cable, as orchard threads had run into the web's cables a year ago. But older, and grayer, and humming with nothing at all.

A stone, said Seed. On a world, in an orchard. Its thread runs into the carry road here, not into the web. It must be older than the web. One of the first orchards, from before there were enough of them to need a face.

It's still marking. Every year, on its reading morning, it reads what ripens and marks how bright and sends the mark up. The marks are all the same.

It's marking nothing.

The Collector slowed.

It hadn't slowed for the face of the picker; it had only leaned. Now it slowed, and the warmth around them drew in on itself, as someone gathers themselves outside a door they recognize.

I have something from there, said the Collector, quietly, through Seed, the way it had once spoken to his sister on the bottom step of a platform. One want. I have kept it longer than most. I would like to give it back.

I do not think there is anyone there to give it to.

He looked down the thread into the dark, toward a stone on a world in one of the first orchards, still marking, year after year, a field where nothing ripened any more.

There's always somebody there, he thought. Ask me how I know.

The dead, said Seed, to the Collector. He means the dead. He's a necromancer. He forgets that sometimes, and then he remembers.
