Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 277Deaf

czverse8 min read

Tam found her on the third night, in the eleventh ship, in the fourth array, and he knew her voice.

"Seri," he said.

He was sitting on the platform of the Awakening Hall with his back against the side of the warm black stone, as Yuanshu sat when he went up a thread, his eyes shut and his face turned up toward the roof and beyond it. He'd sat there since dusk. The Candle's children had brought him food; he hadn't eaten it. Gao Min had brought him a blanket; he hadn't noticed. He was listening.

"From Corraine. The street behind the fish market. She was two years older. She used to throw stones at me." His voice was very steady. "She was an E. They took her the year before me. I thought she'd be on a ship. I didn't know which one."

He opened his eyes.

"She's in the Severin flagship. Fourth array. She's been in the wall twenty-one years."

"Can she hear you?"

"No," said the boy. "They stopped up her ears. I can hear her, but she can't hear anything. She's just burning. Not the way the Candle burned after the cousins came. The old way. Waiting." He looked at the stone beside him. "She's singing."

"Singing?"

"To herself. Inside. The way the girl from Halloran did. A song from Corraine, the one about the boats." He shut his eyes again. "She doesn't know anyone can hear her. She's been singing it twenty-one years."

I'd like to try something, said Seed. I'm asking first. You're awake, so it counts.

And I'd like to ask Tam and Wen Jia and Xiaoyu, because I can't do it alone.

He was on the vault steps at two in the morning. They were all there; they were always all there now. Xiaoyu at the long table with the marker. Kwan with the laptop. Gao Min with her thermos. Lingyue on the stairs with Xiaodeng asleep in the sling and the sword across her knees. Su Mingli, who'd stopped saying she wasn't staying. Tam, back from the hall with Gao Min's blanket round his shoulders because Gao Min had put it there and wouldn't let him take it off.

Go on.

The cells in the walls of the ships are stone. The same black, cut from the stones that read them. I saw it on the Candle and didn't think about it. I'm thinking about it now.

They've put a layer round each cell so the people inside can't hear. Not sound; they could never hear sound. They heard me through the keeper's eye the way the dead hear the Death Law, through the thing they're in. The layer stops that.

It doesn't stop the stone being stone.

Every cell's on the net. Every one, four million one hundred and eighty thousand, tied through the knot in the deep water to every other stone in every orchard. To Wen Jia's. To the one on Anthos where Iselde sent her mark.

A mark can come down a thread into a stone; Wen Jia caught one from Anthos. I'd like to send a mark down into one cell. Not a sound. Not a question. Not hearing. A mark, the way the stone used to mark them. Except this one would say something true.

Nobody spoke for a while.

"What would it say?" said Xiaoyu.

That's what I'd like to ask you. It has to be true. Not true the way words are true: true the way your writing is true, the way the house was empty. So that when she reads it she doesn't just read it, she knows it.

And it has to be short. She's been in a wall twenty-one years with her ears stopped. She hasn't read anything in twenty-one years.

And Tam has to aim it. I can find the ship and the array. I can't find her. There are forty thousand cells in the fourth array of the Severin flagship and I can't hear any of them. He can.

Tam had sat up on the bottom stair with the blanket round his shoulders.

"Seri," he said.

Yes.

Xiaoyu wrote it at four in the morning.

Not on the wall: on the platform floor in front of the warm black stone, in square capitals, as she'd written THE HOUSE IS EMPTY. YOU CAN STOP. She sat on her heels with the marker for a long time before writing anything. Tam sat beside her, and they argued quietly, as two people argue who are both seventeen in some sense and one of whom has been twelve for nineteen years.

"She can hear," said Tam. "Tell her that. Tell her she can hear."

"She can't. That's the point."

"She can hear herself. She's singing. She's been singing twenty-one years. She can hear that."

Xiaoyu looked at him a long moment. Then she wrote it.

SOMEONE CAN HEAR YOU SINGING.

IT IS TAM. FROM CORRAINE. THE STREET BEHIND THE FISH MARKET.

YOU USED TO THROW STONES AT HIM.

Wen Jia tied it. Not as Iselde had tied her first mark, clumsily, like a child's knot, but as Hush had shown her, as water ties itself round a stone. She said through the staff, briskly, that she'd been practicing, and that she'd like it noted this was the first mark she'd ever sent to a person and not a stone.

Tam put his hand on the warm black stone with the blue jacket over its shoulder, as he'd put it there the morning he chose, and shut his eyes, and listened.

Up through the roof, past the sky, to ninety burning cathedrals round the keeper's field. To the largest, in the center, forty times the size of the Candle, spired and buttressed and windowed and lit end to end in the colors of the House of Severin, with a thing in its bow the size of a courtyard that Seed could feel and wouldn't look at directly. Into it, past the corridors and halls and the people who weren't in walls. Down to the fourth array. Forty thousand cells, each burning low the old way, each with its ears stopped.

And one of them singing.

"There," said Tam.

Wen Jia sent it.

It went up her thread into the net. He felt it go, as he'd felt the mark to Anthos go: a small true thing, tied as water ties round a stone, rising out of the crown of the tree of Laws in the Lingzhou stone into the dark, through the knot in the deep water where nobody read anything any more.

And down. Not into the Severin flagship's stone; it didn't have one. Into a cell: a small dark crystal cell cut from the stone that had read a girl on a world called Corraine twenty-one years ago and marked her E. In the fourth array. Behind a layer that stopped her ears.

The layer didn't stop it.

It wasn't sound or hearing or a voice through the Death Law or the keeper's eye. It was a mark: the thing the stone the cell was cut from had been made, ten thousand years ago, to carry. The cell knew what to do with a mark as a lung knows what to do with air. It took it in.

And the girl inside read it.

She's stopped singing, said Seed.

Tam's hand was flat on the stone and his eyes were shut. His face didn't move.

She's reading it. Again, and again. Nine times. She doesn't know how she's reading it. She hasn't read anything in twenty-one years. She has no eyes; she's in a wall. But it's in her, the way a mark's in a stone, and she can read it.

And it's true. She knows it's true. She knows someone can hear her. She knows it's Tam, and the street behind the fish market, and that she used to throw stones at him.

On the platform Tam had begun to shake.

She's— I can't hear her. Tam can.

"She's laughing," said Tam.

His eyes were still shut and his face was wet.

"She's laughing. She says: Tam? Little Tam? Who cried when the boats went out? She says: you were so annoying."

He laughed too. It came out of him all at once, as it had come out of Wen Hao on a bus at the river, as it came out of people on this world when something they'd put down long ago was handed back to them without warning.

"She says: where are you? She says: how can you hear me? What's happening? Am I dead?"

He put his other hand flat on the stone beside the first.

"She's asking," he said. "Yuanshu. She's asking. She's never asked anything. She's in a wall with her ears stopped and she's asking questions."

She can't hear us, said Seed. The layer's still there. She can't hear Tam or me. She can only read.

But Tam can hear her, and Wen Jia can send, and Xiaoyu can write. So she can ask, and Tam can hear, and Xiaoyu can write the answer, and Wen Jia can send it down, and she can read it.

It's very slow. One mark at a time. It's a conversation.

Xiaoyu was already writing. She knelt on the platform floor in front of the stone and wrote as fast as she could while Tam told her what the girl in the wall was saying:

YOU ARE NOT DEAD.

YOU ARE IN A WALL IN A SHIP IN THE SKY OVER A WORLD CALLED BLUE STAR.

TAM IS ON THE GROUND. HE CAN HEAR THE ONES IN THE WALLS. HE ASKED A STONE TO MAKE HIM ABLE TO.

SOMEONE IS GOING TO ASK YOU WHAT YOU WANT.

NOT YET. SOON.

KEEP SINGING SO HE CAN FIND YOU.

Wen Jia tied it and sent it, and Tam listened, and after a while said in a voice that didn't quite work:

"She says: all right. She says: I'll keep singing."

It works, said Seed. On one cell, with one person who can hear her, one who can write, and one who can send.

There are four million one hundred and eighty thousand. And six days.

He sat on the platform with the staff across his knees and his back to the warm stone and looked at his sister kneeling on the floor with the marker, and the boy with both hands on the stone and his face wet, and the words on the floor, true, in square capitals that would never come off.

It's too slow, he thought. One at a time.

Yes. One at a time it'd take eleven hundred years.

But I didn't want to know if it was fast. I wanted to know if it was possible. It is.

Now I'd like to think about how to make it fast. And about the thing in the bow of the Severin flagship, which is a stone, the oldest there is, and on the net like every other.

And I'd like to tell you first: I've got an idea. And it needs somebody to go somewhere and not come back.

And I don't want it to be anyone.
