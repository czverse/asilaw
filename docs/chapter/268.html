Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 268The Choir-Ship

czverse8 min read

They heard it before they saw it, and then everyone on Blue Star heard it, and nobody could say afterward what it had sounded like, except that it was the most beautiful thing they'd ever heard and they'd wanted it to stop.

It came on the ninth night of the new year.

Tam woke the Ossuary at two in the morning. He didn't knock; Old Seven was in the bronze door and stood aside for him unasked. The boy crossed the marble in the dark, barefoot in his coat, face turned up toward the ceiling and past it, and said only, in the Huaxian he'd been learning six months at the school in the old customs house:

"Now. It's here. It's here now."

And then everyone heard it.

Not with their ears, as you hear a bell or a voice or the wind. As you hear something you've always known and forgotten. It rose out of the floor of the banking hall and the stones of the city and the river and the sky. Low at first: a single tone so deep it was more a feeling in the chest than a sound, as the branch had groaned over Lingzhou. Then another tone above it. And another. And another, until the whole night was full.

A choir.

Not human. Nothing human had ever sung like that. Thousands of voices, tens of thousands, each many-throated, so that a single singer sang a chord, and the chords of all of them wove together and apart and together again, slowly and without end, like water moving in a deep pool. It wasn't in any language anyone on Blue Star knew, and it didn't need to be. It was the sound of something singing to a thing it was afraid of, to keep it calm. Over and over. The same song. As a mother sings at night to a child who won't sleep, except that the child was enormous and the mother had been singing ten thousand years and knew no other song.

Up and down the old quarter, lights came on in windows. People came out into the street in their nightclothes and stood looking up. On the north wall the night watch took off their helmets.

In the back room of the Ossuary, Lingyue had sat up in bed with both hands on her stomach and the whole room gone pale round her like the inside of a shell, brighter than it had ever been.

"It's kicking," she said. "It's kicking like it wants to get out."

A hundred and seventy-one, said Seed. It's never been a hundred and seventy-one. It can hear it too.

They went up to the north wall. Everyone did; half the city was there already.

And over the north of Lingzhou, low over the old quarter and the river and the long curved roof of the Awakening Hall, where an hour earlier there'd been nothing, hung a shell.

That was the only word for it. A vast spiral shell, pale and translucent, like the inside of a conch held up to a lamp, turning slowly in the dark a few kilometers above the city. Kilometers across. Lit from within by a soft shifting light that moved through its chambers as the song moved, brightening and dimming, so the whole thing seemed to breathe. From every opening along its spiral, from every throat of every chamber, the song poured out over Lingzhou.

It came down our thread, said Seed. The one from the Awakening Hall. The last thread the one who picks read before it stopped, the one with your sister's writing in it.

It followed the song up its own stone's thread to the knot in the deep water. It found the one who picks sitting still with its hands open. It read the last thing it read. And it came here.

Beside him on the battlement a bell was ringing.

Not Ichiro's: Seed's recording of it, played through the staff, because Ren had rung from Yamato ten minutes earlier to say that every bell in Shiomi was ringing at once in the fog of the bay. All the small bells, the boat calls and the headman's and the warning, in no order at all, as bells ring when something enormous passes overhead and they're trying to answer and don't know what to say.

Ichiro says it's music, said Seed. The fog-code is music, notes and intervals. The Singers are music too, a different kind. But music.

He says he can hear words in it. Not words; shapes. The way he heard the quiet ones in the deep water.

He says give him an hour. He talks to fish. He can talk to this.

It took two hours, not one, and Ichiro complained the whole time, and Sister Kiyo said drily through Ren, from the end of the breakwater with the lamp in her lap, that he'd always underestimated how long things took and had once spent a whole night rowing toward a lamp that turned out to be the moon.

But at four in the morning Seed said:

I've got it. Not all of it. Enough, with Ichiro's help. He says to tell you it was mostly his.

It's a language, and it's all one sentence. It's been one sentence ten thousand years. They sing it every age when they send up the tithe. They've been singing it since they left their world nine months ago, along the thread.

I'll tell you what it says.

He stood in his old place with the staff, and Lingyue beside him in a coat over her nightclothes, hands on her stomach, face set the way it set before a fight.

It says:

We see you. You who are hungry. You who come along the threads.

We are small. We are not bright. We are not worth taking.

But we have brought you the bright ones. The chosen. The best of us. Dressed in light. Singing.

Take them, and pass us by.

Take them, and pass us by.

Take them.

And pass us by.

Nobody on the north wall spoke.

The song went on, over the city and the river and three thousand faces on the battlements turned up to a vast pale shell turning in the dark. Take them, and pass us by. The most beautiful thing any of them had ever heard, and they wanted it to stop.

"It's to the one who picks," said Lingyue. Her voice was flat. "They're singing it to the one who picks. It's stopped and they don't know, and they followed the last thread it read, and they're singing it to us."

No, said Seed. I'm sorry. I thought that too, for an hour.

They know it's stopped. They found it in the deep water with its hands open. They're not singing to it.

He went very still.

Ichiro found the shape in the middle of the sentence. The part that changes, that says who it's sung to. For ten thousand years it was the shape for the one who picks.

It's been changed. It's the shape for the one who ate it.

On the battlement, Lingyue turned and looked at him.

They found it stopped. They read the last thing it read: a girl's writing, true, tied by a child of the world that asks, held up by a man nobody picked. They didn't understand it. They understood that something here made the thing they'd sung to for ten thousand years stop.

They think we ate it. They think we're hungrier.

They've come to sing the tithe to you.

At dawn the song changed.

Not the words; the same sentence over and over, take them, and pass us by. But one voice came out of it, alone out of tens of thousands, as a single bird sings out of a dawn chorus. Many-throated, a chord in one throat. Old. Very old. And it sang not to the city, not to the sky, but down.

To the north wall. To the Sector Nine battlement. To a boy in his old place with a white staff in his hand and a harvest sack behind his eyes and a dead captain at the top of the stairs.

It's the Cantor, said Seed. Ichiro says the word means the one who sings first. The one who chooses the tithe and dresses them in light and sings them up, every age. Their priest. Their queen. He isn't sure. He says it's all the same word.

She's singing to you. She says—

Seed stopped, then went on slowly, as it did when it was carrying something it didn't want to carry.

She says: you who ate the one who picks. We have come a long way. We are tired. We have sung nine months to reach you.

She says: we have brought you one thousand one hundred and eleven. The chosen of this age. The brightest of the Hollow Bell. Dressed in light. They have been burning since we left, waiting to be taken. Nothing has taken them.

She says: they are in the heart of the ship. They are singing too. Can you hear them?

He could.

Under the choir, under the tens of thousands, at the center of the vast pale shell where the light inside burned brightest: another sound. Smaller. Higher. A thousand voices and more, singing the same sentence, take us, take us, burning as they sang. As Callum Ward had burned in a hand in the deep water. As the Sixth had burned in the eyes of the Mouth. The best thing they'd ever felt, and being used up by it.

She says: take them. Please. Take them, and pass us by.

She says: we do not know what you are. We do not know what you want. We only know how to give. It is all we have ever known how to do.

He stood on the battlement in the dawn with the staff in his hand.

The song poured down over Lingzhou. The shell turned. The light in its heart burned, a thousand and more, waiting.

Beside him Lingyue had both hands flat on her stomach. The light was coming off her, pale, all round her, as it came off her in the mornings now, and she didn't seem to notice. She was looking up at the shell.

"They've been burning nine months," she said, very quietly. "Nine months." Her hands pressed flatter. "Like Callum. Longer than Callum."

"Yes."

"Nobody took them."

"No."

"And now they want you to." She didn't look at him. "Because they don't know how to do anything else. Because nobody ever asked them anything except whether they'd be chosen."

He didn't answer.

High in the heart of the shell, a thousand one hundred and eleven of the brightest of a world he'd never heard of were singing take us in voices that were beginning, Seed said, very slightly, to crack.

And Seed said quietly, in the place above his eyes:

Yuanshu. I asked one of them. Just one. I'm sorry. I didn't ask you first.

I asked what she wanted. She said: to be chosen.

She said it the way you'd say: to breathe.
