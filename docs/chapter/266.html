Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 266A Hundred and Fifty-Eight

czverse9 min read

The first person from another world to stand on Blue Star came down a road of light onto the north wall of Lingzhou at five in the morning, and the first thing she did was look at the sky and say nothing at all.

The keeper had laid the road. It had asked first, as it asked everything: whether she wanted one, whether the boy wanted her to come, whether the city did. Everybody had said yes. So at a quarter to five a slant of gold came down out of the north from beside the small white ring, past the crinkle of the Eighth Candle, to the Sector Nine battlement, where he stood in his old place with the staff, Lingyue beside him and Old Seven at the top of the stairs. And down it, slowly, in a gray uniform with gold at the collar, holding a child by the hand, came Iselde Varr-Anthos.

She was taller than he'd expected. Straight-backed. Black hair cropped short, as they cropped it on listener ships, Seed said, so it wouldn't float in the folds. A face taught from the moment it could move exactly what to do in front of other people, doing none of it now.

She stepped off the road onto the battlement with her sister's hand in hers and looked up.

The sky over Lingzhou at five in the morning: gray going blue in the east over the river, the last stars, the small white ring in the north. Beside it, if you knew where to look, a crinkle, and a vast gray ring with a soft white light at its center, and at the end of a branch nobody could see, a small gold point.

"There's so much of it," said the Listener-Captain of the Eighth Candle, in the Federation's language, and Seed carried it. "On a ship you only see it through glass. In the folds you don't see it at all."

Beside her Maelis, twelve in the body and twenty-three inside, barefoot on the cold stone in a blanket Torvin had given her, wasn't looking at the sky. She was looking at Old Seven.

She says: is he dead?

"Yes," said Yuanshu.

She says: does he mind?

He says: not usually.

Maelis looked up at the tall dead captain a long moment, his spear grounded in a finger and a thumb. Then slowly she let go of her sister's hand, crossed the battlement, stood in front of him, and held out her own.

Old Seven looked down at it. Then he crouched, as a tall man crouches to talk to a child, and took it very carefully in the sergeant's hand.

He says you were in a wall eleven years. He was in the ground thirty.

He says it gets better. You won't believe that. It does.

They sat on the battlement while the sun came up. Iselde didn't want a chair. She sat on the cold stone with her back to the parapet, as he sat, and looked at the city waking below them, and talked.

"I'm going to tell you what is coming," she said. "First. The way you do."

"Yes."

"The Candle folds home in nine days. Thirty-two thousand and forty people will carry it, in turns, burning low. When we come out of the last fold over Anthos I will open every wall in the arrays. Torvin has built cradles, sixty of them. It will take a month." Her voice was very even. "By the end of the first day the House will know. By the end of the first week, the Assembly."

"And then?"

"And then they will come here." She looked out over the city. "Not one listener. The Houses. Anthos and the others. They will come to see the world that stopped the Reaper and turned a Listener-Captain into a thief." She was quiet. "They will come with ships. Many. Every one of them made of cores."

He said nothing.

"Some of them will want what you have. The thing in your head. The stones that ask. The way you stopped the Reaper. They will want to know how, so they can use it. Some will want to destroy it, because a stone that asks is the end of the ladder, and the ladder is everything." She looked at him. "Some of them will be like me. I do not know how many. I did not know I was like me until eleven days ago."

Lingyue had been quiet. She sat on his other side with the sword across her knees, looking at the woman from the sky.

"You're SSS," she said.

Iselde turned her head.

"So am I," said Shen Lingyue. "Was. The stone said so. Dawnbreaker." She took the laminated card out of her coat pocket: her photograph at eighteen, unsmiling, the Shen crest, and across the gold letters SSS. DAWNBREAKER a line of black pen. "I crossed it out on television. In front of everybody."

Iselde looked at the card. Then she reached into the collar of her uniform and drew out a thin chain with a small disc of black stone on it, polished, the size of a coin, with three gold marks cut into it.

"Every Federation child is given one on the morning of the reading. Cut from the stone that read them. You wear it until you die. You show it at every door. In the Federation's language the word for it is the same as the word for your face."

Lingyue looked at the three gold marks.

"Are you going to cross it out?"

"Yes," said Iselde. "Not here. In front of the Assembly, on Anthos. In front of my mother." She tucked it back inside her collar. "You did yours in front of your world. I would like to do mine in front of mine."

Lingyue looked at her a long moment. Then she held out her hand: not to shake, but flat, palm up, as she'd held it out on the breakwater at Shiomi with a lamp in it.

There was a flame in it, small and pale.

"For the fold," said Shen Lingyue. "So you can see where you're going."

Iselde Varr-Anthos looked at the pale flame in the palm of a woman from a world her charts called Unlisted Orchard Four-Four-Seven-One. Then she held out her own hand, and Lingyue tipped hers slowly, as you tip a cup to pour, and the light ran across.

And stayed, in the palm of a captain from another world. Burning. Held.

"It's warm," said Iselde.

"It's supposed to be," said Lingyue.

Maelis asked before she left.

She came back across the battlement from Old Seven, barefoot in her blanket, and stood in front of Yuanshu where he sat against the parapet, and looked at him with eyes that were twelve years old and twenty-three.

She says there's a stone on Anthos, the one that read her. Someone would have to be in it to ask, the way the girl's in yours.

She says she said yes to the honor at twelve, because everyone was watching and she didn't know what it was. She knows what it is now. She'd like to say yes again, knowing, to the stone. So no other twelve-year-old on Anthos says yes to the honor without being asked first what they want.

He looked at her and thought about it a long time. Iselde said nothing. Lingyue said nothing. The sun came up over the river.

"Not yet," he said.

Maelis didn't move.

"Your body's twelve," said Yuanshu. "Your yes is twenty-three. I don't know which one I'd be asking. And I'd have to put my hands on a stone on a world I've never seen and graft you into it, and you could never come out except through the door." He held the staff. "I'm not saying no. I'm saying ask me again in a year. When you've had a year with a sky. When you've seen what you'd be giving up."

The girl looked at him a long moment.

She says: that's what the girl in the stone would say.

"Yes."

She says: all right. A year. She'll ask you. You'd better be here.

The Eighth Candle folded on the ninth night.

Everyone watched from the north wall: three thousand people on the battlements, and among them the Candle's children, nine thousand five hundred and fifty-nine, in city clothes, holding hands, looking up. Tam at the front at the parapet beside Xiaoyu, his face turned north.

A crinkle in the sky beside the small white ring. Then nothing.

Tam made a sound.

He says they're singing. All of them. Not just the girl from Halloran. All thirty-two thousand, in the walls, burning low, in turns. Rowing.

He says they're singing because they chose.

He can hear it going away, very fast, very far. He can still hear it.

He stood at the parapet with his face to the north long after the crinkle was gone and there was nothing beside the small white ring but sky. Xiaoyu stood beside him. Then, without being asked, she put her hand on his shoulder: the way you stand when you want to do something and are frightened.

He didn't turn round. But he put his own hand up and held hers there.

Yuanshu.

Two in the morning. He was on the narrow bed in the back room with the window open to the courtyard, Lingyue asleep against his shoulder in the way she slept, all at once, like a soldier. Hush on the sill in the dark with its limbs drawn in.

I'd like to ask her something when she wakes up. I'm telling you first.

Go on.

I can hear it. I've been able to since Shiomi. I didn't listen. I didn't ask to. It isn't mine to listen to. But I can hear it, the way I hear the cores in a wall and the drowned at the bottom of the sea, and you.

A heart. Very small. Very fast.

I'd like to ask her if I may listen properly. And count it.

He lay in the dark.

Ask her. In the morning.

She said yes. Obviously. She said it at breakfast in the back room over a bowl of rice porridge she was actually eating, for once, with his mother at the table and Xiaoyu on the stairs, without looking up from the bowl, as she said everything that mattered: Yes. Obviously. Count it.

Seed counted.

A hundred and fifty-eight.

It's very fast. Faster than yours has ever been. Faster than on the step of the hut when you asked her. Faster than the pier.

Bai Lu says that's right. Bai Lu says that's exactly right. A hundred and fifty-eight.

Nobody at the table spoke.

I'm going to say something now. I know it can't hear me. I know it isn't anyone yet. Article Twelve. Your mother's.

I'd like to start anyway.

Hello. I'm Seed. I live in your father's head. I was a harvest sack once. I count things.

I'm going to ask you what you want. Not now; you can't answer. Every day, until you can. And after.

I'm telling you first.

Lingyue put her spoon down. She didn't look up. Her hand had gone flat on her stomach under the gray sweater without her noticing.

At the end of the table, his mother put her face in her hands.

That night Tam came to the Ossuary.

Old Seven let him in. The boy crossed the marble in the dark, barefoot still, to the vault steps where Yuanshu sat with the staff, and stopped, his face turned not to Yuanshu but to the north. To the wall, and through it, and past it.

He says there's something else. Not the Candle; the Candle's gone. He can't hear it any more, it's too far. Something else. Farther than the Candle. Coming the other way.

Singing.

Yuanshu went still.

Not like the cores. Not like the girl from Halloran. Many voices, very old, very beautiful. All the same song, over and over, the way you'd sing to something you were afraid of, to keep it calm.

He says it's the saddest thing he's ever heard. And it's coming here.

On the long white wall behind him, at the very top, above HUNDRED-ARROW LIN, in the strip the stepladder didn't quite reach, his sister's capitals said THE ONE WHO PICKS. COMING., and under it, written the morning after the deep water:

TOLD. STOPPED.

And beside it, newer and smaller, in the same square black hand:

THE SINGERS. SANG THEIR BRIGHTEST. NOTHING CAME. COMING.
