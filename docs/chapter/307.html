Book 5: The Empty House

Chapter 307Four

czverse10 min read

The bell came at four in the morning on the twenty-seventh day, and Xiaoyu read it with her eyes shut.

Lingyue was on the platform. She'd been there since midnight. Bai Lu had told her at eleven to go home and sleep, and she'd gone home and lain on the bed in the back room of the Ossuary for forty minutes looking at the ceiling, then got up and come back. Bai Lu had watched her come in and said nothing, which was worse than saying something.

Xiaodeng was asleep in the sling. Her husband was asleep on the mattress under the blue jacket. He weighed fifty-four kilos. On the morning he lay down he'd weighed sixty-five.

"It's Ichiro," said Xiaoyu from the bottom tier. She sat up with her palm on the floor and the look she got when a mark came in, as though she were listening to someone talking in the next room. "Through Halvarde. Through the root."

She opened her eyes. "They've decided."

FOUR HOUSES HAVE FOLDED. TWENTY SHIPS. THEY LEFT THE TWELVE AT THE EDGE.

THEY ARE BURNING THE OLD WAY. HARDER THAN THE OLD WAY. THE FOG-RINGER SAYS THE WALLS ARE SCREAMING. HE CAN HEAR THEM THROUGH THE ROOT. HE SAYS HE IS SORRY HE CAN HEAR THEM AND NOT REACH THEM. THEY HAVE CUT THEIR CELLS OFF FROM THE ROOT. HE CANNOT ASK THEM FROM ANTHOS. ONLY FROM CLOSE.

THEY ARE COMING TO THE SHALLOWS.

THEY ARE COMING FOR THE SCRIBE.

THEY WANT HER TO WRITE: THIS IS YOUR FACE.

Xiaoyu stopped.

Her voice hadn't shaken. Lingyue had been listening for it, and it hadn't. Her sister-in-law sat on the bottom tier of the Awakening Hall at four in the morning in a school jacket she'd outgrown, marker in her pocket, palm flat on the cold floor, and read aloud the words of four Great Houses of the Lamplit Worlds who were burning a million people in the walls of twenty ships to come here and make her write a lie.

"There's a last bit," said Xiaoyu. "From Ichiro. Not Halvarde."

TELL THE GIRL WITH THE SWORD: SIX DAYS. MAYBE FIVE. HE'S SORRY. HE'S STILL WATCHING. HE ISN'T GOING ANYWHERE.

TELL THE SCRIBE: DON'T.

The long table in the banking hall was full by seven.

Lingyue hadn't called anybody. At a quarter past four Xiaoyu had written it on the white plaster round the corner of the vault door, FOUR HOUSES. TWENTY SHIPS. SIX DAYS. FOR ME., and gone back to the hall, and by seven the people who read that wall every morning had read it and come.

Su Mingli. Qin Hao with his notebook. Gao Min with both rotas: the holding, and the second one she'd started nine days ago and never finished. Dabao filling the end of the bench. Fat Tan with a thermos. Bai Lu. Tam and Seri, barefoot, side by side, holding hands under the table. Kwan with his laptop. Sister Ning, down from the Listening Pavilion on the first bus. And on the open channel through the stone, faint: Orlov from Novaya Zarya, Roman Sterling from the north pier of Port Halvard, Ren from the shrine.

At the head of the table, in her wheelchair, with the book of names shut on her knee for the first time in three years: Zhou Wanqing.

Nobody had asked Lingyue to take the other end. She took it anyway, Xiaodeng awake in her lap and her sword leaning on the table, because her husband wasn't there and somebody had to.

"Tell me what they can do," she said. "All of it. Then tell me what we can."

Su Mingli told her, short and flat, in the voice she gave orders in, because it was the only voice she had for things that mattered.

"Precedence is broken," said the Master of the Tianshu Institute. "That's the good news. The Lens is whole, the fog-ringer's in it, and it asks. The Ladder isn't in the first stone any more. They can't freeze us like Severin did." She kept her arms folded. "That's all the good news."

"And the rest?"

"They're Paragons. Four of them, with their Houses behind them. Severin was above a King; Seed said so. I stood in front of him and couldn't lift a finger." Her mouth thinned. "That was Precedence. Without it I'd be able to lift a finger. I'd still lose. Just slower." She looked down the table. "I'm one of the strongest things in Huaxia. Shen Jinghong's older and better, and he'd lose too. Sterling would lose. Orlov would lose."

On the channel, faint, Orlov said in his stiff old-fashioned Huaxian: "She is correct. I would lose with great dignity."

"And the ships," said Su Mingli. "Twenty. Each one ten times the size of the Candle, burning a million people in the walls. We've nothing that flies like that. Lords go up. They don't go along."

Lingyue sat with her daughter in her lap.

"So we can't fight them."

"Not in the sky," said Su Mingli. "Not on the ground either, if they land. Not and win."

"They don't want to fight," said Qin Hao.

Everyone looked at him. He wasn't looking at anyone. He was writing in his notebook in his small square hand and didn't stop.

"If they wanted a fight they'd have brought eighty ships. They brought twenty; the other twelve Houses said no." He turned a page. "They want one thing. They want her to write four words. They think if she writes it it'll be true, because everything she writes is true, and then they'll have their faces back. And the Ladder. And Precedence." He looked up at last, down the table, at the girl on Lingyue's left. "So they need her alive. And willing. A Scribe writes what she writes. You can't make her."

"Can't you?" said Lingyue.

There was a pause.

"You can make anyone write anything," said Zhou Wanqing from the head of the table, very level, "if you hurt enough people in front of them."

Nobody answered that.

"That's what the walls are for," said Tam.

He said it very quietly. He was twelve in the body still and thirty-something inside, and he'd spent most of a year with his ear against walls, listening to the ones inside them. He was holding Seri's hand under the table so hard his knuckles had gone white.

"They're screaming," said Tam. "I can hear them. Not well; it's very far. But it's twenty ships and they're burning them harder than anybody ever burned anybody, to get here fast." He swallowed. "When they get here, they'll hold one up in front of her. A wall. And they'll say: write it, or we burn this one out. Then the next one. Then the next."

He looked at Xiaoyu.

"That's what I'd do," said Tam, "if I was them. It's what they did to my mother. They didn't hurt her. They showed her me."

Xiaoyu hadn't said a word since four in the morning.

She said one now. "I'll go."

Lingyue's hand closed on the edge of the table.

"Not to write it," said Xiaoyu. "I'm not writing it. I'm never writing it." She didn't look at her mother; she looked at Lingyue. "But they're coming here for me. Here, to Lingzhou, to the hall where he's lying on a mattress with a line in his arm and six thousand people holding him." Her voice was steady; it had been steady all morning. "If I'm in the hall, they come to the hall. If I'm somewhere else, they come somewhere else."

"No," said Lingyue.

"You haven't heard it yet."

"I don't need to."

"Yes, you do." Xiaoyu looked at her across the table: eighteen, Scribe, unmarked, with her mother's voice and her brother's eyes. "You need to ask me. That's how it works now. That's his whole point. You don't get to decide for me because I'm his sister and you're frightened."

Lingyue went very still.

She thought of her husband on a mattress under a blue jacket, asleep, terribly thin, impossibly far. She thought of every time she'd wanted to decide for him. On the north wall. At the pier. On the platform with her hand on his chest: nine hours, I don't care if the house is empty or full or on fire. She'd decided for him that once, and warned him first. Once.

She thought of a dead nun on a shore for twelve hundred years who'd said: I'm not going to sit on the shore while he goes out again.

"All right," said Shen Lingyue. "I'm asking."

Her sister-in-law waited.

"Where do you want to be," said Lingyue, "when they come?"

Xiaoyu told her. She'd been thinking about it since four that morning. She'd been thinking about it, Lingyue understood, since the night on Anthos when a mark she'd written went out through a fog-ringer in a lens to every Paragon in the Lamplit Worlds at once and turned their faces dark. She'd known they'd come. She'd written THAT'S NOT A QUESTION on a wall because she knew.

"Not in the hall," said Xiaoyu. "Not in the city. Not anywhere they'd have to come down to."

She looked up, through the ceiling of the banking hall, through the bank, through the sky over Lingzhou. North.

"The keeper," she said. "I want to meet them at the keeper, in its field, where the cousins are. Two hundred and six million of them in the walls, who've been asked every morning for two years, who row." She laid her hand flat on the table, as she laid it on the floor of the hall. "Tam says they'll be screaming in their walls when they get there. And the keeper's field is full of people who used to scream in walls and stopped, because somebody asked them what they wanted."

"They're cut off," said Tam. "The cells. From the root. Ichiro can't reach them."

"Not from Anthos," said Xiaoyu. "From close. Marks pass deafness; you told me, the night we found Seri. The cell's stone, and a mark goes through stone." She looked at him. "I'll write the marks. All of them. True ones. And you tell me who to write them to."

Tam stared at her.

"Two hundred and six million people who know what it's like," said Xiaoyu. "Twenty ships of people who don't yet. And me in the middle with a marker." She looked back at Lingyue. "That's where I want to be. Away from him. Where they have to come to me through the place that's full of everyone they ever burned."

The long table was silent.

At its head, Zhou Wanqing opened the book of names on her knee, found her place, and shut it again on her finger.

"She's right," said Yuanshu's mother. "God help me. She's right."

Lingyue sat with her daughter in her lap and didn't speak.

She thought about the keeper's field. She'd been in it once, on an island, in the dark, for eleven nights. She thought about twenty ships the size of cities, burning people in their walls harder than anyone ever had, coming out of a fold at the edge of the Shallows in five days or six. And finding, instead of a small blue world with a hall in it and a man on a mattress, a girl in a school jacket standing in a ring of light the size of a continent, writing.

She thought about what she'd do if she were them. She'd hold up a wall. She'd burn one out in front of the girl. She'd do it to win.

She thought about her husband, who'd have known what to say. Who always asked.

"I'm coming with you," she said.

"You can't. You're the one who pulls." Xiaoyu didn't look away. "If the holding drops, someone has to be on that platform with a hand on his chest. That's you. You said so yourself, on the north wall, to the sky."

Lingyue opened her mouth.

"I'll go," said Su Mingli from the side of the table, arms folded. "I'd lose, slower. That's worth something."

"And I," said the faint voice of Roman Sterling on the channel, from a pier on the far side of the world. "I am very good at deciding things very quickly. I would like, for once, to stand somewhere and not."

"And the night watch," said Dabao from the end of the bench, enormous, not crying for once. "I asked them on the way over. They said: obviously."

Lingyue looked down the table. At the faces. At her sister-in-law. At Tam and Seri, holding hands so hard under the table that both their arms were shaking. At Wanqing at the head, finger in the book.

"Gao Min," she said.

"I'm drawing it," said Gao Min without looking up. "Second rota. The keeper. Five days." Her pencil didn't stop. "I'll need to know who's holding and who's going. You can't do both. Nobody can do both."

"I know," said Shen Lingyue.

She looked down at her daughter, awake in her lap, looking up at her with dark eyes that weren't cloudy any more. His.

"I'm holding," she said. "I'm the lamp."

She hated it. She said it anyway.

At noon, on the white plaster round the corner of the vault door, under FOUR HOUSES. TWENTY SHIPS. SIX DAYS. FOR ME., Zhou Xiaoyu climbed the stepladder and wrote in square capitals:

GOING TO THE KEEPER. TO MEET THEM. TO ASK.

ZHOU XIAOYU (SCRIBE). TAM. SERI. SU MINGLI. THE NIGHT WATCH. R. STERLING (NOT DECIDING).

SHEN LINGYUE: ON THE PLATFORM. WITH HIM. WITH THE LAMP.

And under that, smaller, because she couldn't help it, and because it was true:

I'M NOT WRITING IT.

THAT'S NOT A QUESTION EITHER.
