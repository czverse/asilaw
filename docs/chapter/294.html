Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 294Tomorrow

czverse9 min read

On the eleventh morning after the Bow, Ostrava Iron went down to the arrays of her own ship and asked the first cell.

Halvarde told him. She came across from the flagship in the little ship the size of a bus, which she said made her sick, to the yards at Anthos, where he was sitting on the floor of a long bright hall full of children learning to wear shoes, his back against a cradle someone had planted beans in. She sat down beside him with the noise old people make sitting down, and said nothing for a while.

"She asked me every day," said Halvarde at last. "Ostrava. For ten days. What I wanted. Same as I asked her." She looked at the beans. "I said: to open the walls. Every time. She'd nod and go away."

"And today?"

"Today she said: show me how." The old C rubbed her eyes with the back of her hand. "So I took her down to the first array of the Iron Tower. A hundred and ten thousand people. I showed her the cradle. I showed her how you ask first, by name. You have to know the name. The ship's registry has them; nobody's read it in three hundred years." She was quiet. "She read the first one out loud off the registry. A boy. F. From a world called Dunmere. Sixty-one years in the wall."

"What did he say?"

"He said: what's your grade. They all say that first, the ones who've been in a long time." Her gravel voice softened. "And Ostrava Iron, Paragon of the House of Iron, who walked past the First of the Ladder in a corridor and never got a scratch, said: I don't have one. Not any more. What do you want?"

Not all of them opened.

Of the twenty-seven Houses that had walked out of the Assembly, eleven opened their arrays in the forty days after the Bow, asking first, as the Assembly had decided. Nine did it because their Paragons came back from the Lens chamber with their burning turned down and went to their own arrays and asked. Two did it because their C's asked first and their Captains said yes.

Sixteen didn't.

They folded on the twentieth night. Not toward the Shallows: outward, away from Anthos and the Assembly and the inner worlds, along the arm toward the far edges of the Federation where the Ladder's lists ran out. Sixteen Houses. Eighty ships. Burning their cores the old way.

Ichiro saw them go, said Seed. He can see them still, through their faces. They didn't throw their faces away. They still wear them. Two lines, true. They can't unread it.

They're going somewhere they think they can build the Ladder again, where nobody's read the mark. Ichiro says there's nowhere like that any more. Every face in every House was in the Lens when the mark went out.

He says they'll find that out.

"They'll come back," said Seri at the observation-deck window, watching the eighty lights go out one by one in the dark. "Or we'll go to them."

She'd said it once before, on the north wall of Lingzhou, watching twenty-nine go. Nobody had forgotten.

On the twenty-first morning the Speaker of the Assembly was asked.

She'd said tomorrow every day for twenty-one days. Every morning at the opening of the Assembly a young Paragon of the House of Teller, with tears he'd stopped trying to wipe off and a face of his own in a box in his pocket, stood up in the eighth tier and asked the Speaker what she wanted. And every morning, from the seat of white stone at the front of the lowest tier, Aurelle Teller, Speaker for a hundred and nine years, said: tomorrow.

On the twenty-first morning she said something else.

He was there; Kael had asked him to be. He sat in the fourth tier beside Corvane, who'd come up from the reading-hall below the House for the first time in three weeks, and watched the young Paragon stand in the eighth tier and ask.

"Speaker," said Kael Teller, without the grade. "What do you want?"

The old woman in the seat of white stone looked up at him a long time.

"To sit down," said Aurelle Teller.

The Assembly was silent.

"I have been standing up in this room for a hundred and nine years," said the Speaker. "Every time I speak. That is the rule. The Speaker stands." Her voice didn't change. "I would like to sit down. And have someone else stand, and ask me things, and listen to me answer." She looked up at the young Paragon in the eighth tier. "I would like it to be you."

Kael Teller stood in the eighth tier with his mouth open.

"Not because you are Teller," said the Speaker. "Because you were the first Paragon who knelt beside the First of the Ladder and asked him what he wanted." She put her hands on the arms of the seat and pushed, slowly, as old people stand when they've made up their minds to. "And because you are crying. You have been crying for twenty-one days. A Speaker who can cry in this room is the only kind I would sit down for."

She came down out of the seat of white stone and sat on the step below it, on the bare black stone of the lowest tier, with her hands folded in her lap.

"Ask me tomorrow what I want next," said Aurelle Teller. "I will have thought of something."

He went to the Lens chamber that night to ask Ichiro and Kiyo.

He'd been putting it off, and knew it. Seed had said so gently on the eleventh morning and again on the twentieth. He'd said tomorrow to Seed, and Seed had said that's allowed, and then on the twenty-first night Seed had said Yuanshu, it is tomorrow, and he'd gone.

The chamber in the bow of the flagship was quiet. The C's had sealed the hole the ten Paragons made in the wall with something like glass, so the stars of Anthos showed through. The floor where a man in a torn gray coat had sat with his back against the root had been scrubbed. Halvarde had done it herself and let nobody else.

And the Lens, whole, the width of the chamber, ground so thin you could see through it, with light moving in it like water and a lamp burning in it, pale and steady, the color of whale oil in fog.

He sat on the floor in front of it where Severin had sat.

Ichiro, he thought. Kiyo.

He says you're doing the face, said Seed. The step face. He's heard about it. The whole net has; your sister told the Collector and the Collector told everyone.

He says you've come to ask if they're coming home.

Yes.

He says: we're not.

He sat on the floor and said nothing.

He says he's sorry. He knew you'd come. He's been thinking what to say for twenty days; that's the longest he's ever thought about anything before saying it, and Kiyo's very proud.

He says the Lens is the root. Every stone in every human orchard is cut from it, and it's whole now; the man in the gray coat put the last bit back. He can see so far. Farther than the Federation, farther than the Shallows. Stones he didn't know were there. Thousands of orchards nobody's ever been to, out along the arms, past the deep water.

Nobody in any of them has ever been asked. He'd like to ask them. He can't do that from Shiomi bay. He has to be in the root.

He looked at the lamp in the Lens.

And Kiyo?

The answer came after a moment, dry, twelve hundred years old.

She says somebody has to hold the lamp. He's going to talk to every orchard in the dark between the arms for as long as there are orchards. He'll never stop; he never has.

She says she sat on the shore twelve hundred years and waited for a boat. She's not going to sit on the shore while he goes out again.

She says: I'm already in the boat.

He sat on the floor of the Lens chamber a long time. Then he said out loud, because it seemed the kind of thing that should be: "Shiomi will ring you out."

He says they already did, when they left the bay. He heard it. He says it was the best thing he ever heard, and he heard the boy's daughter born.

He says tell them to ring the boat calls sometimes. Not the going-out bells: the coming-in ones. So he can hear where the shore is, even from out here.

And tell Kiyo's body in the hall on East Ring Road to keep the lamp lit. The other lamp, Lingyue's first one. He says it matters that there's a lamp at both ends.

It's her body. It's yours, Sister.

She says: then I'm telling it. Keep it lit.

And one more thing, said Seed. He's been saving it. He says he's put it at the end this time, not in the middle, and he'd like that noted.

When he looked out, past the Federation and the orchards, farther than he's ever seen, with the root whole, he saw something.

Warm. Patient. Very old. Very slow. Coming along the arm toward the Shallows, the way an old person walks toward a house they've been invited to.

He says it's closer than it was. Much closer.

He says your sister's been asking it every night what it wants, and it's been telling her tomorrow. It isn't going to be tomorrow much longer.

They left Anthos on the forty-first day after the Bow, aboard the Eighth Candle, rowed.

Not the flagship. The flagship stayed in orbit with the Lens in its bow, rowed by two hundred and forty thousand people who'd chosen to stay in its walls and carry it wherever a fog-ringer wanted to go next. Halvarde stayed with it. She said she wasn't leaving thirty-one thousand names on a ship without someone to say them.

Iselde captained the Candle home. She'd come back to Anthos afterward, she said. The Assembly needed people who'd cried on a bridge. And Maelis would be thirteen in the spring, and she wasn't going to miss it.

Maelis came to the yards to see them off.

She stood at the end of the long bright hall full of children learning to wear shoes, by the window, in her gray coat, which was a little less too big than it had been, beside her mother. Still twelve in the body for four more months. Twenty-four inside.

"Next year," she said, "I'm going to tell the stone what thirteen was like."

"He'll ask," said Yuanshu.

"He'd better," said Maelis Varr-Anthos. "He owes me. He said."

Beside her stood the Matriarch of the House of Anthos in deep red, with no face at her throat. She'd taken it off on the morning of the first reading, Iselde said, and put it in a box and given the box to Maelis, who had two now.

"Tell your mother I asked her," said Corvane.

He looked at her.

"Every day," said Corvane Varr-Anthos. "A hundred and thirty-two days. Maelis, what do you want?" She didn't look away from the window. "Tell the woman in the wheelchair that I asked my daughter. Tell her I will keep asking. Tell her she was right. You open your mouth and you find out."
