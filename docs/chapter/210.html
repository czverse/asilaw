Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 210Where Two Rivers Meet

czverse7 min read

Shuanghe wasn't on most maps any more. The last eleven kilometers of road to it were gravel, and Liang Fu walked every meter with his weight off his right leg and refused to get in the truck.

He says he'd like to arrive on foot, said Seed. The sergeant always said you should arrive somewhere the way you left it. He left on foot, on the march, in the Black Tide.

So they walked.

It was spring. The plain south of Heishui was green, with the old Northern War Zone fortifications running along the horizon like a line ruled in gray pencil. The road went between fields of young wheat toward a stand of willows. Yuanshu carried the staff. Lingyue had her grandfather's sword on her back and a bag of mandarins she'd bought at the last petrol station, which she ate steadily and did not share. Old Seven walked a pace behind the corporal, the way a captain walks behind a man who is going home: close enough to catch him, far enough not to have to.

Nobody else had come. Liang Fu had asked them not to. He said he didn't remember enough to want a crowd.

The village was about sixty houses of gray brick under tiled roofs, on a low bank where two rivers met beneath the willows. A wide, slow brown one from the north, a narrow fast green one from the west, swirling together in a long seam of two colors that refused to mix for a kilometer downstream. A school. A shop with a faded drinks sign. A shrine with a red door. And at the point of land where the rivers joined, a stone landing worn into steps, with a flat wooden ferry rocking at it and a rope running across the water to a post on the far bank.

Liang Fu stopped at the edge of the village and stood there.

He says he doesn't remember it, said Seed. He says the rope's in the right place, though.

There was a woman sitting on the landing steps. Fifty or so, broad, sunburned, in a straw hat and rubber boots, with a cigarette and a thermos, waiting for somebody to want to cross. She looked up as they came down between the willows: a young man with a white staff, a young woman with a sword far too big for her and her cheeks full of mandarin, and two skeletons.

She didn't get up and she didn't drop the cigarette. She looked them over the way people on Blue Star had learned to look at the dead in the last couple of years: carefully, without running.

"You're him," she said. "Off the television."

"Yes."

"The one who asks." Her eyes went to the shorter skeleton at the top of the landing, standing on one leg's worth of weight. "And who's that?"

"His name's Liang Fu," said Yuanshu. "He used to run the ferry here, before the Black Tide. He marched north with Lu Xuanming and didn't come back." He paused. "He's back."

The woman sat very still.

Then she put the cigarette out carefully on the step and stood up.

"My mother's brother," she said. "My uncle. Liang Fu. I was sixteen when he marched. He had a bad knee and he talked to the river." She looked at the skeleton. "He took people across for nothing if they looked sad. My mother used to shout at him about the money."

He didn't remember her. Seed was sure. He didn't remember her at sixteen on this landing, or the house she'd been born in, or the shrine, or what anybody called the willows. There was a hole where all that had been.

But he went down the steps to the ferry, slowly, favoring the knee, and stood at the edge of it, and put his hand on the rope.

He says it's the right rope.

He says it isn't the same rope, obviously. It's been over thirty years and ropes don't last thirty years. But it's the right place and the right thickness, and it's tied the right way at the post on the other side. He can see it from here. It's tied the way he used to tie it.

The woman on the steps made a small noise.

"My mother tied it," she said. "After. She took over the ferry when he didn't come home. She said she tied it the way he showed her." Her voice had gone rough. "I tie it the way she showed me."

He says: then it's the right way.

He asked if he could take them across.

The woman didn't answer straight away. Then she came down the steps in her boots, untied the ferry from the iron ring, stepped on, and held the rope out to the skeleton with the bad knee.

"It's yours," she said. "It always was. Mind the third board, it's soft."

They crossed: Yuanshu and Lingyue and Old Seven and the woman in the straw hat, standing on a flat wooden ferry in the middle of a river of two colors while a dead ferryman pulled them over hand over hand on a rope tied the way he used to tie it.

Halfway, he stopped. He stood in the middle of the ferry in the middle of the river, where the brown water and the green water pushed against each other under the boards, and looked down.

He says: I remember this.

Not the village, not the house, not the names. This. The middle, where you can see both colors.

He says he used to stop here when he had somebody sad on board and show them. Two rivers going the same way and not mixing. He'd tell them: they both get there anyway. To the sea.

He says he doesn't know why he remembers that and not his own mother's name.

He says maybe that's the bit that was his.

Lingyue put the last mandarin back in the bag without eating it.

On the far bank he tied the ferry to the post, the way he'd always tied it, the way the woman's mother had, the way she did. Then he stood under the willows with his hand on the post and looked back across the water at the village. Then he turned to Yuanshu.

He says: you said you'd say the sergeant's name. At the ferry.

"I did."

Yuanshu looked north. Past the village, past the green plain and the gray fortifications at Heishui, past the Wastes and the wall of Lingzhou and the small white ring over the end of the world, out into the dark of the Sea. To a cold cracked wheel with no eyes, very far away. To a curtain inside it, and sixty soldiers of the Third Battalion standing in front of it in a line, facing out.

"Huo Changsheng," he said, out loud, on a riverbank in spring, to nobody in particular. "Sergeant. Third Battalion. You held."

He says: again tomorrow. That's how you do it. Every day. He told me.

"Every day," said Yuanshu.

Liang Fu stood with his hand on the post a while longer. Then he lowered himself onto the bank under the willows, bad leg stuck out in front of him, and watched the river.

He says he'd like to stay here.

Not always. He doesn't know what always is. For now. Ask him again in a month.

He says he'd like to take people across. For nothing. If they're sad.

The woman in the straw hat had been crying since the middle of the river and didn't seem to mind who saw.

"I'll ask him," she said, wiping her face on her sleeve. "Every month. I'll come down to the landing and ask him what he wants." She sniffed. "My mother's going to come back and haunt me about the money."

That night, in the old banking hall of the Ossuary, the bell rang.

Not the old pattern. The monks under the gates in Yamato had kept listening every night while the island was away, through Ren's phone, and Sister Kiyo had heard it change on the night the Mouth's eyes went out. She hadn't wanted to tell anyone until the boy was home.

Ren rang at midnight. Seed played it for the hall.

The long low tone. The wait. The small bells saying Shiomi, shorter now and faster and nearer. Ichiro's five quick notes, a bit too quick as always. Then, in the fog-code of a fishing village that had been gone for a thousand years, slower than before, as if whoever rang it wanted to be absolutely sure it was understood:

WE SEE THE COLD FIRE.

WE SEE THE SIXTY.

THEY ARE STANDING. FACING OUT.

WE WILL WAIT WITH THEM.

Nobody in the banking hall moved.

At the end of the INSIDE column, round the corner on the second wall, where the seconds went small under every line, a playing card was pinned to the plaster. A queen of hearts, soft at the corners.

Xiaoyu climbed the stepladder with the marker and wrote under the card, small:

HUO CHANGSHENG. SIXTY. AT THE COLD FIRE. FACING OUT. SOMEONE IS WAITING WITH THEM.

Then, smaller still:

SAY HIS NAME.

Fat Tan, at the bottom of the ladder holding it steady, said it out loud, and so did the rest of the hall, one after another, not together, the way people say a name in their own time.
