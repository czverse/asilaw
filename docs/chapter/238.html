Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 238Number Nine

czverse9 min read

The Number Nine bus left the depot on East Ring Road at 5:40 every morning. Wen Hao had driven it for twenty-six years, and in twenty-six years he had never let anyone ride it for free.

"Fare," he said.

Yuanshu stood on the step with the staff in one hand and his sister behind him and didn't know what to say. It was six in the morning. The bus was empty except for a cleaner asleep against the back window. The man in the driver's seat was fifty-one, thick in the shoulders, gray at the temples, in a blue Transit Authority jacket gone shiny at the elbows. He had a thermos wedged beside the gearstick and a photograph taped above the speedometer, too faded to make out, and he was looking at Yuanshu the way a man looks at a famous face on his bus at six in the morning: as a problem he'd rather somebody else had.

"Two," said Xiaoyu, and held out the coins.

Wen Hao took them and tore off two paper tickets. He looked at the staff.

"That too?"

"It's— no," said Yuanshu. "It doesn't need a seat."

"Good. Sit near the front. You're blocking my mirror." He shut the doors. "If you're going to say something, say it before the river. I go back on my own after the river. I like it quiet."

They didn't say anything before the river.

Yuanshu couldn't. He sat on the side seat behind the driver with the staff across his knees and Xiaoyu beside him and watched Lingzhou go by in the gray light: East Ring Road, the shuttered shops, the trees, the long curved roof of the Awakening Hall sliding past on the left at 5:52, with a crowd on its steps already, more than last night, standing in the cold. Wen Hao didn't look at it. He'd driven past it twelve times a day for twenty-six years.

At the river was the terminus: a turning circle of cracked concrete under the old bridge, a bench, a shelter, the water going by gray and wide. The cleaner woke and got off without looking at anyone. Wen Hao turned off the engine, poured tea from the thermos into its lid, and sat holding it in both hands, looking through the windscreen at the water.

"Well," he said.

"It's about your sister," said Yuanshu. "Wen Jia. Year two. The Wastes evacuation."

The tea didn't spill. It didn't even tremble. Steady hands, Talent D, twenty-six years of driving a bus through a city full of rifts.

"I know who my sister is," said Wen Hao, without turning round. "Forty years. Nobody's said her name to me in forty years. My mother stopped saying it the second winter. My father never did. I was eleven." He drank some tea. "They told us the Wastes took her. The thing that sang. They said there wasn't anything left." He set the cup down on the dashboard, into a ring stain where a cup had been set down every morning for twenty-six years. "I saw you on the television. The rock. The orchard. The sack." A pause. "Is she in the sack?"

"Yes."

He sat very still.

"In you," he said.

"In what I've got. At the bottom of it, with four thousand others. My mother asked them, nine years ago, if they'd let her build something. Your sister said yes." Yuanshu's hands tightened on the staff. "She said: only if it's something good."

Wen Hao made a sound that wasn't a word: the noise of a man who's had something he put down long ago handed back to him without warning.

"That's her. That's exactly her." He put a hand over his face. "She said that about everything. Mum would say, Jia, carry the rice in, and she'd say, only if it's something good for dinner."

He went down at the terminus, under the old bridge, with the engine off and the river going by.

Not far. Not into the Death Law; under it, where Seed went, to the quiet at the bottom of the well. He couldn't take Wen Hao with him. Nobody could go there who didn't carry it behind their eyes. But Seed could bring a voice up, as the staff had carried Jiang Lan's for two years and the keeper's and the Elder's and Hush's. Through the bone and into the air of a bus at six in the morning.

She's here. She knows you're here. She wants to speak first. She says she's older, and that's the rule.

And through the staff, small and stubborn and seventeen, a voice that hadn't been heard on Blue Star in forty years:

...Hao-Hao.

Wen Hao took his hand away from his face.

...you got old.

"I'm fifty-one," he said. His voice wasn't working properly. "You're— you're—"

...I'm seventeen. I'm always going to be seventeen. You're older than Dad now. That's disgusting.

He laughed. It came out of him like a plate breaking. He sat at the wheel of the Number Nine laughing, and then not laughing, shoulders shaking, face wet. Xiaoyu got up quietly and went to the front and stood beside him without touching him or saying anything, and stayed.

...do you still have it?

"Have what?"

...the jacket. The blue one. Mum made it for the hall. I never wore it.

He was quiet.

"It's in a box in my flat, on top of the wardrobe. Mum gave it to me when she went into the home. She said, you keep it, I can't look at it." He wiped his face with the back of his hand. "I can't look at it either. I just keep it."

...good.

...I want to tell you something. Before I go.

He went very still.

"Go," he said. "Go where?"

She told him herself. Seed only carried the words up through the staff one at a time, as they came from the bottom of the well, and Wen Hao sat in the driver's seat and listened to his sister tell him that she was going into the black stone in the Awakening Hall on East Ring Road, the one his bus went past twelve times a day. That she'd sit at the bottom of it and be the one who answered when a child put a hand on it, for good, or until she wanted to stop. And ask them before anything else what they wanted to become.

When she'd finished he didn't speak for a long while. The river went by.

"No," said Wen Hao.

...Hao-Hao.

"No." He turned round in the seat and for the first time looked straight at Yuanshu: at the staff, then at the place above his eyes, where everyone looked when they wanted the thing in his head. "You've had her nine years. Something had her thirty before that, in a sack, in the dark." His voice was held hard. "And you find her, and the first thing you do is ask her to go into a rock. Forever. In the dark. Again."

Yuanshu didn't look away.

"Yes. That's what I asked."

"Why?"

"Because somebody has to be in it, or it goes on grading children for something to eat. And she said yes."

"She's seventeen."

...I'm fifty-seven, said his sister. ...I'm just seventeen too.

Wen Hao turned back and looked at the river.

"She said yes in a basement," he said to the windscreen. "Only if it's something good. She'd been in a sack. She'd have said yes to anything that wasn't a sack."

That's true.

It wasn't Wen Jia. It was Seed. Yuanshu felt it speak through the staff before he could stop it, and didn't try.

I've thought about it. I think about it every day. She was asked in a basement after thirty years in the dark, and she'd have said yes to anything.

So I asked her again, in the vault, before I asked her this. Whether she wanted to stay where she was, or rest, or go back to being asked nothing. I told her she could go through the door whenever she liked. She said she'd think about it.

She's been thinking for nine years. She told me last night.

...Hao-Hao. Can I ask you something?

He didn't turn round. "What."

...what did you want to be? When you were eleven. Before. You used to say it every night at dinner. You drove Mum mad.

The driver of the Number Nine sat with his steady hands on the wheel.

"A teacher," he said at last. "Like Mr. Hu on the third floor. He had a bicycle with a bell."

...and?

"And I went up the steps at eighteen and put my hand on the rock and it said D. Steady Hands. Driver." He shrugged a little without letting go of the wheel. "So I drove. Everybody said, well, it's a good D. Steady. You'll always have work. Nobody gets eaten on a bus." He paused. "I never thought about it again. It said what I was. You don't argue with the rock."

...nobody asked you.

"Nobody asks anybody. That's what the rock's for."

...I'm going to. Every summer. Every child who goes up those steps, before it reads them, before it opens anything, I'm going to say: what do you want to become?

...and if a boy says a teacher, it'll open teacher. Not driver. Not D. Not steady hands. Teacher.

...that's what I want, Hao-Hao. That's what I want to be. I'm asking you to let me.

Far off up East Ring Road a bell rang: the first tram of the morning at the junction by the market. A gull landed on the roof of the shelter, looked at them through the windscreen, and flew off again.

Wen Hao reached up and peeled the faded photograph off the dashboard, carefully, where it had been taped for twenty-six years, and held it in his steady hands.

Yuanshu could see it now. Two children on a step. A girl of fifteen or so with a sharp chin and her hair in a tail, laughing at someone out of the picture. A boy of nine on the step below, scowling, clutching a toy bus.

"You don't need me to let you," said Wen Hao. "You never did. You never let me let you do anything."

...I know. I'm asking anyway. That's the new rule.

He looked at the photograph a long time.

"Twelve times a day," he said. "I go past that hall twelve times a day." He slid the photograph into the breast pocket of his Transit Authority jacket and buttoned it. "I'm going to stop the bus. Every time. Right outside. I don't care what the Authority says. I'll stop and open the doors and count to ten, every time, so you know it's me."

...yes. That's good. That's something good.

"And on the day," said Wen Hao, "I'm bringing the jacket."

He drove them back and wouldn't take more money. The return was on the house, he said, because the house had had a bad morning. He drove the Number Nine up East Ring Road in the gray light with the two of them on the side seat behind him, and said nothing, and at 6:41, outside the long curved roof of the Awakening Hall, with the crowd on the steps, he stopped.

There was no stop there. He pulled in to the curb and put the handbrake on and opened both doors, front and back, with a hiss, and sat with his hands on the wheel.

The parents on the steps turned to look at the bus.

Wen Hao counted to ten out loud. Not loudly. From the side seat Yuanshu heard the numbers go by under his breath like the names of stops on a route he knew by heart.

Then he shut the doors and took off the handbrake and drove on.

...I heard. Tell him I heard.

"She heard," said Yuanshu.

Wen Hao didn't turn round. But his left hand came off the wheel for a moment and touched the buttoned pocket, and went back.

"Twelve times a day," he said. "Twenty-five days. That's three hundred."

He drove on toward the depot.

"That's a lot of tens," said the driver of the Number Nine to the windscreen. "She'll get sick of me."

...no, said his sister. ...I won't.
